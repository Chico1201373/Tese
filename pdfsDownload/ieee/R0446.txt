Abstract—The recent Artificial intelligence development, 
proven by the progress in Large Language models (LLMs) and 
generative systems, has triggered a broad surge in AI adoption 
in various sectors and domains, especially in the Education 
Sector. Most of the LLM ou tcomes are supporting human 
learning, whether directly through tutoring, explanations, and 
feedback, or indirectly through improving decision quality, 
reducing cognitive load, and accelerating access to information 
that shapes learning -related choices. Thi s is creating a lot of 
opportunities for the Educational Institutions as the AI powered 
Question Answering is now expanding to provide 
more accurate output and guidance. However, the 
challenge remains with closed domains where we need to keep a 
certain lev el of security and privacy. Most of the tasks in the 
education domain often depend on closed, sensitive, and policy-
governed data, where privacy, access control, and answer 
traceability are essential. In this paper, we propose a governed 
closed-domain QA f ramework designed for educational 
decision assistance across both academic and administrative 
tasks. The Framework works in three phases. It starts with the 
query processing step, where we review the user input, then 
Intelligent Routing & Retrieval, which routes requests to 
relevant source and finally, retrieval and reinjection, where the 
responses are produced and constructed into structured 
outputs.  
Keywords— Education, Closed-Domain Question Answering, 
Large Language Models, Multilingual NLP, Privacy Governance, 
Role-Based Access Control, Retrieval -Augmented Generation, 
Auditability. 
I. INTRODUCTION  
Artificial intelligence (AI) has reached a significant level 
of maturity. The last two years witnessed a noticeable surge in 
the use of AI and Question Answering (QA) tools by a broad 
range of users. It was reported in [1] that, since 2022 and till 
July 2025, ChatGPT products have been used by 10% of the 
world’s adult population, which is around 800 - 850 million 
users. The core functions done using these tools can be 
summarized into the following: 
1. Guidance & Tutoring: such as Learning support, 
ideation, explanations 
2. Information Seeking: People, events, recipes, 
and general knowledge 
3. Content Assistance: Email writing, document 
drafting, summarization, translation 
The use of AI has extended beyond education into areas 
such as customer service, research support, consulting, and 
information technology workflows [2]. This is widely the case 
for all LLMs, not just ChatGPT, noting that currently we 
have a wide variety of LLMs, including Google’s Gemini [3] 
and Anthropic’s Claude [4]. The analysis of Claude's 
interactions shows that the users concentrate heavily on 
cognitively demanding activities such as problem solving, 
writing, programming, and troubleshooting [5].  
All previously mentioned LLMs are open  domains and 
publicly work for all domains; however, education is a 
particularly high -impact domain for such systems, as it 
combines multiple stakeholders and language -intensive 
processes. The Educational Institutions  need to support 
student needs, such as providing guidance, enabling 
information retrieval, a s well as institution -facing tasks and 
questions, such as program outcomes and  intake of quality, 
admission process. Also, for the institute, it covers the same 
points from an administrative point of view. For example, 
admission and enrollment -related decision processes can be 
pivotal for program success from the institutional viewpoint 
[6], [7], while student readiness and support mechanisms are 
essential from the l earner perspective [8].  Accordingly, 
education offers a strong practical case for QA tools that can 
interpret user questions using Natural Language 
processing, locate relevant institutional content, and respond 
in a way that aligns with institutional expectations.  
However, many current education systems target 
learning assistance, like teacher/student support and 
classroom usage [9], or use LLMs as a discovery interface for 
educational resources like a searchable library [10].  These 
uses are valuable, yet they do not fully address institution -
critical data where answers have constraints that need to be 
followed depending on internal regulations, operational 
procedures, or sensitive records. In such settings, open -
domain generati on is insufficient because institutions  must 
ensure that responses originate from the right sources, also 
respect role -based privileges that are defined based on the 
domain logic, and finally avoid inappropriate disclosure of 
protected or personally identifying information constraints 
commonly associated with closed -domain data access in 
institutional environments [6].  
This challenge becomes more complex in multilingual 
educational contexts, particularly in Arabic data in 
institutions. With different languages, the data are distributed 
across languages, and they might not have one source of truth 
for the data, with inconsistent terminology and phrasing. 
Without clear multilingual handling, systems may misclassify 
the user's question, or r etrieve a mismatch, and ideally 
produce outputs that are difficult to justify or align with the 
asked question. These limitations are dr iving us to a closed -
domain QA approach in which governance and  verification 
are essential parts of the structure and are embedded within

This paper proposes a private constrained closed -domain 
question answering framework for decision assistance in 
education, designed to support institution queries for different 
users while preserving privacy and enforcing policy 
constraints. The proposed a pproach follows three 
phases/Steps: (1) “Secure Data Preparation”: starts with the 
student queries that will be examined, and then the PII data are 
extracted and masked, along with the key academic context

a structured version of the question. (2) Intelligent Routing 
and Retrieval, where the system identifies the task type based

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
Question Answering systems have evolved significantly 
alongside advances in artificial intelligence. The development 
of Large Language Models (LLMs) has impacted user 
behavior and work across multiple domains. The education 
domain is one of the majorly imp acted areas, as most of the 
tasks done using LLMs revolve around learning. In this 
section, we provide a review of the various approaches, 
techniques, and architectures for question answering, then 
review the impact on the education domain. 
A. Question-Answering Approaches, Techniques, and 
Architecture 
Question Answering research has advanced massively 
with the recent AI development. The Question Answering 
systems started with a rule-based system [11]. The rule-based 
approach relied on handcrafted linguistic rules, keyword 
matching, and predefined question patterns, so the 
effectiveness of these rules depended heavily on the 
completeness and correctness of encoded rules [12]. 
These approaches are commonly grouped into 3 types in 
[13]: Template -based methods that populate predefined 
answer frames [14].  Syntax-based methods that apply 
grammatical and logical structures  [15]. The semantic-based 
methods that map language to meaning using ontologies or 
semantic networks [16]. 
The rule -based QA performance is good in narrow, 
controlled domains, as the rules are specific and answer 
specific questions. However, it typically struggles with 
language variation, unseen questions, and deeper context and 
conclusions, which limit scalability and robustness [17]. 
Accordingly, to improve the flexibility and overcome this 
limitation, the research started to move toward machine 
learning (ML) based QA, where subtasks such as question 
classification, answer selection, and ranking are extracted 
from annotated corpora rather than specified as rules [18]. 
Traditional ML uses supervised or semi -supervised 
classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs), 
decision trees, and Conditional Random Fields (CRFs). 
These classifiers are trained on lexical  and shallow semantic 
features extracted from questions and answers [19]. 
These feature -driven models improved generalization 
compared to rule -based systems, yet they remained 
constrained by feature engineering effort, domain -specific 
preprocessing, and labeled data requirements, and they often 
underperformed on deeper semantic understanding and 
multi-step reasoning limitations that pushed research toward 
deep representation learning [13]. 
The next step for QA was deep learning; QA systems 
learned hierarchical and contextual representations directly 
from data. The Neural architectures, for example  
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural 
Networks (RNNs), and attention-based encoders have created 
semantic matching improvement between questions and 
answers, especially for knowledge base QA and neural 
reading tasks [20]. Surveys show that deep learning improved 
the contextual extraction and answer accuracy, but at the 
same time, it  increased reliance on large datasets and 
significant computation, while still facing issues with 
explainability, robustness, and complex reasoning [21], [22]. 
Moving from Deep learning, a major turning point 
happened, which was the Transformer architecture. It started 
by introducing self-attention for global context modeling and 
became the key element in modern QA [23]. Building on 
transformers, pretrained models such as BERT made 
significant improvements in QA extraction and reading 
comprehension through fine -tuning. The se models 
outperformed the older version by improving their extraction 
through using large datasets such as SQuAD [24]. The 
transformer-based sy stems then evolved toward generative 
QA and open -domain settings, which included retrieval -
augmented designs that enhanced factual grounding by 
merging retrieval with generation [25]. 
Recently, the emergence of Large Language Models 
(LLMs) changed QA approaches drastically towards a 
general-purpose reasoning that uses natural language 
generation. It usually generates the answers using pretraining, 
which is represented by the GPT -style models. The 
transformer-based retrieval -generation systems (RAG [25]) 
and LLMs (GPT -4 [26], Claude 3 [2 7], Gemini [3]) enable 
broad reasoning and generation and demonstrate strong zero-
shot and few-shot QA as model and data scale increased [28]. 
These LLMS can be fine-tuned using specific datasets, which 
create what is called instruction -tuned models and 
reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). These 
are used in InstructGPT [29], which results in more controlled 
and reliable answers. 
Newer models such as GPT -4 [28],  Claude 3[29], and 
Gemini [3] extended the QA progress to a stronger reasoning, 
multilingual interaction, and long -context or multimodal 
capabilities. But, because LLM knowledge is  bound by

Finally, hybrid QA combines symbolic and neural 
components, usually using knowledge graphs (Ontology) to 
support structured reasoning while keeping the balance [31]. 
The improvement happens with embedding and integrating 
structured knowledge based on the available data sets, which 
ideally improves  multi-hop reasoning and context -aware 
answering [32]. In [33], the recent hybrid LLMs showed 
improved control using validation rules, schemas, and 
ontologies, which highly reduced the hallucination and 
improved reliability. This is extremely useful in safety-critical 
domains such as education. 
Along with the approaches, changes, and development, the 
techniques used in each approach are also developed. It goes 
3 sets of techniques: the question analysis techniques, 
Information retrieval, and answer extraction and generation. 
These different techn iques aim to strengthen the query  of 
understanding, question classification, and expected answer 
types. The classical Machine learning methods, such as 
Support Vector Machine (SVMs) and logistic regression, are 
still used for question classification as it works with 
constrained and even with low -resource settings [34]. They 
matter the most with the limited training data, which reduces 
the feasibility of more complex models [19]. 
The query analysis techniques aim to transform the raw 
natural language and understand the user’s intent using 
lexical, syntactic, and semantic processing. It includes query 
expansion and handling out-of-vocabulary terms (OVVs), and 
using contextual embeddings, which, at the end, improve 
precision and F1, though may affect ranking stability [35]. The 
extraction is done using Preprocessing such as segmentation, 
keyword extraction, and answer-type detection, which is also 
widely recognized as a critical layer  in QA architecture 
pipelines [36]. 
Once keywords are extracted, our focus will shift to 
information retrieval, which remains central for QA, as this is 
the stage where we find the evidence to then extract the 
answer. The retrieval methods start with the sparse retrieval, 
such as BM25, which  is Term Frequency-Inverse Document 
Frequency (TF -IDF) weighting and ranking functions for 
evidence and document estimation [37]. Then, moving from 
the BM25 used for efficiency and strong lexical matching to 
the K -Nearest Neighbors (KNN),  measures the simi larity 
between query vectors and indexed documents [30]. The 
Second retrieval method is the Dense retrieval, which enables 
semantic search by mapping queries and documents into 
shared spaces, with methods such as DPR [38], ColBERTv2 
[39], and Contriever [40]. It shows a wide improvement in the 
retrieval’s quality and generalization.  Knowing the 
techniques, modern tools mostly use a hybrid approach 
between sparse recall and dense ranking. 
 After retrieval, systems start the extraction process with 
different extraction techniques, such as span selection using 
transformer encoders and attention, or generative techniques 
such as sequence -to-sequence and autoregressive generation 
models [13]. M ore recently, RAG has become a dominant 
technique for generation, using strategies such as Fusion -in-
Decoder (FiD), re -ranking, and multi -document aggregation 
[41]. It is improved further using prompt engineering, domain 
fine-tuning, and adaptive retrieval , especially relevant for 
educational settings [42]. 
Considering all the techniques and approaches that are 
used to create question answering, a lot of architectures have 
been produced to orchestrate the work between all  the 
different tools and techniques. The typical architecture for QA 
is implemented as a multi-stage architecture. This architecture 
starts with a query and then transforms it into an answer 
through modular components. The pipeline begins with 
question proc essing, which includes tokenization, syntactic 
parsing, question classification, and semantic interpretation to 
extract the user's intent and answer type. This step is crucial in 
the retrieval strategy and response formulation [43]. The 
second stage is information retrieval, where the system 
searches for the relevant evidence and documents from 
available corpora, datasets, knowledge bases, or hy brid 
repositories. Earlier architectures relied on keyword methods 
such as TF-IDF and BM25, then moved to the dense retrieval 
based on transformer embeddings or dual -encoder designs, 
and finally, the hybrid approach as mentioned before [38]. 
The last stage is answer ing processing, where systems 
either extract spans (extractive QA) or generate natural -
language responses using generative QA and LLMS based on 
the retrieved context. It also includes retrieval -augmented 
generation models to ensure that the mode outputs are 
supported by the provided information and knowledge page, 
or what is called improving the factual grounding [44]. 
Many architectures then apply answer selection and re -
ranking using scoring functions or neural rankers to ensure 
relevance and coherence. In high -reliability domains, post -
processing layers may include rule -based validation, factual 
consistency checks, and justification mechanisms to increase 
trustworthiness. Even when end -to-end LLMs appear to 
compress these stages into a single model, modern QA 
systems still frequently preserve retrieval augmentation and 
validation layers to maintain grounded answers and  support 
domain-specific requirements, including educational use 
cases [38], [44]. 
Many QA architectures select the best answer, rank the 
candidate’s answers, and reorder using scoring methods and 
ranking models to show relevant and clear results. In a closed 
private domain setting, they add an extra layer after

III. PROPOSED FRAMEWORK AND METHODOLOGY 
This research proposed a framework that targets privacy 
and security. It is stated as a governed closed-domain question 
answering (QA) framework for decision assistance in 
education, which is designed to operate under institutional 
constraints related to privacy, access control, and auditability. 
The framework targets both academic and administrative 
areas where confidential/personal data should be constrained 
and used anonymously, and the answers must align and be 
reviewed based on the approved institution al knowledge 
rather than unconstrained open -domain sources. It targets 
assistance in scenarios such as advising, academic path, Score 
evaluation, curriculum clarification, and study planning.  To 
apply this, the proposed design adopts a controlled, multi -
stage pipeline that minimizes exposure of sensitive data, 
restricts retrieval scope based on task needs, and enforces 
validation before returning responses. 
A. Framework Overview and Design Goals 
The proposed framework is designed to support privacy 
using privacy -aware question answering. It is combining 
approved closed institutional sources with public data; at the 
same time, it is keeping a clear control over what can be used 
in each case. Instead of treating all queries the same, the 
framework applies the routing rules that decide which parts of 
the request can be handled using public information through 
LLMs, and which parts must be answered using internal 
institutional sources and processes. 
This design is generated based on the privacy constraints 
and user needs in educational QA. Most of the questions are 
classified based on the tasks,  such as admissions, curriculum, 
student services, and career evaluation & personalized study 
Plan. So , routing the query to the right flow improves 
relevance and avoids unnecessary retrieval.  
The framework aims to merge the best of both approaches; 
we can use the fast-developed LLMs freely with the allowed 
public and open data in the educational domain. While 
keeping all confidential data as a second layer and applying 
internal rules and extractio n based on the internal records 
and confidential/Personal data  sources. So, we can list the 
data sources for this  system as public knowledge (Online 
sources), institutional data sources and repositories, 
domain rules, and constraints.  
Thus, the framework follows a few clear sets of goals: 
1. Data source Controlling, as the system evaluates and 
controls what to share publicly and what not to share 
intentionally through routing. 
2. Privacy by design, as it limits the exposure of 
personal or sensitive data and enforces masking when 
needed. 
3. Task routing,  send each query through the most 
suitable path. 
4. Auditability and Logs , by keeping a clear log and 
evidence of what was accessed and how the answer 
was formed.

C. Synthesis & Final Review 
In this final phase, the system reviews the previously 
generated results against the access policy and the allowed 
data scope before returning them. Once reviewed, the system 
creates and synthesizes the answer with the personal data after 
injecting the personal data only when needed and  permitted. 
The framework then produces a clear, structured output, such 
as an academic roadmap or a verified policy answer, and 
delivers the final response to the student.  
Once data is returned, the system evaluates the response 
and starts PII and domain data embedding inside the content. 
They provide the final delivery message for the 
user, stating actions and a plan for the user to enhance their 
performance. Then, at the e nd, a log of the task is generated 
with the Task ID, PII Object, Domain Objects, Queries,  and 
answers.

VI. CONCLUSION & FUTURE WORK 
In this paper, we proposed a closed domain question 
answering system that supports education constraints to make 
sure privacy is preserved for the users.  Based on the domain 
role and constraints, we provided a well-structured and robust 
tool for the institute to support the different users and people 
in the organization. While privacy check might cause latency 
for the output; however, it ensures that the response 
is following the regulation, such as General Data Protection 
Regulation (GDPR), which is required in the European Union 
(EU), and Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act 
(FERPA), which is required in the USA. 
Despite the strengths, there are some limitations that we 
need to improve. It is required to enhance latency and perform 
end-to-end testing for the model and outputs considering the 
external factor. Reduce the semantic gap that happens due 
to removing, then injecting the personal data into the desired 
answer. Work on an ongoing conversation handling especially 
with complex queries that have multiple intents.  We aim to 
improve daily usability in a real university environment by 
working on pilot deployment inside it.
