Abstract 
In large-scale classrooms, students often struggle to ask questions 
due to limited instructor attention and social pressure. Based on 
findings from a formative study with 24 students and 12 instructors, 
we designed AskNow, an LLM-powered system that enables students 
to ask questions and receive real-time, context-aware responses 
grounded in the ongoing lecture and that allows instructors to view 
students’ questions collectively. We deployed AskNow in three uni-
versity computer science courses for a week and tested with 117 
students. To evaluate AskNow’s responses, each instructor rated the 
perceived correctness and satisfaction of 100 randomly sampled 
AskNow-generated responses. In addition, we conducted interviews 
with 24 students and the three instructors to understand their ex-
perience with AskNow. We found that AskNow significantly reduced 
students’ perceived time to resolve confusion. Instructors rated 
AskNow’s responses as highly accurate and satisfactory. Instructor 
and student feedback provided insights into the role of such systems 
in supporting real-time learning in large lecture settings.

1 Introduction 
Large-scale classrooms present unique challenges for student-
instructor interactions, including students feeling reluctant to ask 
questions due to social pressure [10] or unable to attract instruc-
tor attention [27], challenges exacerbated by more advanced or 
fast-paced classes [9]. Instructors, on the other hand, can be over-
whelmed with teaching while managing a large classroom and moni-
toring student attention, confusion, and questions [51]. Researchers 
and educators have explored various technology-mediated inter-
ventions to address these challenges. Audience Response Systems 
(ARS), such as Poll Everywhere, Slido, and Top Hat, allow students 
to participate anonymously through multiple-choice polling, en-
hancing engagement, attendance, and ability to give and receive 
immediate feedback [15, 38, 46]. Similarly, classroom backchannel

systems, such as Hotseat [1] and Backchan.nl [33], enable students 
to post open-ended questions during lectures without interrupting 
the instructor [5, 49]. Although these tools effectively promote stu-
dent participation and relay student confusion to instructors, these 
technologies still offer limited support to students, due to lack of 
personalized, real-time feedback, and instructors, due to the added 
demand on their attention [29]. 
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have expanded 
possibilities for educational support across domains, including pro-
gramming education [36, 42, 68, 75, 76], early childhood learning 
[35, 77], and tutoring [28]. Systems, such as SPHERE, assist instruc-
tors in reviewing and prioritizing feedback on student code [68], 
while a virtual teaching assistant, named Jill Watson, answers ques-
tions in online education [67]. Although these systems demonstrate 
the potential of LLMs for personalized feedback and support, they 
are primarily designed for asynchronous interactions or special-
ized contexts such as real-time code assessment. Little work has 
been done to explore how LLMs could provide students and instruc-
tors with contextualized, real-time support during synchronous, 
in-person lectures, particularly in large-scale classroom settings. 
To address these challenges, we conducted a two-phase study. 
In Phase 1, we interviewed 24 students and 12 instructors to un-
derstand their experiences with learning and teaching in large 
classrooms, respectively. In particular, we discussed challenges in 
real-time interaction, strategies for resolving confusion, and expec-
tations for AI-powered support tools. To address the challenges 
identified in our formative study, we designed AskNow, an interac-
tive system that leverages LLMs to provide students with immedi-
ate, context-aware answers to their questions during live lectures. 
AskNow synchronously records and transcribes the lecture content, 
which serves as context for the LLM’s responses. In Phase 2, we 
deployed AskNow across three large university computer science 
courses of varying difficulty levels, where 117 students used AskNow 
over two sessions of their class and responded to pre- and post-
surveys measuring perceived effectiveness of AskNow. We further 
evaluated the system through expert assessments, where instruc-
tors rated the accuracy of AskNow-generated responses. Finally, we 
interviewed all three instructors and 24 students regarding their 
experience using AskNow. 
Our data showed that AskNow significantly reduced students’ 
perceived time to resolve confusion during lectures. Instructors 
rated AskNow’s responses as highly accurate and satisfactory. Stu-
dents demonstrated a variety of use patterns based on when and 
why they used AskNow to seek clarification, and instructor feedback 
revealed insights into how real-time AI systems could complement 
traditional classroom teaching to better support large-scale learning 
environments. 
Our work makes the following contributions: 
• Formative Data: An empirical understanding of large class-
room needs from both instructor and student perspectives.
• System Contribution: AskNow, a real-time AI-based question-
answering tool for large-scale classrooms.
• Evaluation Data: An empirical understanding of how AskNow
was used across three university courses.
• Design Implications: Insights from our real-world deployment
synthesized into implications to inform the design of future
LLM-powered classroom support systems.

2 Related Work 
2.1 Challenges in Large-scale Classrooms 
Although large-enrollment courses are often valued for their ef-
ficiency, they consistently constrain meaningful interaction and 
make it difficult for instructors to monitor individual student under-
standing [27, 30, 50]. Within crowded lecture halls, many students 
refrain from asking questions due to communication apprehension, 
which substantially limits overall participation [10, 51]. The time 
pressure of covering extensive material, coupled with the tradi-
tional single-speaker paradigm, further restricts opportunities for 
turn-taking, such that only a small subset of vocal students tends 
to dominate classroom discourse [27, 70]. As class size increases, 
empirical work has shown declines in active engagement and reduc-
tions in teacher–student interactions, with particularly pronounced 
effects for students with lower prior attainment [8]. Foundational 
analyses of lecturing similarly highlight that conventional lectures 
are primarily designed for efficient information transmission rather 
than adaptive, dialogic exchange [9, 31]. 
The pedagogical costs of these interaction gaps are considerable. 
Misconceptions often remain unvoiced during the rapid pace of 
delivery, and opportunities for in-the-moment clarification are lost 
[30, 50]. A substantial body of research underscores the importance 
of immediate feedback, demonstrating that students benefit when 
misunderstandings are addressed promptly rather than after delays 
[4, 23, 39, 65]. Interactive and team-based learning formats similarly 
rely on rapid clarification cycles, which have been linked to greater 
engagement and deeper knowledge construction [12, 59]. In sum, 
scale itself introduces structural barriers to inclusive participation 
and timely feedback, leaving widespread confusion easily over-
looked in the context of large-scale lectures [2, 20, 27, 51, 65, 70]. 
2.2 Technology for Large-scale Classrooms 2.3 AI-based Systems for Learning Support 
To mitigate challenges in large-scale classrooms, researchers and 
educators have explored various technology-mediated interven-
tions. One prominent line of work is Audience Response Systems 
(ARS)–commonly known as clickers or live polling tools–such 
as Poll Everywhere, Top Hat, and traditional handheld clickers 
(e.g., TurningPoint). These tools enable simultaneous student re-
sponses to multiple-choice questions, providing aggregated and 
real-time feedback to the class [15, 38, 46]. Many studies have 
demonstrated the positive impact of ARS in large-scale classrooms, 
including improved attendance via increased student motivation 
[13, 15, 32]; enhanced attention through interactive, real-time 
polling [7, 13, 15, 21]; and increased participation by leveraging 
anonymity to reduce anxieties associated with public speaking 
[61, 62, 66]. Furthermore, ARS usage correlates positively with 
overall classroom engagement, as students actively reflect and re-
spond to lecture content rather than passively receiving information 
[22, 57]. Finally, empirical evidence consistently demonstrates that 
ARS can promote students’ learning performance, knowledge reten-
tion, and academic achievement across diverse disciplines, includ-
ing physics [11], biology [57], pharmacy education [63], medicine 
[56, 60], and general undergraduate instruction [37, 40]. These 
improvements are largely attributed to ARS’s ability to facilitate 
frequent formative assessment, promote active engagement, and 
provide immediate feedback. 
Another technology-based strategy is the classroom backchan-
nel, a secondary communication stream enabling students to post 
questions, comments, or feedback in real-time alongside the pri-
mary lecture[1, 33]. Examples such as Hotseat at Purdue University 
[1], Backchan.nl [33], and Backstage [55] enable participation in 
large lectures via web or mobile interfaces. Hotseat, in particular, 
has shown effectiveness in lowering barriers related to anxiety or so-
cial pressure by offering anonymous participation [1]. Evaluations 
of backchannel systems indicated increased student interaction, 
especially during fast-paced or complex lectures, as students were 
able to ask questions without interrupting the instructor [5, 49]. 
Students also reported greater engagement due to the immediate 
visibility of their questions or feedback [1, 34]. Overall, backchannel 
tools offer students ways to resolve confusion and enhance their 
engagement during lectures. 
However, despite their demonstrated benefits, both ARS and 
backchannel systems have notable limitations. ARS tools usually 
only allow students to respond through simple multiple-choice or 
polling interactions, limiting their ability to capture more detailed 
or complex student confusions. Backchannel systems, although sup-
porting open-ended queries, still rely heavily on manual responses 
from instructors or moderators, hindering real-time interaction 
in large lectures. Furthermore, continuous monitoring of student 
inputs may increase instructor cognitive load, disrupting lecture 
flow [29]. In addition, students may get distracted from the ongoing 
lecture while posting questions through backchannel [1, 5]. Most im-
portantly, neither type of system provides immediate, personalized, 
and detailed feedback, often leaving student misunderstandings 
unresolved during class. 
Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and large lan-
guage models (LLMs) have created new opportunities for enhancing 
education. With capabilities such as real-time natural language in-
teraction, personalized feedback generation, and flexible content 
creation, these technologies offer the potential to support learning 
at scale and to augment traditional instruction with more adaptive, 
individualized support. Researchers have begun exploring how AI-
driven systems can address challenges across various educational 
domains, improving both student learning experiences and teacher 
workflows. 
In programming education, several systems have leveraged LLMs 
to support scalable learning. VizProg visualizes student coding 
progress to help identify misunderstandings [75], while CFlow ex-
tends this work by enabling large-scale semantic flow analysis of 
student code [76]. SPHERE builds on these approaches by review-
ing LLM outputs to prioritize critical feedback for instructors [68]. 
Other applications have explored the use of LLMs for learning sup-
port beyond computing. Storyfier supports vocabulary acquisition 
through AI-generated storytelling [54], while LessonPlanner [24] 
and LitLinker [25] assist novice teachers in designing pedagogy-
aligned lesson plans and interdisciplinary learning contexts, re-
spectively. In early childhood education, Mathemyths introduces 
co-creative storytelling to teach mathematical language [77], SET-

for robot-mediated learning [35], and StoryMate personalizes inter-
active story reading experiences for children [16]. For assessment 
and tutoring, systems such as ConQuer facilitate concept-based 
quiz generation [28], while Jill Watson acts as a virtual teaching 
assistant that answers student questions in online education based 
on course materials [67]. Building on this line of work, Tiwari et al. 
introduced an AI-assisted teaching support tool that automatically 
answers frequently asked questions [69], and Askademia uses a 
vision–language model to provide responses to student queries 
during lectures based on course materials and slides [48]. 
While these systems demonstrate the versatility of AI and LLMs 
in educational contexts, much of the current work has focused on 
asynchronous, one-on-one, or home-based learning environments. 
A small but growing body of work explores the integration of AI 
into real-time classroom settings. For example, Askademia and Pair-
Up both investigate how real-time agents can support students 
and teachers during live sessions [48, 73]. However, these systems 
primarily evaluate correctness, efficiency, and orchestration, and 
provide limited instructor-facing awareness of students’ collective 
confusion, offering little discussion of how such agents may in-
fluence students’ in-class attention or perceptions of instructional 
authority. Subsequent work on AI co-orchestration tools further ex-
amines how teachers conceptualize sharing control with AI systems, 
highlighting concerns around trust, responsibility, and efficiency 
[41]. 
Despite these advances, there remains a significant gap in re-
search on how LLMs might directly support students’ real-time 
clarification needs during live lectures while also preserving instruc-
tor oversight and authority. Existing AI-based classroom systems 
primarily aim to assist instructors in managing class dynamics or 
provide delayed feedback, and even real-time virtual teaching assis-
tants rarely give instructors an aggregated view of ongoing student 
confusion in large lecture halls. To address this gap, the present 
work explores the use of LLMs as real-time classroom assistants, 
supporting dynamic clarification in large-scale lectures. 
3 Formative Study 
Based on the prior work, we identified that instructors face chal-
lenges in gauging individual student understanding and facilitating 
real-time interaction, while students have difficulties in voicing 
questions and receiving immediate feedback during lectures. To 
inform how we can design a system to bridge these gaps, we con-
ducted a formative study with 24 students and 12 instructors to 
understand their experiences in classroom learning, including the 
challenges they face, current strategies for resolving confusion, and 
their expectations for AI-powered support tools. 
3.1 Methods 
3.1.1 Participants. We recruited a total of 36 participants, includ-
ing 24 students (13 female, 11 male; Age between 18 and 29,

from a large public university in the Midwestern United States. 
Recruitment was conducted university-wide. Student participants 
were enrolled in a variety of disciplines and included both under-
graduate and graduate students across all years of study. Instructor 
participants were similarly diverse, from different academic depart-
ments and teaching at least one course during the current academic 
semester. Demographic details of participants are presented in Ta-
ble 1 (Students) and Table 2 (Instructors). Each Participant received 
$10 as compensation for completing the interview. The study was 
approved by our university’s IRB. 
3.1.2 Semi-structured Interviews. Each participating student and 
instructor took part in a semi-structured interview, which lasted 
for around 30 minutes. For both students and instructors, the in-
terviews began with open-ended questions about recent classroom 
experiences, challenges, and current strategies for resolving con-
fusion. Only in the latter part of the interview did we introduce a 
short vignette about a hypothetical AI assistant, using it to probe 
participants’ expectations and concerns rather than to elicit con-
crete feature requests. Consistent with prior work highlighting that 
users may struggle to articulate their underlying needs directly [53], 
we anchored our questions in concrete past experiences and probed 
for the reasons behind participants’ suggestions. Student interview 
questions explored their in-class confusion experiences (e.g., “Have

3.1.3 Data Analysis. To anonymize participants, we assigned IDs 
based on the order of study completion (e.g., S1 refers to the first 
student participant, I1 refers to the first instructor participant). For 
qualitative data, we conducted a reflexive Thematic Analysis (TA) 
following Clarke and Braun [18] and McDonald et al. [47] to iden-
tify patterns and understand student and instructor perspectives. 
The first author and the third author, experienced in qualitative 
coding, transcribed and familiarized themselves with the data from 
audio recordings. Initial semantic codes were generated by the 
first author and organized into categories as the initial codebook. 
The team collaboratively coded data from the first participant us-
ing the initial codebook, discussed interpretations, and created a 
consensus-based codebook. Using the codebook, the remaining data 
were independently coded by the two authors, with peer reviews 
and iterative discussions to finalize codes. We constructed final 
themes from recurring, meaningful patterns in the data. 
3.2 Findings 
Through interviews with students and instructors, we identified 
five central themes reflecting the challenges and needs of students 
and instructors in lecture settings. These findings serve as founda-
tions for deriving design implications, guiding the development of 
AI-powered systems that can more effectively mediate real-time 
learning support and instructional feedback. 
3.2.1 Challenges in Following and Retaining Lecture Content. Many 
students described experiencing confusion during lectures for a 
variety of reasons. For many, the fast pace or density of the course 
material was difficult to follow (S6, S7, S18, S21, S22). For exam-
ple, S6 stated, “Sometimes professors rush through the content and I 
can’t follow.” Furthermore, several students noted that confusion 
often arose when instructors introduced complex concepts without 
providing sufficient elaboration or examples (S3, S5, S10). As S10 
explained, “Sometimes in organic chemistry he’ll bring up a new 
concept that is pretty confusing because there’re a lot of moving parts.” 
Language and cultural barriers were particularly salient for interna-
tional students (S2, S3, S8). For example, S2 stated, “For the statistics 
class, I learned the concepts in Chinese before, but learning them in 
English is totally new.” In addition, S8 added, “When certain refer-
ences are local, it is a bit challenging from time to time to grasp what 
this localized knowledge is assuming”. Besides in maintaining focus 
during long lectures, students also face challenges in recovering 
when they missed part of the lecture (S2, S11, S16–S22). Several 
participants noted that missing a key point often caused them to 
fall behind and struggle to catch up, leading to cascading confusion 
throughout the lecture. As S21 described, “When there are times 
when I get behind in a lecture. Then I get stuck on it and then I fall 
behind in the rest of lecture.” 
Design Implication #1: These findings suggest the need for a 
system that can provide immediate clarification to help students re-
cover from momentary lapses and support continuous participation 
during class. 
3.2.2 Barriers to Asking Questions During Large Lectures. Many 
students expressed reluctance to ask questions during class, partic-
ularly in large lecture settings. Social anxiety (S17, S19, S23), fear of 
judgment (S9, S13, S16, S18, S21, S22), and concern about interrupt-
ing the lecture (S1, S3, S8, S17, S19, S20, S21, S23) were common 
deterrents. For instance, S16 expressed concerns “to be that person 
to ask a stupid question,” while S1 felt like “it’s rude to interrupt the 
whole class just for me.” Several students felt isolated in their con-
fusion and assumed others understood the material (S3, S10, S15). 
For example, S3 felt like “the only one who has the problem here.” 
Others referenced cultural or language barriers that made it harder 
to speak up (S2, S3, S8, S24): “I’m worried about my oral English 
(S24). ” The issue was compounded in large classrooms, where the 
substantial number of the students discouraged active participation. 
As S13 expressed, “it can be a little daunting to ask questions in 
class because there’s so many other people in the room.” Conversely, 
students reported greater comfort asking questions in smaller or 
discussion-based classes, or when they had built familiarity with 
the instructor (S4, S7, S8, S10, S16, S17, S20).

Design Implication #2: These results suggest that our system 
should be able to reduce the social costs of participation by enabling 
private, low-friction question submission. 
3.2.3 Limitations in Student Strategies for Confusion Resolution. 
Students employed a variety of strategies to resolve confusion, 
including asking peers, searching online, reviewing lecture record-
ings, and attending office hours. AI tools like ChatGPT were also 
commonly used. 
However, each of these methods had limitations. Peers were 
sometimes helpful but not always accurate: “When I asked my 
friends, I feel less embarrassed. But it could almost be more confus-
ing (S16). ” Online searches and videos were often time-consuming 
(S2, S9, S15, S16, S22): “Searching online takes more time (S2), ” and 
“YouTube videos are less effective” sometimes if it is not the right 
video (S16). Students noted that ChatGPT was convenient but could 
produce vague or incorrect answers (S3, S14, S20, S21). For instance, 
S14 said that “It tends to provide some general generic answers some-
times,” while S3 mentioned that “sometimes ChatGPT gives false 
ideas, so I double check with the professor.” 
Attending office hours was frequently cited as helpful but im-
practical due to scheduling conflicts (S4, S10, S11, S16, S20, S22), 
distance between home and school (S4, S12, S20, S22), or social 
anxiety (S17, S19, S23). For example, S22 shared that “office hours 
have very set times...if I’m far from campus...I can’t really make it.” 
Design Implication #3: These limitations underscore the need 
for an accessible and context-aware support system that minimizes 
effort and delay in resolving confusion during lectures. 
3.2.4 Student Expectations for an AI-Powered Support Tool. Stu-
dents articulated a set of desired features for a classroom AI as-
sistant, including real-time transcription (S3, S17, S18, S22) and 
summarization of lecture content (S9, S13, S15, S17, S20, S23). S13 
said that “it would be nice if it could give me a summary of what 
the professor said for the past five or ten minutes (S13). ” They also 
wanted the ability to ask questions and receive real-time, concise, 
and contextually relevant answers (S7, S15, S18). For example, S18 
said that it would be nice “if it knows what’s going on and I can ask it 
questions. ” Several students expressed interest in additional features 
such as generating practice questions (S10, S16, S17), summarizing 
key terms (S23), and explaining how different concepts connect 
(S21, S23). 
In addition to describing desired functionalities, students also 
highlighted expectations for how the tool should fit into existing 
classroom practices. Many preferred a lightweight, low-friction 
interface that runs directly on devices they already use, such as 
laptops or tablets. They explained that such a tool could “just split 
screen and have it in the corner, ready and available” (S15), and that 
a website “linked to Canvas” aligns well with current workflows 
since “most people already have their laptops out” (S19). Several also 
favored using their own devices rather than carrying additional 
hardware, noting they “didn’t have to also have another device open” 
(S18). 
Design Implication #4: The tool should be seamlessly inte-
grated into students’ workflow, preferably accessible on devices 
they already use, such as laptops or phones, and should not require 
additional setup or effort during class. 
3.2.5 Instructor Needs and Opportunities for AI Support. Instruc-
tors identified various needs and concerns regarding classroom 
dynamics and the potential integration of an AI assistant. First, 
instructors confirmed that students were often apprehensive about 
asking questions in large classes. Despite encouragement, “students 
are very apprehensive of asking questions in large classes, and so it 
doesn’t matter how often you try and invite answers or questions, no 
one asks (I2). ” I10 similarly observed, “typically the students who are 
asking the questions are the most confident...the students who are the 
least confident often sit in the back and don’t want to voice anything.” 
Second, instructors found it difficult to accurately track student 
confusion during lectures, lacking real-time indicators of student 
understanding. As I11 explained, It’s very hard to tell how many 
students have already fully understood the concept...I have no way 
of measuring how and to what extent students are not understand-
ing, except for exams.” Similarly, I4 shared, “I don’t feel like I have 
indicators on whether the students actually know what I’m talking 
about.” Some instructors had attempted to collect student questions 
during class using simple tools but yielded low participation (I2, I8). 
For example, I2 “tried Google Sheets...but students don’t seem to be 
interested in filling in questions during lecture.” 
Therefore, instructors expressed strong interest in having a tool 
that not only encourages students to ask questions, but also pro-
vides a safe, anonymous channel for doing so. A key reason be-
hind students’ reluctance was their concern about privacy and 
fear of judgment from peers. Several instructors emphasized that 
anonymity would be critical to making students feel comfortable 
asking questions (I2, I5, I10). As I2 explained, “I would like every-
thing to be anonymized in some way, shape or form so that a student 
can ask questions and not have to worry about other students knowing 
who is asking the question.” 
Beyond facilitating anonymous question submission, instructors 
also hoped that an AI assistant could help respond to student ques-
tions in real time. However, they raised concerns regarding the 
accuracy and appropriateness of AI-generated answers (I4, I6–I8, 
I12). Instructors emphasized the importance of ensuring the con-
sistence of responses from AI with the course content and their 
own teaching style (I1, I10, I12). As I12 highlighted, “I would want 
to have a chance to look at the type of content that is being sent to 
students.” Consequently, instructors wanted the ability to review 
and monitor AI responses to maintain quality and consistency. 
In addition, instructors want to know what questions students 
were asking during class. They wanted access to both raw questions 
(I7, I8) and aggregated questions grouped by topics (I2, I3, I5–I8, 
I10, I12). For example, I7 mentioned “based on my current experi-
ence, almost all questions are important questions.” Other instructors 
emphasized the importance of grouping similar questions by topics 
or learning objectives to highlight common areas of confusion. For 
instance, I2 noted “I’d like some sort of grouping and ranking of 
problems so I know which topic was generating the most questions.” 
To utilize the questions in real-time, instructors favored simple, ag-
gregated displays like top-question lists. As explained by I5, “being 
able to have an aggregated summary list of the top five questions, but 
then also seeing the individual questions because it may be complex 
or may be a little nuanced.” They stressed that interfaces must be 
quick to check and minimally disruptive (I2, I4, I6, I8–I11).

Finally, instructors saw great potential for such a system that

4 AskNow 
Based on the formative study findings, we designed and developed 
AskNow, an interactive system powered by a large language model 
(LLM) to support real-time question answering in large-scale class-
rooms. AskNow consisted of a student and an instructor interfaces. 
We show the architecture of AskNow in Figure 2. 
We carefully structured AskNow’s functionality based on the ex-
plicit challenges and requirements identified from our formative 
study. In particular, each core feature directly addresses the five 
Design Implications (DIs #1–#5) we derived in Section 3. Students 
reported significant difficulties in following fast-paced lectures, 
language and cultural barriers causing comprehension issues, and 
hesitancy in public questioning due to social anxiety. Conversely, 
instructors expressed challenges in gauging real-time student com-
prehension, and wanted effective tools to detect and address areas of 
widespread confusion promptly. In response, AskNow incorporates 
three core phases to directly address these identified needs: (1) real-
time lecture transcription, (2) context-aware question answering, 
and (3) question aggregation. 
4.1 Real-time Lecture Transcription 
On the instructor interface, AskNow provides real-time, automatic 
speech-to-text transcription of lecture audio. This real-time tran-
scription functionality not only provides instructors with a compre-
hensive textual record of their lecture but also creates an essential 
context data for the language model, serving subsequent function-
alities such as context-aware question answering. This ensures 
that AskNow-generated responses are precise, contextually relevant, 
and directly linked to the ongoing lecture content, which directly 
addresses Design Implication #1 by offering students immediate 
clarifications to help them catch up on missed content and supports 
Design Implication #3 through continuous real-time availability of 
contextual transcript information.

On the student interface, AskNow allows students to privately sub-
mit questions and obtain real-time, contextually accurate responses,

4.3 Question aggregation 
We used multiple strategies, including embedding-based clustering, 
to aggregate student questions and provide instructors with a con-
cise yet comprehensive view of common misunderstandings. Once 
students submit their questions through the AskNow interface, these 
are automatically processed and displayed on the instructor-facing 
web dashboard. Aligned with Design Implication #5, this approach 
offers real-time, actionable insights into student confusion. Draw-
ing on our formative study (Section 3.2.5), we implemented four 
distinct methods for organizing and presenting questions, and the 
UI design is presented in Figure 4: 
1. Sort by Time. All submitted questions are displayed in re-
verse chronological order, prioritizing recent student queries at 
the top of the instructor interface. This approach helps instructors 
quickly identify emerging points of confusion in real-time during 
the lecture. 
2. Sort by Topic. To facilitate concise and efficient compre-
hension of complex growing student inquiries, we embedded each 
question into a semantic vector space. Each question is preprocessed 
into a high-dimensional embedding vector that captures its seman-
tic content. Leveraging these embeddings, we performed clustering 
by calculating pairwise Euclidean distances between vectors and 
recursively applying a threshold-based, K-means algorithm that 
does not require a fixed K. When a new question arrives, it is as-
signed to the existing cluster whose centroid is within a predefined 
distance threshold, or a new cluster is created if no such cluster 
exists. This predefined distance threshold, selected through an it-
erative testing to balance cluster granularity and stability, ensures 
that questions are clustered based on actual semantic similarity 
rather than arbitrary or predefined group sizes, thus maintaining 
consistent clustering accuracy regardless of class size variations. 
For each cluster, we use the question whose embedding lies clos-
est to the cluster centroid as the representative “topic question, ” 
and display this question as the title of the block in the instruc-
tor interface. After clustering, the resulting topic-based question 
groups are sorted by the most recent timestamp, allowing instruc-
tors to easily identify and respond to current, collective student 
misunderstandings. 
3. Top 5 Topics. Due to instructors’ expressed need to swiftly 
identify and address the most critical and common questions during 
a lecture, we provided a streamlined "Top 5 topics" view showcas-
ing only the top five most frequently occurring question clusters. 
To maximize relevance and minimize noise, we applied heuristic 
filters excluding overly general or non-productive queries, such 
as requests to "catch me up, " greetings ("Hello AskNow"), overly 
broad summaries of lecture content, or extremely short questions.

instructor input remain visible and prioritized. 
4.4 System Implementation and Technical 
Details 
AskNow is designed as an interactive system with an architecture 
tailored to the dynamic, high-concurrency, and low-latency require-
ments of real-time classroom use. In this section, we report the 
main technical challenges and the corresponding implementation 
strategies. 
4.4.1 Dynamic, Low-Latency Context Management. To precisely 
understand and utilize a constantly changing lecture context in 
real time, we developed a decoupled full-stack architecture. The 
student-facing interface is implemented in Next.js (TypeScript) 
and deployed on Vercel for elastic, Internet-facing scale, while the 
instructor interface is hosted privately on cloud servers for secure, 
real-time monitoring. A Node.js + Express backend on AWS EC2 
1 provides a reliable infrastructure for concurrent requests, with
Socket.IO handling real-time bidirectional communication. 
We implemented efficient context retrieval through differential 
updates strategy. Returned transcripts are incrementally materi-
alized on the server as structured, segmented JSON with stable 
segment indices and timestamps. When answering, it fetches only 
1https://aws.amazon.com/ec2/

the newest transcript segments necessary for context, thereby re-
ducing token costs, avoiding redundant context, and keeping end-
to-end latency low. Explicit segmentation also supports traceability 
of responses against specific transcript spans. 
In addition, we integrate AssemblyAI’s real-time Speech-to-Text 
API 2 to support uninterrupted streaming transcription. Its ability 
to handle indefinite-duration sessions prevents context loss or la-
tency that would otherwise occur from session renewals during 
long lectures. To balance semantic completeness with responsive-
ness, lecture transcripts are segmented into 30-second windows, 
a trade-off identified through iterative testing. Shorter windows 
risk capturing incomplete semantic units, while longer ones dilute 
the context for time-sensitive questions and introduce unaccept-
able end-to-end latency. These windows serve as the basic units 
for incremental transcript storage and timestamping, whereas the 
context retrieved for question answering may span all accumulated 
windows since the beginning of the lecture. 
4.4.2 High-Concurrency, Real-time Semantic Aggregation. To con-
vert the high volume of incoming student questions into an inter-
pretable dashboard for instructors, we implemented a multi-stage 
aggregation pipeline. A threshold-based, recursive K-means algo-
rithm groups semantically similar questions without forcing them 
into arbitrary cluster sizes. This approach maintains high seman-
tic coherence even under rapid increases in question volume and 
ensures clusters remain stable, giving instructors a consistent real-
time view. The distance threshold can also be configured to adjust 
the granularity of generated topics. 
Our technology choices were performance-driven. We delib-
erately avoided computationally intensive alternatives, such as 
running local embedding models or employing LLMs for dynamic 
summarization or classification, as these would introduce unaccept-
able latency, disrupt transcript storage and retrieval, and consume 
unsustainable levels of API tokens in large classrooms. Instead, 
the aggregation path is engineered to remain lightweight and non-
blocking. We selected OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small 3 API, 
which offers a favorable balance of embedding quality, speed, and 
cost efficiency. This design enables accurate, contextually relevant 
aggregation while maintaining low latency and system respon-
siveness when many students interact simultaneously, ensuring 
that instructors receive timely and reliable insights into student 
needs. Additional implementation details—including the full sys-
tem prompt, transcript segmentation configuration, example tran-
script records, question logging schema, and pinned model and API 
versions—are provided in Appendices A–E to support reproducibil-
ity of our implementation.

5.3 Data Analysis 
To comprehensively assess the effectiveness and usability of 
AskNow, we conducted a multi-method analysis that combined ex-
pert validation of system responses, quantitative comparisons of 
pre- and post-survey results, and qualitative analysis of student 
and instructor interviews. Below, we detail the methods used for 
each analysis component. 
5.3.1 AskNow Response Validation. To evaluate the accuracy and 
perceived quality of AskNow’s answers, we performed expert val-
idation using instructors’ ratings. For each course, we randomly 
sampled 100 question-answer pairs generated during the study ses-
sions. Instructors were asked to independently assess each pair 
along two dimensions: perceived correctness and perceived 
satisfaction, both rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly 
disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Because instructors evaluated 100 
question–answer pairs per course, we adopted a shorter 5-point 
scale to reduce their rating burden while still capturing meaningful 
variation in their judgments. 
We calculated descriptive statistics for both dimensions across 
each course. These ratings provided an expert-grounded evaluation 
of the reliability and relevance of AskNow’s responses within the 
context of real classroom use. 
5.3.2 Quantitative Analysis. Quantitative analyses focused on com-
paring students’ self-reported learning experiences before and after 
using AskNow. 
We administered matched pre- and post-surveys to assess four 
perceived dimensions: (1) overall understanding of course material, 
(2) information retention from lectures, (3) frequency of confusion 
during lectures, and (4) time required to resolve confusion when 
confused. Responses were collected using 7-point Likert scales (1 
= strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). To maintain consistency 
across student-facing measures, and in line with the standard USE 
questionnaire [45], all student survey items in our study–including 
both the pre/post learning items and the USE scales–used 7-point 
response options. 
For each dimension and within each course, we conducted paired-
sample 𝑡-tests comparing pre- and post-survey scores. Because 
we ran four related tests per course, we treated them as a fam-
ily of comparisons and applied Bonferroni correction to control 
the family-wise Type I error rate [71], adjusting the per-test sig-
nificance threshold to 𝛼adj = .0125. Following current reporting 
recommendations [72], we report both unadjusted and Bonferroni-
corrected 𝑝-values, along with standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s 𝑑 
for repeated measures) and 95% confidence intervals for the mean 
pre–post differences (Table 4). For interpretation, we use Cohen’s 
conventional benchmarks for 𝑑 (0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 indicating small, 
medium, and large effects, respectively) [19]. 
Additionally, we analyzed responses to the USE (Usefulness, 
Satisfaction, and Ease of Use) questionnaire administered in the 
post-survey. We computed mean ratings for each of the four sub-
scales—Usefulness, Ease of Use, Ease of Learning, and Satisfaction—to 
assess students’ perceptions of AskNow’s usability. 
5.3.3 Qualitative Analysis. We conducted thematic analysis on 
semi-structured interview data collected from 24 students and 3 
instructors. 
All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and 
analyzed using an inductive thematic coding approach. Two re-
searchers independently reviewed the transcripts, generated initial 
codes, and iteratively refined emerging themes through discussion 
and consensus-building. We used the same analysis methods as 
we did in 3.1.3. In this phase, we similarly assigned IDs based on 
the order of study completion (e.g., P1 refers to the first student 
participant, In1 refers to the first instructor participant). 
The qualitative findings complement our quantitative analyses 
by offering nuanced insights into users’ experiences and percep-
tions, highlighting the strengths of AskNow as well as areas for 
further system refinement.

Thoughts about the AskNow Instructor-side Features. Instructors

Regarding the four ways to display student questions, instruc-

For instructors, AskNow offered opportunities for post-lecture

lectures in person, potentially missing nuanced explanations and 
interactive elements that are essential to learning.

pact.

7 Discussion 
Our deployment and evaluation of AskNow reveal promising op-
portunities and important considerations for real-time AI-powered 
support in large-scale classrooms. Students leveraged AskNow to 
clarify confusion efficiently and without social anxiety, while in-
structors found the system’s responses to be largely accurate and 
satisfactory. However, our findings also highlight challenges such 
as managing cognitive load, ensuring alignment with pedagogical 
goals, and balancing immediate support with the promotion of 
deeper critical engagement. In this section, we discuss the impli-
cations of our findings for the design and deployment of real-time 
educational support systems, reflect on limitations of our study, 
and propose directions for future work. 
Our study demonstrates that AskNow effectively supported stu-
dents’ real-time learning needs in large lecture settings. Students’ 
perceived time to resolve confusion decreased significantly across 
all three courses, and instructors consistently rated AskNow’s re-
sponses highly in correctness and satisfaction. Students primarily 
used AskNow to catch up on missed content and clarify concepts 
without disrupting the lecture, motivated by the desire to avoid 
social anxiety, maintain lecture flow, and receive timely answers. 
Instructors valued AskNow’s potential to enhance post-lecture re-
flection but emphasized the need to manage cognitive load and 
align system responses with course objectives. At the same time, 
our evaluation relies on students’ self-reported experiences and 
instructors’ expert judgments collected over a short deployment, 
rather than on objective performance metrics, so our findings pri-
marily characterize perceived in-class support rather than definitive 
short- or long-term learning gains. 
7.1 A Chain of Affordances in Real-Time 
Classroom Support 
Our findings suggest that AskNow enables a unique chain of affor-
dances that collectively foster student engagement and provide 
instructors with richer pedagogical feedback. First, the system low-
ers the social barrier for participation by allowing students to ask 
questions anonymously and without interrupting lecture flow (A). 
Second, it provides immediate, context-sensitive answers to stu-
dent questions (B). The combination of these two features increases 
students’ willingness to actively seek clarification in real time (C). 
As a result, instructors gain access to a more comprehensive and 
authentic aggregation of student confusions and areas of misun-
derstanding (D). This chain—low barrier (A) plus real-time answers 
(B) leading to increased questioning (C) and ultimately to richer 
instructional insights (D)—highlights the systemic value of inte-
grating AI-powered support into live classroom settings. 
7.2 Comparison with Existing Tools 
This chain of affordances also clarifies why existing classroom tech-
nologies cannot serve as direct baselines for our study. Clicker 
systems (e.g., TopHat) provide a low social barrier (A) and enable 
instructors to collect aggregate feedback, but only on instructor-
defined prompts, thus offering at best a limited version of (D) with-
out supporting real-time individualized answers (B) or encouraging 
spontaneous questioning (C). Backchannel platforms (e.g., Piazza) 
similarly reduce social barriers (A) and can encourage some student 
questioning (C), but they rely heavily on human moderation and 
are not feasible for real-time deployment in large-scale classrooms. 
Without the ability to provide immediate responses (B), backchan-
nels also fail to generate the same comprehensive aggregation of 
student confusion (D). By contrast, AskNow uniquely integrates all 
four elements (A+B→C→D), thereby creating conditions for both 
immediate individual learning support and broader instructional 
insight. 
Finally, while existing systems cannot serve as direct compara-
tors in our evaluation, future research could explore broader cross-
system comparisons. For instance, it remains an open question 
whether the immediacy of real-time support provided by AskNow 
yields long-term learning benefits beyond those enabled by clickers 
or post-hoc backchannel discussions. Investigating such trade-offs 
across modalities would help advance our understanding of how 
different technologies shape not only in-class engagement but also 
sustained learning outcomes. 
7.3 Design Implications 
Our findings suggest several important design implications for real-
time AI-powered classroom support systems. Systems should be 
designed to minimize cognitive overload by delivering concise and 
contextually appropriate responses. Offering adjustable response 
lengths would allow students to tailor the level of detail to their 
immediate cognitive needs. Given that a significant proportion of 
student questions were related to catching up on recent lecture 
content, the system could include shortcut functionalities, such as 
a "Catch me up" button, or even dynamic real-time summarizations 
to facilitate efficient information retrieval. However, care must 
be taken to avoid adding cognitive burden through information 
overload. 
Another critical implication is the grounding of AI responses 
in course materials. Integrating resources such as lecture slides, 
syllabi, and readings would enable the system to deliver more ac-
curate and pedagogically aligned responses. Personalization and 
accessibility must be prioritized by offering multi-language support 
and adjustable explanation depth, ensuring that diverse learners, 
including non-native English speakers, can effectively engage with 
the system. 
Moreover, AskNow presents opportunities to not only support in-
dividual learning but also enhance collective learning environments 
[64]. Future designs could incorporate selective sharing of student 
questions through a moderated backchannel, enabling peers to 
benefit from each other’s inquiries without disrupting the lecture 
flow [74]. Finally, for instructors, providing analytic dashboards 
that summarize and categorize collected student questions after 
the lecture could offer valuable insights for refining instructional 
strategies and understanding common points of confusion. In addi-
tion, our findings and prior work suggest that real-time assistants 
should be designed not only to deliver answers quickly but also to 
scaffold active cognitive engagement. Rather than always provid-
ing fully worked-out solutions, systems could sometimes respond 
with prompts that ask students to predict, explain, or connect ideas 
before revealing the answer, or offer brief hints that encourage re-
trieval and reflection. Such interaction patterns may help mitigate

the risk that easy access to correct answers leads students to rely 
on the system in ways that reduce effortful thinking during lecture. 
Beyond these design implications, we highlight two deployment 
considerations–privacy and instructor adoption–that are critical 
for real-world integration. 
7.4 Balancing Student Privacy and Real-time 
Support 
While AskNow enabled anonymous, low-friction question submis-
sion that reduced students’ social anxiety, its use of real-time lecture 
transcripts and logged interactions also raises important privacy 
considerations. Instructors in our formative study explicitly empha-
sized the need for anonymization to make students feel comfortable 
asking questions. Our deployment confirmed this: students were 
more willing to engage when assured that their questions were 
not identifiable. At the same time, providing aggregated views of 
student questions to instructors created new tensions: some stu-
dents expressed concerns about whether their usage data might 
later be linked back to them, particularly for sensitive or personal 
confusions. These findings highlight the importance of designing 
classroom AI systems that preserve individual privacy while still 
offering instructors sufficient visibility to address common areas of 
confusion. Future iterations should explore more transparent con-
sent mechanisms, data minimization practices, and flexible controls 
for what is shared with instructors. 
7.5 Instructor Adoption Concerns 
Instructors valued AskNow’s ability to surface collective confu-
sion and provide immediate support, yet they also raised adop-
tion concerns that shaped their perceptions of long-term integra-
tion. A central issue was the accuracy and appropriateness of AI-
generated responses. Instructors worried about inconsistencies be-
tween AskNow’s outputs and their intended teaching points, as well 
as the potential erosion of their pedagogical authority if incorrect 
answers were delivered in class. They also emphasized the need for 
low-disruption interfaces: while aggregated summaries were useful, 
monitoring them in real time added to their cognitive load. These 
concerns suggest that adoption of classroom AI systems will depend 
on building instructor trust through transparent prompt design, 
opportunities for instructor oversight, and integration strategies 
that complement–rather than compete with–instructors’ teaching 
practices. Addressing these adoption barriers is critical for moving 
from short-term trials to sustained classroom use. 
7.6 Limitations & Future Work 
While our study offers valuable insights, several limitations warrant 
consideration. The study was limited to two lectures per course, 
which may not fully capture long-term adoption patterns or sus-
tained learning outcomes. Additionally, the study was conducted 
within a single university and within computer science and HCI-
related disciplines, limiting generalizability across different aca-
demic domains. Participant self-selection may have introduced bias, 
as students who were more motivated or more comfortable with 
technology might have been more likely to participate. Further-
more, our system operated solely based on live audio transcripts 
without integration of visual materials such as slides, which might 
have constrained the relevance and specificity of responses. More 
fundamentally, our primary outcome measures were subjective: the 
pre–post comparisons capture students’ self-reported understand-
ing, retention, confusion, and time to resolve confusion, and the 
instructor ratings reflect perceived correctness and satisfaction with 
individual answers, all collected during a single week of use. We 
did not obtain objective performance metrics such as quiz scores, 
assignment grades, or delayed retention tests, nor did we include a 
separate control condition. As a result, our current data speak to 
how students and instructors experienced AskNow in situ, rather 
than to causal effects on short- or long-term learning outcomes. 
Recent work further cautions against equating perceived help-
fulness with actual learning. Chen et al. show that, in AI-supported 
note-taking, providing more automated assistance can make the 
experience feel easier while at the same time reducing learners’ 
cognitive engagement and lowering post-test performance [17]. In 
a complementary line of work, Butowska-Buczyńska et al. demon-
strate that varied, effortful retrieval practice leads to more effective 
learning than repeatedly retrieving or re-reading the same infor-
mation [14]. Together, these results suggest that tools like AskNow, 
which lower the friction of obtaining correct answers, may simul-
taneously support timely clarification and risk undermining the 
effortful retrieval processes that are critical for durable learning. 
Our qualitative findings already reflect this tension. Several stu-
dents reported using AskNow when they were late, momentarily 
distracted, or had “stopped paying attention, ” describing the system 
as a way to quickly catch up, while instructors worried that long or 
overly detailed answers during class could overload students and 
cause them to miss ongoing explanations. We did not directly mea-
sure attention allocation, time-on-task, or depth of processing, so 
we cannot determine whether interacting with AskNow ultimately 
helps students remain engaged with the lecture or encourages them 
to offload thinking to the AI. 
Future work should therefore complement subjective reports 
with objective indicators of learning and cognitive engagement. 
Larger deployments could, for example, randomly assign students 
or lecture sessions to different conditions (e.g., AskNow, a traditional 
backchannel, or no additional tool) and compare performance on 
concept-specific quizzes, delayed retention tests, transfer problems, 
and behavioral measures of attention. Building on the AI-assistance 
and retrieval-practice literature, such studies could also explore 
interaction designs that explicitly prompt students to restate or 
apply concepts themselves, or that defer more detailed explanations 
to after-class review, in order to preserve the benefits of real-time 
clarification while supporting deeper learning. 
Beyond these study-specific limitations, future deployments 
must also address technical challenges inherent to real-time educa-
tional support systems. Although expanding access to richer course 
materials–such as syllabi, lecture slides, and assigned readings– 
would likely improve response quality and pedagogical alignment, 
large language models (LLMs) face fundamental context length 
limitations. Directly incorporating extensive course materials risks 
exceeding the model’s context window, potentially leading to de-
graded performance. Future work should explore integrating con-
text management strategies such as retrieval-augmented generation 
(RAG) [26, 43], sparse attention mechanisms (e.g., Longformer [6]),

chunking combined with hierarchical attention models (e.g., Hierar-
chical Transformers [44]), and other techniques for multi-document 
retrieval and understanding to support scaling without compromis-
ing accuracy. 
Moreover, some instances of incorrect responses were not solely 
attributable to the limitations of LLMs but also to inaccuracies in-
troduced during the speech-to-text (STT) transcription process. For 
example, during one lecture, the STT system misrecognized the 
term "crowdsourcing" as "cross sourcing, " leading AskNow to ini-
tially misinterpret the instructor’s discussion until further context 
allowed the model to self-correct. This highlights the critical role 
of transcription quality in the overall system performance. Future 
versions of AskNow could enhance STT accuracy by allowing in-
structors to predefine key course-specific terminology, enabling the 
STT system to better recognize specialized vocabulary and feeding 
more reliable textual input to the LLM [52]. Improving transcrip-
tion accuracy can in turn improve the precision and reliability of 
real-time responses. 
Additionally, our study revealed that both students and instruc-
tors emphasized the critical importance of privacy protection. While Acknowledgments 
the large volume of student interaction data collected by systems 
like AskNow holds significant potential for informing future instruc-
tional design and improving educational practices, it is imperative 
to ensure that student privacy is rigorously safeguarded. Future 
implementations must adopt strict privacy-preserving mechanisms, 
such as comprehensive data anonymization, careful handling of 
sensitive information, and clear limitations on data usage [58]. It 
is essential that the use of collected data does not cause harm to 
individual students and that its application remains firmly focused

Looking ahead, future work should also explore extended de-
ployments across diverse disciplines and course types, including 
both theoretical and applied fields, to better understand the broader 
applicability of real-time AI support systems. Building on the in-
sights from data collection, longitudinal deployments spanning 
multiple semesters could allow instructors to refine and customize 
AI responses over time. With accumulated data, instructors might 
even fine-tune local models tailored to their specific courses, re-
ducing deployment costs, improving pedagogical alignment, and 
enhancing system stability. Furthermore, exploring more efficient 
and less cognitively taxing ways of delivering real-time lecture 
summaries—such as through visualizations or tiered information 
layers—represents an exciting avenue for future system develop-
ment. 
8 Conclusion 
In this work, we explored how LLM-based systems can support real-
time learning and interaction in large-scale classrooms. Through 
formative interviews with students and instructors, we identified 
key challenges around real-time question-asking, cognitive load, 
and access to immediate support. To address these challenges, we 
developed AskNow, an LLM-powered system that enables students to 
ask questions during lectures and receive immediate, context-aware 
answers. Our deployment across three large university computer 
science courses demonstrated that AskNow significantly reduced 
students’ perceived time to resolve confusion, was rated highly 
by instructors for correctness and satisfaction, and was positively 
received by students in terms of usability. Our findings provide 
empirical evidence that real-time, AI-mediated question-answering 
can help lower barriers to help-seeking, enhance student engage-
ment, and offer valuable post-lecture insights for instructors. At 
the same time, our results surfaced important considerations for 
future system design, including managing cognitive load, ground-
ing AI responses in course-specific materials, safeguarding student 
privacy, and supporting instructor trust in AI-generated responses. 
Looking forward, we see opportunities to expand this work by in-
tegrating richer multimodal classroom contexts (e.g., lecture slides, 
whiteboard content), supporting longitudinal use across a full se-
mester, and investigating the impacts of AI-mediated interaction 
on learning outcomes at scale. We hope that our study offers both 
practical tools and conceptual foundations for designing real-time, 
LLM-powered educational systems that enhance learning equity, 
efficiency, and engagement in large classrooms.
