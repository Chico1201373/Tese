Abstract
Recorded presentations have become pervasive in education, yet
they often reduce knowledge transfer to a one-way broadcast: view-
ers cannot clarify their confusion or gauge whether others share
their struggles. We present EchoMind, a system that augments
recorded presentations with a conversational agent grounded in
presentation materials and collective viewer signals (behavioral
traces and affective reactions), alongside timeline-aligned commu-
nity awareness visualizations. In a between-subjects study (N=75),
participants watched a 5-minute recorded lecture under one of
three conditions: video-only, video with a generic LLM chatbot, and
video with EchoMind. EchoMind significantly improved the sub-
jective learning experience across multiple dimensions, including
confusion clarification, in-the-moment expression, and co-learning
perception, without increasing perceived workload. The generic
LLM condition did not significantly outperform video-only and re-
ceived the lowest mean ratings on usability. These findings suggest
that contextual grounding and community awareness, rather than
the presence of conversational AI alone, make AI a meaningful
support in asynchronous video learning.

1 Introduction
Recorded online presentations have become ubiquitous in aca-
demic and educational settings, particularly following the wide-
spread adoption of remote instruction during the COVID-19 pan-
demic [2, 35]. However, as Moore’s classic framework suggests,
effective distance education involves multiple forms of interaction,
including learner–content, learner–instructor, and learner–learner
interaction [49]. Recorded presentations primarily support learner–
content interaction, while offering limited opportunities for learners
to ask instructors clarifying questions or benefit from interactions
with other learners [3]. As a result, asynchronous viewers often
lack timely support when confusion arises and may need to rely on
replay, selective revisiting, or delayed follow-up channels to resolve
it [3, 64, 69].
Live and asynchronous formats therefore differ in when learners
can seek clarification [3]. In live settings, learners can ask questions
and receive feedback in the moment [3]. In contrast, when lectures
are consumed asynchronously, learners lose direct engagement
with instructors and peers, and questions or feedback are more
likely to occur through delayed follow-up channels rather than
during the viewing moment itself [3, 69]. As a result, asynchronous
viewers often rely on replaying difficult segments, revisiting record-
ings selectively, or seeking clarification through separate resources
and communication channels [36, 39, 64].
Prior work has explored several forms of support for asynchro-
nous video learning. One line of work provides timeline-based nav-
igation, annotation, and note-linked access to help learners revisit
relevant parts of a video in context [23, 48, 53]. Another line of work
aggregates collective viewer traces, such as comments, reactions,
and playback behaviors, to make shared points of difficulty and dis-
cussion more visible over time [36, 40, 71, 74]. More recently, LLM-
powered conversational systems have begun to provide on-demand
explanation and interactive guidance during learning [7, 55, 66, 76].
However, these approaches still leave important gaps. Timeline-
anchored comments and learning traces can make difficult moments
easier to notice and revisit, but they often leave learners to inter-
pret those traces on their own rather than turning them into direct
support for the current moment [14, 40, 74]. Conversational agents
can respond directly to learner questions, but existing work places
more emphasis on question answering than on how explanations
are grounded in presentation materials, local video context, or accu-
mulated learner traces [7, 66, 76]. Taken together, prior work shows
the value of making difficult moments visible and answerable, but
less often connects trace-based visibility, context-grounded expla-
nation, and instructor follow-up within a single support framework.

To address these, we present EchoMind, a system that augments
recorded presentations with a context-grounded conversational
agent and collective viewer signals. When viewers have a question,
the video is automatically paused to allow them to ask questions of
an agent that has access to the current position in the video, the pre-
sentation transcript, slides, supporting papers, and other materials
the speaker provides, as well as dynamically accumulated viewer
signals such as prior questions, reactions, and video behavioral
traces. The system uses specialized retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG) [41] to ground responses in the actual content and current
viewing context. When the agent’s confidence falls below a thresh-
old, questions are asynchronously escalated to the speaker, with
human-provided answers integrated back into the knowledge base
for future viewers. EchoMind strengthens learner–content inter-
action through context-grounded explanation, supports learner–
instructor interaction through human-in-the-loop escalation and
speaker feedback, and enables an indirect, trace-mediated form of
learner–learner interaction through accumulated viewer questions,
reactions, and behavioral traces.
We conducted a between-subjects study with 75 participants
recruited via Prolific 1, comparing three conditions: video-only,
video with a generic LLM chatbot lacking access to presentation
materials or collective viewer signals, and video with EchoMind
(25 participants per condition). The participants watched a five-
minute recorded lecture on computer science topics and interacted
with their assigned system. EchoMind users reported significantly
more positive learning experiences across multiple dimensions, sug-
gesting that grounding agent responses in presentation materials
and collective viewer signals meaningfully improves the subjective
experience of asynchronous video learning in this study.
The contributions of this work are the following: (1) EchoMind,
a system that augments recorded presentations with a context-
grounded conversational agent, community awareness visualiza-
tions, human-in-the-loop escalation, and a speaker dashboard; (2) a
RAG-based architecture that integrates presentation materials with
viewer signals, enabling context-aware and presentation-aligned
responses; (3) empirical findings from a 75-participant between-
subjects study demonstrating that EchoMind significantly improved
the subjective learning experience across multiple dimensions,
while ungrounded LLMs failed to provide reliable benefits over
video-only viewing; (4) a design rationale for reintroducing multiple
forms of educational interaction into asynchronous video learning
through accumulated questions, reactions, behavioral traces, and
speaker clarifications (with the viewer-facing subset evaluated in
this study).
2 Related Work
This section reviews prior work on asynchronous video learning,
focusing on its key challenges and how prior systems leverage tem-
poral interaction traces and LLM-based conversational approaches
in attempts to support understanding.

2.1 Predicaments of Asynchronous Video
Learning
With the growing adoption of online education and academic com-
munication, recorded presentations have become a central medium
in higher education, academic talks, and professional training.
Recorded videos offer learners substantial temporal flexibility, al-
lowing them to progress at their own pace and revisit complex
concepts through pausing and replaying [36, 42]. Prior work doc-
uments distinct patterns of selective access and video interaction
when learners use live and archived course materials [10, 26]. De-
spite these benefits, asynchronous viewing does not necessarily
lead to smooth understanding [68]. More broadly, research on on-
line learning at scale suggests that prerecorded and large-scale
online learning environments often struggle to sustain learners’
attention over time and can easily slide into passive consumption
when interaction is limited [17]. Prior work suggests four recurring
difficulties in asynchronous video learning: learners may get stuck
at specific moments in the video, these moments can interrupt at-
tention continuity, it is often hard to tell whether others struggled
in similar ways, and immediate instructor clarification is usually
unavailable.
Local comprehension difficulties in video. Research based on learn-
ing analytics and video interaction data consistently suggests that
comprehension difficulties in video learning often surface around
specific moments during viewing rather than as a uniform loss
of engagement [26, 27, 68]. Prior work further indicates that play-
back behaviors such as pausing, rewinding, and seeking can pro-
vide useful signals about how learners are managing those mo-
ments [36, 39, 42, 72]. This is also consistent with research on
instructional videos showing that learners must continuously pro-
cess changing audiovisual information, which makes pauses and
segmentation important for following and understanding the mate-
rial [47].
Attention continuity and illusion of understanding. Further studies
indicate that these breakdowns are local and temporally situated:
learners may remain engaged overall while suddenly becoming
confused at a particular concept, derivation, or example, or ex-
perience added cognitive load when navigating between a video
and accompanying documents [ 19, 27, 36, 51]. When confusion
is not adequately addressed, learners may develop an illusion of
understanding, believing they have grasped the material despite
persistent conceptual gaps [15]. In such moments, unresolved local
confusion can also interrupt attention continuity, pushing learners
away from active sensemaking toward passive viewing or disen-
gagement [17].
Limited social visibility. Unlike face-to-face classrooms or live-
streaming settings, learners cannot observe peers’ struggles, pauses,
or questions, making it difficult to contextualize their own moments
of confusion [10, 20, 65]. Beyond education-specific systems, the
social translucence framework argues that making socially signif-
icant activity visible can support awareness and accountability,
enabling people to draw on social information to structure and co-
ordinate their interactions in otherwise opaque digital spaces [21].
In video learning, time-anchored comments make peer reactions
visible at relevant moments and can reshape attention allocation
and perceived engagement during viewing [14, 40]. Without such

social references, learners struggle to distinguish common con-
tent difficulties from individual knowledge gaps, often falling back
on repeated rewatching and other high-effort retrospective strate-
gies [11, 27, 30, 42].
Reduced opportunities for immediate instructor clarification.
Distance-education frameworks distinguish learner–content,
learner–instructor, and learner–learner interactions as central in-
teraction types [32, 49]. Recorded lectures primarily preserve the
first of these, while immediate instructor-mediated clarification
is substantially weakened. As a result, even when learners recog-
nize a difficulty, they often have limited means to obtain timely
explanation or feedback at the point where confusion occurs.
2.2 Temporal Learning Traces in Video-Based
Learning
To address the difficulty of revisiting confusion that arises at par-
ticular moments in a video, prior work has proposed a range of
time-aligned comments, annotations, and learning trace systems
that bind questions, feedback, and notes to specific timestamps or
segments [40, 48, 71]. One common form centers on comments
and peer discussion tied to moments in the video, allowing later
viewers to encounter prior learners’ reactions when they reach the
same point [40, 71]. Other systems attach notes, navigation aids,
or prompts to specific parts of a video so that learners can revisit
relevant content in context rather than through detached discus-
sion alone [23, 48, 53]. Taken together, these systems re-anchor
otherwise scattered learning activity to the video timeline, making
difficult moments more recoverable and interpretable in place.
This class of systems contributes in at least three ways. First,
it restructures discussion and feedback around the timeline, help-
ing learners quickly locate relevant moments during review rather
than search through disconnected forums or notes [40, 74]. Second,
it increases social visibility in asynchronous learning by show-
ing that others also commented, questioned, or lingered at similar
points [40, 71]. Third, it can shape how learners allocate attention
during viewing. Research on timeline-anchored comments shows
that learners switch attention frequently between the video and
the comments, and that layout choices affect switching frequency,
retrieval speed, and learning outcomes [14]. Related systems also
use time-synced traces to support collective review, restore a de-
gree of social presence, or help instructors reflect on where learners
struggle [22, 33, 44, 62, 74].
However, these systems also show clear limits. In time-anchored
comment systems, later viewers still need to read prior comments
and decide for themselves which ones are relevant to their current
difficulty [40, 71]. CoKnowledge makes collective comments easier
to browse by organizing them around the timeline, but its main
contribution is still to support assimilation and review of collective
knowledge rather than to generate direct help for the learner’s
current question [74]. EduLive similarly uses transcript-linked an-
notations to recover a sense of interaction in synchronous live
learning [22], while TSConnect aggregates learner traces to help
instructors reflect on where students struggle after class; in both
cases, these traces primarily support awareness, reflection, and ret-
rospective analysis, leaving immediate in-the-moment explanation
less developed [44]. Existing work also suggests that time-aligned
traces must be integrated carefully, since added comment layers
can redirect attention during viewing, and different visual layouts
can change switching frequency, retrieval speed, and learning out-
comes [14]. Overall, these systems make difficult moments easier
to notice, revisit, and discuss. Their limitation is that these traces
are rarely connected to direct support grounded in the learner’s
current moment, the lecture materials, and the explanation being
given.
2.3 Interactive Support in Video-Based Learning
If time-aligned comments and learning trace systems primarily
help make difficult moments visible, conversational support sys-
tems take a further step by trying to answer questions about those
moments when they arise. Although annotations and traces can
help learners identify where to return, they do not usually answer
the specific questions learners form while watching. Recent work
has therefore introduced conversational agents and AI-based tu-
tors into learning settings to support question asking [7], provide
personalized guidance [66], and scaffold reflection through staged
interaction [76].
The main contribution of these systems is that they turn rela-
tively passive video viewing into a more interactive learning pro-
cess. Conversational systems go beyond showing where others
commented or which parts may matter by directly responding to
learners’ current questions with explanation or guidance. For ex-
ample, SAM provides context-aware question answering around
lecture content and positive user experiences and benefits for some
learner groups compared with video-only baselines [7]. LearnMate
offers personalized learning plans and guidance tailored to learner
progress [66]. AutoPBL similarly uses LLM-based support to scaf-
fold reflection and iterative sensemaking through staged interac-
tion [76]. Taken together, these systems show that on-demand expla-
nation can make learning more interactive and give learners more
direct opportunities to ask for clarification during study [7, 66, 76].
However, several limitations remain. First, many conversational
learning systems still depend on learners to recognize their own
confusion and articulate it clearly before support can begin, so assis-
tance remains largely reactive [7, 46, 58, 61, 76]. This leaves weaker
support for moments of difficulty that remain implicit, fleeting,
or only partially formed, such as when learners pause or rewind
without being able to formulate a precise question [27, 36]. Second,
the current literature emphasizes question asking and response
generation, while offering less detail on how explanations are tied
back to specific lecture materials, playback positions, or contextual
evidence visible to the learner [7, 66, 76]. Third, these systems gen-
erally focus on providing automated support within the interface,
while offering less discussion of how unanswered, ambiguous, or
instructor-dependent questions should be handled [7, 66, 76].
2.4 Communication with Instructors and
Human Handoff
Although time-aligned traces and conversational support can par-
tially reduce comprehension difficulties in video learning, they
do not eliminate the role of the instructor [ 22, 44, 57]. Learner–
instructor interaction remains important for clarification, feedback,
and continuity of support in online learning environments [1, 18,

32]. Work on online learning similarly shows that instructor com-
munication and facilitation are closely tied to teaching presence
and to learners’ sense of support in online courses [1, 24, 43].
In asynchronous settings, this instructor support is often pro-
vided through email, discussion boards, forums, or office hours
rather than through immediate question answering during viewing.
Such channels allow learners to ask follow-up questions after the
fact and give instructors a way to see which issues recur across
learners [4, 18, 69]. Research on asynchronous online discussions
further suggests that instructor participation, feedback practices,
and discussion design can influence participation and performance,
although the effects depend on factors such as class size, course
goals, and the form of instructor involvement [18, 69]. Related re-
view work on feedback in higher education also emphasizes that
effective feedback depends on its integration with learners’ ongo-
ing activity as opposed to its availability as a separate resource [6].
These mechanisms therefore remain an important source of human
support when automated or peer-based resources are insufficient.
However, existing instructor communication mechanisms still
fit awkwardly with recorded presentation settings. In many online
courses, learner–instructor communication is organized through
separate channels such as discussion spaces, course communication
structures, and feedback activities alongside the learning content
itself [4, 6, 18]. These channels support follow-up questions and
feedback, but asynchronous discussions necessarily separate some
responses from the original viewing moment [ 69]. For recorded
presentation settings, this suggests a practical gap: instructor sup-
port may remain available, yet it is not necessarily provided within
the same moment of viewing in which the difficulty first arises.
This points to a further design problem: not every question
should be handled by the system alone, yet existing work offers
limited guidance on how automated support should connect to
instructor involvement when AI responses are insufficient or when
escalation is more appropriate. From this perspective, the challenge
in asynchronous video learning is not only whether learners can
contact an instructor, but also how support should be distributed be-
tween automated assistance and instructor intervention while still
preserving moment-specific context for learners and aggregated
insight for instructors.
Overall, prior work addresses asynchronous video learning from
several complementary angles. Research on the predicaments of
asynchronous learning identifies local comprehension difficulties,
interruptions in attention continuity, limited social visibility, and
reduced opportunities for immediate clarification. Time-aligned
comments and learning trace systems improve the visibility and
revisitation of difficult moments. Conversational support systems
add on-demand explanation. Instructor communication and asyn-
chronous discussion mechanisms provide a human support channel
when system support is limited. Yet these forms of support often
remain separate from one another: some make difficult moments
visible, some answer questions, and some provide delayed instructor
feedback. What remains underdeveloped is an integrated approach
that can connect situated clarification, social visibility, material
grounding, dual-role needs, and human fallback within the same
support framework. The next section distills these unresolved ten-
sions into EchoMind’s design challenges.
3 Design Challenges: Conversational Agents for
Online Videos
We derived several design challenges from prior literature and pilot
observations (Figure 2). These challenges guided the design of our
work and may inform future systems that augment video-based
learning with conversational agents.
• DC1 - Loss of Context. Learners’ confusion during edu-
cational video viewing is often immediate and tied to the
specific context [27]. When they must pause the video, leave
the interface to search for information, or reformulate a
question, their attention is easily disrupted and the original
learning flow is broken [29, 55]. This suggests that explana-
tory support should occur within the viewing context, rather
than requiring users to switch interfaces or tasks.
• DC2 - Lack of Community Awareness. In traditional
video-based learning, learners cannot see others’ states of
understanding and thus cannot easily judge whether their
confusion is personal or common [ 57]. This lack of refer-
ence can make learners feel isolated, reduce self-efficacy, and
increase unnecessary pressure when confronting complex
content [43]. Both prior literature [8, 38] and our long-term
observations of online learning show that lightweight behav-
iors, such as pausing, rewinding, adjusting playback speed, or
using emoji reactions, although not formal questions, often
reflect learners’ momentary difficulty or shifts in attention.
• DC3 - Lack of Reliability and Provenance. Conversa-
tional agents in educational settings can produce responses
that diverge from the speaker’s intent, lack supporting ev-
idence, or hallucinate content entirely [ 56, 75]. Without
clear attribution to source materials, learners cannot ver-
ify whether an explanation accurately reflects the lecture
content or stems from unconstrained model generation. This
undermines trust and makes it difficult for learners to cali-
brate their confidence in system-provided answers [56].
• DC4 - Competing Information Needs.Speakers and learn-
ers have different priorities: learners need localized, in-the-
moment understanding support [12], while speakers need ag-
gregate patterns and questions requiring their attention [6].
Educational video learning also carries inherently high cog-
nitive load, and excessive visual changes, frequent interface
updates, or complex interactions may introduce additional
distraction [22]. A single unified interface risks overwhelm-
ing both parties with information irrelevant to their imme-
diate task.
• DC5 - Limits of Automated Response. Not all questions
are suitable for model-only responses. Some involve speaker
perspectives, clarifications of complex reasoning, pedagog-
ical intentions, or suggestions related to presentation con-
tent [70]. Automated generation in such situations may pro-
duce inaccurate explanations, deviate from the speaker’s
intent, or fail to address learners’ actual needs—yet systems
rarely signal when they have reached the boundary of their
competence.

4 EchoMind
EchoMind is a web-based video player that augments recorded
presentations with an LLM-powered conversational agent. View-
ers watch a presentation and, at any moment, can pause to ask
questions. The agent retrieves evidence from speaker-provided ma-
terials, the transcript, slides, and supplementary documents, using
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [41]. When the agent can-
not answer confidently, questions escalate to the original speaker,
whose responses become part of the knowledge base for future view-
ers. The system also captures viewer behavior: pauses, rewinds,
playback speed changes, and emoji reactions. These signals aggre-
gate into visualizations that help both viewers (who see where
others struggled) and speakers (who see which segments need clar-
ification).
EchoMind separates functionality into two interfaces. Theviewer
interface (♂play-circle) supports real-time engagement during playback: the
conversational agent, emoji reactions, and visualizations of aggre-
gate viewer behavior. Thespeaker dashboard (/char◎-bar) provides post-hoc
analytics: engagement patterns, confusion hotspots, escalated ques-
tions awaiting response, and tools to augment the knowledge base.
This separation ensures viewers focus on learning while speakers
focus on improving content—neither is distracted by functionality
meant for the other.
The remainder of this section describes how EchoMind addresses
the design challenges from Section 3. We first explain how the sys-
tem maps each challenge to a design response. We then describe the
viewer interface and speaker dashboard in turn. Finally, we detail
implementation choices. Figure 3 shows the system architecture.
4.1 Design Rationale
We address the design challenges (DC1–DC5) from Section 3
through five corresponding design responses:
DC1→ In-situ clarification ( /commen◎s) Viewers ask questions directly
within the playback interface. The agent’s responses appear
alongside the video, grounded in presentation content. This
allows clarification without task-switching or leaving the
viewing context.
DC2→ Community awareness (/users) Viewers express reactions
using emoji during playback. The system aggregates these
reactions across viewers and visualizes them on the timeline.
This lets viewers see where others found content confusing
or engaging, reducing the isolation of asynchronous learn-
ing.
DC3→ Grounded responses (🕮) All agent responses cite spe-
cific sources: transcript segments, slide content, or supple-
mentary materials. As questions and answers accumulate,
they become part of the knowledge base, enabling consistent
explanations and reflecting prior confusion patterns.
DC4→ Role separation ( /exchange-alt) Viewers and speakers have distinct
interfaces tailored to their tasks. Viewers see the conversa-
tional agent, source citations, and aggregate reactions. Speak-
ers see engagement analytics, confusion hotspots, and esca-
lated questions. Neither interface contains distractions from
the other role.
DC5→ Human-in-the-loop escalation ( /user-check) When the agent
detects insufficient evidence or low confidence, questions
escalate to the speaker. Speakers review the agent’s pre-
liminary answer and supporting evidence, then revise or
augment as needed. Approved responses integrate back into
the knowledge base for future viewers.

4.4 Knowledge Base Construction
EchoMind maintains a two-layer knowledge base that evolves
over time, consisting of a static foundation constructed at up-
load time (Figure 3-a) and a dynamic layer (Figure 3-b) that grows
through viewer interaction.
4.4.1 Static Layer Construction. When a speaker uploads a video,
the system constructs a knowledge base through automatic pro-
cessing (Figure 6). The video’s audio is transcribed to produce
timestamped text (Figure 6-a). An LLM-based refinement module
then corrects recognition errors, merges segments into semantic
units, and aligns timestamps precisely. In parallel, the system parses
speaker-provided slides and supplementary materials (e.g., related
papers, extended readings). All content (Figure 6-b) is chunked
and encoded into dense vector embeddings, which are stored in
the static knowledge base for subsequent retrieval during viewer
interactions.
4.4.2 Dynamic Knowledge Base Expansion. As viewers engage with
the video, the knowledge base grows (Figure 7). Questions, agent
responses, escalated answers from speakers, and aggregate reaction
patterns all become retrievable context for future queries (Figure 3-
b). This aims to create a form of emergent collaboration [63]: early
viewers’ questions benefit later viewers who encounter the same
confusion.
4.5 Implementation
EchoMind consists of a backend server managing data and LLM
pipelines, and a frontend web interface. For the frontend, the viewer
interface and speaker dashboard are implemented in JavaScript
using React. Because emoji appearance may vary across operating
systems and fonts, and because users’ interpretations of emoji can
diverge [5], we adopt a unified visual style using the Noto Color
Emoji font 2. For the backend, the server is implemented in Python
using FastAPI, with data stored in PostgreSQL. REST APIs provide
2https://fonts.google.com/noto/specimen/Noto+Color+Emoji
core functionality for video management, question handling, and
analytics aggregation.
The system integrates OpenAI’s Responses API 3 for LLM in-
ference. We use GPT-5.1 for conversational responses to viewers’
questions. For tasks requiring lower latency and reduced token
usage—transcript refinement, context note generation, and question
classification—we use GPT-5-nano. Automatic speech recognition
uses OpenAI’s Whisper model.
5 User Study
We conducted a crowdsourced study to evaluate the core functions
in EchoMind. Prior to the main study, we ran a pilot study (N=6) to
refine the study procedure and seed the system with initial interac-
tion traces. From the pilot study, we found that context notes would
introduce additional experimental complexity beyond the scope
of this study and that escalation to speakers requires real involve-
ment from the original presenter; we therefore disabled these two
components. The dynamic layer was initialized with behavioral
data and reactions collected from pilot participants as accumulated
viewer signals. To ensure all participants within each condition
experienced identical system states, we disabled dynamic knowl-
edge base expansion during the study; all participants interacted
with the same knowledge base initialized from pilot study traces.
All other viewer-facing mechanisms remained active. Accordingly,
our study focuses on how EchoMind supports individual learners
during recorded-presentation viewing.
RQ: How does EchoMind reshape learners’ experi-
ence of navigating the learning process during asyn-
chronous video learning?
To operationalize the question, we formulate the following hy-
potheses:
HP1. Compared to baselines, EchoMind provides greater support
for addressing the in-situ needs of learners.
HP2. Compared to baselines, EchoMind fosters a more active and
supported learning experience.
HP3. Ungrounded conversational AI fails to reliably improve dif-
ficulty resolution or the learning process compared to video-
only viewing.

5.2 Procedure
Figure 8 shows the study flow. Participants logged into the study
system and were randomly assigned to one of the three condi-
tions. They first completed a pre-task questionnaire assessing de-
mographic information and prior knowledge of deep learning. A
brief tutorial then familiarized them with their assigned interface.
During the main session, participants watched the video and
interacted with their assigned system. To encourage authentic en-
gagement with the interactive features, participants in the QAOnly
and EchoMind conditions were guided to ask at least one ques-
tion during viewing, and EchoMind participants were additionally
guided to record at least one emoji reaction. VideoOnly participants
simply watched and completed a quiz. After viewing, all partici-
pants completed a comprehension quiz and post-task questionnaire.
We instructed participants not to use external tools (search en-
gines, AI chatbots, Wikipedia) during the study. We could not en-
force this restriction technically, but compliance was incentivized
through task framing and time constraints.

5.4 Measurement
We assessed learning outcomes through a comprehension quiz
administered after viewing. We assessed subjective experience
through a post-task questionnaire using 7-point Likert scales (1 =
Strongly disagree / Not at all; 7 = Strongly agree / Extremely). The
questionnaire covered four dimensions:
Usability. For usability of the system, referring to [ 9, 44], we
designed these items:
• Ease of use;
• Learning support;
• System satisfaction; and
• Likelihood of future use.
Workload. Adapted from NASA-TLX [31], these items were as-
sessed:
• Perceived mental demand;
• Perceived success in completing the task;
• Effort required to perform well; and
• Feelings of discouragement, stress, or frustration.
Learning experience. To examine how EchoMind supported par-
ticipants’ learning experience during video viewing, we assessed
the following aspects, informed by evaluation approaches used in
TSConnect and Glancee [44, 45] and closely related work:
• Clarifying confusing points [44, 45];
• Sustaining engagement during learning [37, 45];
• Encouraging reflection and active thinking [13, 44];
• Supporting moment-to-moment expression [44, 52];
• Fostering a sense of learning with others [33, 65]; and
• Promoting active rather than passive learning [7, 16].
Feature-specific perceptions (EchoMind only). For participants
with access to EchoMind, referring to the feature-specific useful-
ness batteries used in prior system evaluations [44, 45], we designed
additional items to assess perceptions of:
• Emoji reactions supporting reflective understanding;
• Cognitive curve revealing struggle points;
• Reaction chart conveying overall learning patterns;
• Usefulness of AI responses grounded in video materials;
• Usefulness of AI responses conditioned on user behavior;
and
• Emoji reactions facilitating active participation.
6 Results
We report results across four dimensions: usability, workload, learn-
ing experience, and system-specific perceptions. Overall, EchoMind
outperformed both baseline conditions on learning experience mea-
sures, with significant improvements in confusion clarification,
expression support and sense of co-learning. Usability was compa-
rable across conditions, though EchoMind showed higher intended
future use. Workload did not differ significantly, though QAOnly
exhibited the highest mental demand. EchoMind-specific features
(cognitive curve, reaction visualizations, grounded AI responses)
were rated positively by participants. Figure 9 summarizes the re-
sults.

7 Discussion
Our results lead us to accept HP1, partially support HP2 and HP3,
revealing that the perceived value of AI in video learning depends
critically on its contextual integration. Validating HP1, EchoMind
substantially raised ratings for supporting in-situ expression com-
pared to VideoOnly (𝑀 = 5.60 vs. 2.84). We note, however, that
this difference partly reflects the availability of the emoji reaction
channel in the EchoMind condition, and should not be attributed
to contextual grounding alone. We partially support HP2: while
EchoMind fostered a distinctive sense of social support (𝑀 = 5.20),
the shift toward active thinking did not statistically exceed QAOnly.
Workload did not differ significantly across conditions, but the de-
scriptive pattern, with the highest mental demand under QAOnly
and the lowest under EchoMind, points to a qualitative difference in
how the two forms of AI support were experienced, which we exam-
ine in Section 7.4. Finally, we partially support HP3. Consistent with
the hypothesis, the ungrounded agent failed to yield statistically
significant improvements in confusion clarification or engagement
compared to VideoOnly baseline. While it also elicited the high-
est mental demand and numerically lower future use intentions
(𝑀 = 4.48 vs. 4.92), these negative impacts were observed as trends
rather than statistically significant detriments. This suggests that
without context, conversational AI may function as cognitive noise,
imposing friction without delivering reliable experiential benefits.
7.1 Reframing Asynchronous Video Learning
EchoMind participants reported more positive experiences on sev-
eral process-related measures, particularly confusion clarification,

in-the-moment expression, and co-learning. Prior work has shown
that learners frequently experience localized breakdowns during
video learning, which are typically addressed through rewatching
or external search [30, 36, 42]. These strategies provide flexibility
but separate clarification from the original viewing moment and
from synchronous peer interaction [3, 10, 69]. EchoMind reframes

More specifically, EchoMind significantly outperformed both

and supporting in-the-moment expression, while the latter two did
not differ reliably. This finding further suggests that conversational
AI is most useful when it is contextually integrated into video
platforms [7, 14, 36, 39].
Beyond individual clarification, EchoMind also reshapes the so-
cial dimension of asynchronous learning. EchoMind aligns collec-
tive reactions and behavioral traces directly with the video timeline.
This alignment allows learners to situate their understanding within
a broader social context, consistent with prior work on social navi-
gation and time-anchored peer interaction [21, 40, 71].
These results suggest that asynchronous video learning can be
meaningfully reframed as a socially grounded process in which
even static displays of prior learners’ traces shape in-session in-
terpretation, a reframing we view as a starting point rather than a
conclusion about cumulative effects.
7.2 Why Contextualized AI Matters
Our results show that conversational AI becomes valuable in video-
based learning when it is contextually integrated. Although QAOnly
introduced an LLM-powered question-answering interface, it did
not reliably outperform VideoOnly in supporting confusion clarifi-
cation or in-the-moment expression. This finding challenges the
increasingly common assumption that conversational AI alone con-
stitutes meaningful learning support. Prior work on AI tutors and
learning assistants has reported positive effects on engagement
and perceived usefulness [7, 28, 56]. However, many such systems
require learners to reconstruct context or formulate decontextual-
ized questions, placing additional cognitive demands on learners.
Our results suggest that without access to learning materials and
temporal context, AI responses are prone to vagueness or misalign-
ment, which may reduce their perceived usefulness and learners’
willingness to continue using the system (consistent with QAOnly’s
lowest future-use ratings).
By contrast, EchoMind grounds AI responses in video transcripts,
slides, and collective interaction traces. This contextual grounding
can make explanations more concrete and easier for learners to
interpret against the presentation materials. Consistent with prior
work on grounded and interpretable AI systems [41, 67], our find-
ings suggest that grounding also matters for learners’ experience
in this setting.
Importantly, EchoMind’s design extends contextualization be-
yond material grounding to include temporal and social context. By
aligning AI responses with learners’ viewing position and collec-
tive difficulty patterns, the system allows AI support to reflect not
only what the content says, but how others have struggled with
it. This integration is consistent with the pattern observed across
conditions: EchoMind was rated significantly higher than QAOnly
on confusion clarification and sustained engagement, alongside a
trend toward stronger future-use intentions. Within the EchoMind
condition, participants likewise rated both video-grounded and
behavior-aware responses as useful (Section 6.4), providing con-
verging—though not directly comparative—evidence for the value
of contextualized responses.
These findings suggest that conversational AI should not be con-
ceptualized as a standalone tutor layered on top of learning systems.

Effective educational AI must function as a context-sensitive me-
diator that aligns with learning materials, temporal progress, and
collective learner signals. Without such alignment, AI risks becom-

support for learning.
7.3 Social Signals and the Emergence of
Co-Learning
EchoMind’s effect on learners’ sense of co-learning highlights the
importance of social visibility in asynchronous video learning.
While QAOnly and VideoOnly showed no meaningful difference
on this dimension, EchoMind significantly increased learners’ per-
ceptions of learning together with others. This result suggests that
co-learning does not arise from AI presence alone, but from the
system’s ability to surface others’ learning traces in ways that are
temporally and contextually meaningful.
Prior work has shown that learners rely on comments, annota-
tions, and discussion forums as social navigation cues in online
learning environments [40, 43, 50, 71]. However, such cues are of-
ten detached from the precise moments when confusion occurs,
limiting their ability to support in-situ sensemaking. EchoMind ex-
tends this line of work by persistently aligning collective reactions
and behavioral traces with the video timeline, allowing learners to
encounter social signals at the moment they are most relevant. By
making others’ confusion, hesitation, and understanding visible,
EchoMind enables learners to situate their own comprehension
within a broader social context. This social reference can help learn-
ers interpret whether a difficulty is individual or shared, consistent
with prior work on social navigation and peer-linked video com-
ments [21, 40, 71]. In this way, lightweight social signals function
not merely as navigation aids, but also as interpretive supports that
shape how learners read their own progress [14, 21].
Importantly, the emergence of co-learning in EchoMind is not
driven by any single feature. Rather, it arises from the combined
effects of emoji reactions, behavioral traces, and AI-mediated ex-
planations that reflect collective learning states. This integrated
design supports a form of collaboration that unfolds across time,
without requiring synchronous interaction or explicit discussion.
7.4 Cognitive Fluency Without Added Workload
Although EchoMind substantially improved learners’ subjective ex-
perience across multiple aspects of the learning process, these bene-
fits were not accompanied by increased perceived workload. Across
all workload measures, no significant differences were observed
between conditions, and EchoMind consistently showed mean val-
ues comparable to or more favorable than those of QAOnly and
VideoOnly. This pattern suggests that EchoMind can support learn-
ing processes without requiring learners to invest additional effort
in interpreting or validating support. Prior research has cautioned
that adding interactive or intelligent features to learning systems
may increase cognitive load and disrupt learning flow [60, 75]. Our
findings offer a contrasting perspective: when support is tempo-
rally aligned, contextually grounded, and socially informed, richer
interactive support does not necessarily come at the cost of greater
perceived workload.
In this sense, EchoMind does not add cognitive tasks; rather, it
reorganizes existing cues into more accessible forms. The higher
mental demand and effort reported in the QAOnly condition further
illustrate this point. One plausible reading of QAOnly’s descrip-
tively higher mental demand and effort is that, without contextual
grounding, learners were required to interpret and evaluate generic
AI responses, which increased uncertainty and the effort required
for decision-making [73]. On this reading, EchoMind’s traceable,
context-aligned explanations allowed learners to allocate cognitive
resources to understanding rather than to verification. We describe
this pattern as cognitive fluency, a state in which learners remain
active while scaffolded by reliable context. In this sense, cognitive
fluency in learning systems is less about minimizing interaction and
more about minimizing ambiguity. When explanations are situated,
traceable, and socially contextualized, learners experience learning
as smoother and more coherent, even when engaging with complex
content.
7.5 Toward a Speaker–GenAI–Audience
Ecosystem: A Design Perspective
The full EchoMind architecture suggests a possible triadic ecosys-
tem involving speakers, audiences, and generative AI, extending
beyond the dyadic interaction between an individual learner and in-
structional content. We present this ecosystem as a design perspec-
tive rather than as a claim validated by the present study. Speaker
escalation, the speaker dashboard, speaker feedback integration,
dynamic knowledge-base expansion, and cross-cohort trace accu-
mulation were not active in the evaluation. The following discus-
sion therefore describes the interaction model that EchoMind is
designed to support and motivates the longitudinal studies required
to evaluate it.
Lightweight behavioral traces and expressive reactions serve as
the connective tissue of this ecosystem. Rather than being treated
as noise or secondary analytics, these signals function as shared
learning artifacts that mediate how content is interpreted and re-
visited. For audiences, they provide social reference points that
support reflection and self-positioning. For speakers, they reveal
collective difficulty patterns that inform retrospective content re-
finement. For AI, they constitute essential context for generating
situated and traceable explanations. Within this structure, genera-
tive AI does not operate as an autonomous tutor or a proxy for the
speaker. Instead, it functions as an ecological mediator, integrating
instructional materials, audience behaviors, and collective reactions
into coherent, moment-aligned support. The value of AI thus lies
not in replacing human interpretation, but in organizing and re-
distributing collective learning signals in ways that are intelligible
and actionable.
The complete EchoMind architecture is intended to support the
circulation of questions, reactions, behavioral traces, and speaker
responses across viewers and viewing sessions. In a longitudinal
deployment, these persistent signals could potentially allow later
viewers to benefit from earlier interactions and provide speakers
with information about recurring difficulties. However, the present
study used a fixed pilot-seeded set of collective signals and did
not involve speaker participation or cross-cohort accumulation. We
therefore cannot determine whether the proposed triadic ecosystem

hypotheses for future longitudinal evaluation.
7.6 Limitations and Future Work
This study has several limitations.
First, the evaluation focused on EchoMind’s learner-facing ex-
perience rather than the full speaker–GenAI–audience ecosystem.
The speaker dashboard and speaker-side escalation were not eval-
uated, and the dynamic knowledge layer was initialized with pi-
lot traces and held constant across participants to ensure a con-
sistent collective-information environment. Because participants
completed the study asynchronously, we also could not examine
escalation as an ongoing speaker–audience interaction, including
potential delays between question submission, speaker handling,
and learner feedback. More broadly, the study did not examine
how the dynamic knowledge layer would continuously accumulate,
evolve, and shape later learners’ interactions in real multi-cohort
settings. Thus, our findings mainly concern context-grounded con-
versational support, timeline-aligned collective signals, and light-
weight expression for individual viewers.
Second, EchoMind was evaluated as an integrated system rather
than as a set of isolated components. VideoOnly served as a non-
interactive baseline, QAOnly provided basic question-answering,
and EchoMind combined contextual grounding, collective signals,
timeline visualizations, and expression channels. Therefore, the
observed differences should be interpreted as the effect of a richer
bundle of viewer-facing capabilities rather than as evidence for the
contribution of any single component. More controlled comparisons
or ablation studies would be needed to distinguish the respective
effects of contextual grounding, collective signals, and lightweight
expression mechanisms.
Third, the guided interaction requirements may have introduced
an engagement-related confound into some of the subjective mea-
sures. Participants in QAOnly and EchoMind were guided to ask at
least one question, while EchoMind participants were additionally
guided to record at least one emoji reaction. These instructions
were intended to ensure that participants experienced the interac-
tive mechanisms available in their assigned condition. However,
they also increased the amount of overt interaction in the inter-
active conditions by design. As a result, differences in perceived
activeness, expression, or co-learning may partly reflect this addi-
tional prompted engagement, rather than the specific mechanisms
through which EchoMind supported the learning experience.
Fourth, the study captured short-term experience rather than
long-term learning. Participants were recruited through Prolific
and watched only a five-minute learning unit, which may not re-
flect sustained engagement in authentic lecture settings. Although
participants were instructed not to use external tools, the online
setting made this difficult to enforce. Quiz performance also did not
differ significantly across conditions. The present results should
therefore be interpreted primarily as evidence about learners’ per-
ceived support experience during a brief viewing session, rather
than as evidence for improved comprehension or durable learning
outcomes. The brief, locally focused study material also means that
the system’s temporal retrieval design was not stress-tested: the
±30-second window may work well for highly localized concepts
such as the introductory example used here, but its effectiveness
for domains involving long conceptual dependencies or cumulative
reasoning across larger portions of the material remains an open
question.
Fifth, we did not separately measure trust in AI responses, re-
liance on collective cues, or susceptibility to social influence. Visible
collective signals may reassure learners and help them contextual-
ize moments of difficulty, but they may also redirect attention or
encourage overreliance on frequently marked moments. Similarly,
grounding responses in presentation materials may improve their
perceived relevance without guaranteeing their factual correctness
or appropriate trust calibration.
Future work can extend EchoMind in several directions. Studies
should examine the system in more realistic learning contexts, in-
cluding longer videos, repeated use, and delayed assessments, to
determine whether the experiential benefits observed here translate
into more durable learning outcomes. Future evaluations should
also allow the dynamic knowledge layer to evolve from real learner
traces over time, enabling the effects of accumulated questions,
reactions, and behavioral signals to be studied across cohorts. The
speaker-facing components, including escalation and the dash-
board, should be evaluated in settings where speaker feedback
can be meaningfully integrated, with particular attention to re-
sponse delays, question prioritization, and speaker workload under
concurrent demand. More controlled comparisons of system vari-
ants would help clarify the relative contributions of contextual
grounding, collective signals, and lightweight expression tools. Fu-
ture work should also examine how learners interpret and trust
AI responses and collective cues, including whether these signals
support reflection or lead to overreliance. Finally, EchoMind could
be extended beyond single educational videos by linking traces
across chapters or sessions and by adapting agent responses to
learner history and group behavior. Similar ideas may also apply
to meeting replays, professional talks, and other asynchronous
knowledge-sharing contexts.
8 Conclusion
This paper introduced EchoMind, a system that augments recorded
presentations with context-aware conversational agents grounded
in both instructional materials and accumulated audience inter-
action signals. By integrating in-situ question answering, light-
weight expressive reactions, and temporally aligned behavioral
traces, EchoMind offers a design direction for transforming asyn-
chronous video viewing from an isolated, one-off activity into a
more cumulative and socially grounded learning process. Through
a controlled between-subjects study, we showed that EchoMind sig-
nificantly improved key aspects of learners’ experience, including
confusion clarification, in-the-moment expression and a sense of
learning together, without increasing perceived workload. Impor-
tantly, our results indicate that these benefits do not stem from the
mere presence of AI, but from how AI is situated within the video’s
temporal context and connected to collective learning signals.

Beyond the specific system, this work contributes a broader

could act as a mediator that organizes prior learners’ traces into

approach has implications not only for educational videos, but also
for recorded talks, professional training, and other time-shifted
knowledge-sharing contexts.
