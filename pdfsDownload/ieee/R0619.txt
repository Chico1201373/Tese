Abstract—Large Language Models (LLMs) face significant
challenges when attempting to utilize knowledge not encoded
during pre-training. In-context learning (ICL) addresses this lim-
itation by embedding relevant information directly into prompts,
enabling LLMs to leverage external knowledge without updating
their parameters. Recent advances have explored integrating
knowledge graph (KG) information into prompts, taking advan-
tage of the structured and factual representations of entities and
relationships that KGs provide. A common approach involves
identifying key concepts in a task, grounding them to KG
nodes, extracting ego-subgraphs centered on these concepts, and
incorporating them into prompts. However, not all subgraphs are
equally relevant to the task, and selecting appropriate knowledge
remains a critical challenge, as irrelevant or noisy subgraphs can
reduce model accuracy. This study investigates how KG-based
information related to task-specific concepts influences LLM
performance on multiple-choice question answering (MCQA)
tasks. We implement a four-stage pipeline that (1) identifies
key concepts from questions and grounds them in the KG, (2)
extracts individual ego-subgraphs for each concept, (3) integrates
these subgraphs into prompts, and (4) evaluates their impact on
LLM reasoning via probability scoring. Our analysis highlights
which concept-specific subgraphs enhance performance, which
introduce misleading information, and which have neutral effects.
We show that carefully selected KG subgraphs can substan-
tially outperform others in semantic relevance. Furthermore,
we examine how the size and connectivity of selected KG
subgraphs influence model performance. Overall, this work
deepens understanding of KG-based knowledge selection in ICL
and informs the design of more effective, targeted prompting
strategies. This project’s source code is publicly available at https:
//github.com/maryam-ghanbari/InformativenessOfKGs.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated re-
markable capabilities across diverse natural language tasks,
yet they face inherent limitations in accessing and utilizing
specialized knowledge not explicitly encoded during their
training. These models are fundamentally bounded by their
parametric knowledge, i.e., information embedded within their
weights during pre-training, which may be incomplete, out-
dated, or insufficient for domain-specific reasoning tasks [1].
In-context learning (ICL) has emerged as a promising ap-
proach to address these knowledge limitations by embedding
relevant information directly within prompts, allowing LLMs
to leverage external knowledge without requiring model pa-
rameter updates [2]–[4].
Despite the effectiveness of this approach, a critical chal-
lenge remains in determining which information should (and
which should not) be included in prompts to maximize
performance. Previous work has shown that injecting ex-
ternal knowledge from knowledge graphs (KGs) enhances
the reasoning capabilities of LLMs in question answering
tasks. Many studies have focused on developing methods to
incorporate such knowledge effectively [5]–[15]. KGs provide
structured representations of entities and their relationships,
offering factual precision that unstructured text often lacks.
By integrating structured information from KGs, LLMs can
reduce incorrect reasoning and hallucinations without requir-
ing costly model updates. Nevertheless, in some cases, simply
injecting knowledge from KGs that are semantically related
to the question answering task into prompts is insufficient
to improve performance. This limitation is particularly evi-
dent in commonsense reasoning tasks, where questions often
require information from multiple KG subgraphs. In such
cases, correct responses rarely follow from a single subgraph;
instead, they depend on the combined information provided by
several moderately relevant subgraphs that collectively support
a coherent answer.
To address this gap, our study explores how integrating task-
specific knowledge from KGs influences LLM performance
on Multiple-Choice Question Answering (MCQA) tasks. We
design a four-stage pipeline that (1) identifies key concepts
from questions and grounds them in the KG, (2) extracts ego-
subgraphs for each concept, (3) incorporates these subgraphs
into prompts, and (4) evaluates their impact on LLM reason-
ing through probability scoring and informativeness metrics
(e.g., Lift, Consistency, Help-rate, Margin Improvement). Our
analysis shows which concept-specific subgraphs enhance
performance, which introduce misleading information, and
which have little effect. We also study how subgraph size
and connectivity shape model behavior. Our goal is to provide

II. R ELATED WORK
LLM-based QA methods fall into two broad paradigms:
those that rely solely on the model’s internal knowledge [16]–
[18], and those that incorporate external sources, notably KGs,
to compensate for parametric limitations. Knowledge Graph
Question Answering (KGQA) enhances factual accuracy and
reasoning by combining textual and structured knowledge
from KGs. Existing approaches to integrating KGs with LLMs
can be grouped into four main categories:
Knowledge Injection during Pre-training or Fine-tun-
ing These methods encode KG information into the model
weights [7]–[9]. Models like KGT5 [19] and RoG [10] fine-
tune on KG-grounded data, aligning outputs with KG.
Embedding-based Methods These represent KG entities and
relations in latent space, using architectures tailored for rea-
soning. Early efforts like KV-Mem [20] evolved into models
like EmbedKGQA [11], QA-GNN [5], GreaseLM [21], and
TransferNet [22]. Though effective, these methods often re-
quire rigid, task-specific designs with limited flexibility.
Semantic Parsing (SP) SP-based approaches translate ques-
tions into logical forms (e.g., SPARQL) that are executed over
the KG [23], [24]. These enable precise symbolic reasoning
but depend heavily on annotated data [25].
Retrieval-Augmented Methods These retrieve relevant KG
subgraphs during inference and append them to the input
prompt [26]–[28]. PoG [29] constructs multi-hop paths and
prunes irrelevant facts; GCR [30] integrates KG structure via
graph-constrained decoding. Some methods frame LLMs as
agents that explore KGs iteratively [12]–[15], though this
introduces latency [31]. While retrieval-based methods are
model-agnostic and computationally efficient, their success
depends on retrieving semantically relevant and complete
facts. Poor retrieval can inject noise, harming answer quality.

Q is the set of all possible questions, and each question is
associated with a set of answer choices C = {a1, a2, . . . , an},
where ac ∈ C is the unique correct answer and all others

C \ {ac} are incorrect. A knowledge graph (KG) is defined
as G = ( V, E), where V is the set of entities and E is the
set of relations. Each edge (h, r, t) ∈ E, which connects a
head entity h to a tail entity t via a relation r, is considered a
knowledge statement . In this view, the KG can be interpreted
as a collection of statements that encode factual information
about the entities and their relationships.
A knowledge extraction mechanism retrieves a set of sub-
graphs Gq = {G(h)k1 , . . . , G(h)km } ⊆G, containing informationabout KG concepts grounded in and linked to the question
q and answer candidates C, via extract (G, q, C) : G × Q ×
C → Gq. Given a question q, we first extract a set of key
concepts Kq = {k1, k2, . . . , km} relevant to the reasoning
task. Following [28], concepts are identified in q using simple
string matching. For each concept ki ∈ Kq, we extract its
corresponding ego h-hop subgraph from the KG, defined as
the set of nodes reachable from ki within h hops G(h)ki ={ v | distG(ki, v) ≤ h }. This process produces a collection of
concept-specific subgraphs Gq = {G(h)k1 , G(h)k2 , . . . , G(h)km }. Inthis work we use h = 1.
These subgraphs vary in size depending on the connectivity
of the ego node in the KG, which can reflect the concept’s
importance within the domain. However, not all extracted
subgraphs are equally relevant to the reasoning task. A key
challenge is selecting knowledge that supports the model’s
reasoning and enhances performance while avoiding irrelevant
or noisy information that could reduce accuracy. Instead of
combining all subgraphs into a single prompt, we evaluate
each subgraph individually. For each concept-specific sub-
graph Gki ∈ Gq, we construct a separate augmented prompt
using an encoding function Γ, pi = Γ(Gki , q,{a1, . . . , an}),
which serves as input to an LLM. In this work, we simply
serialize the statements in Gki into text and concatenate them
with the question and answer candidates to form the prompt.
We then evaluate the model’s performance using a metric
ρ(pi, ac), such as accuracy or probability scoring, to measure
its ability to select the correct answer given the contex-
tual prompt. This individual evaluation approach allows us
to systematically analyze which concept-specific subgraphs
contribute to performance improvements, which introduce
misleading information, and which have neutral effects.
IV. E XPERIMENT SETUP
A. Dataset
MCQA Tasks: We evaluate our method on the Common-
senseQA (CSQA) dataset [32], a widely used benchmark for
multiple-choice commonsense reasoning. The dataset contains
12,102 questions, each accompanied by five candidate an-
swers, only one of which is correct. Answering these questions
typically requires implicit commonsense knowledge that is
not explicitly stated in the question or choices. We use the
development set of CSQA, which includes 1,221 questions.
KG: We use ConceptNet [33], a general-domain common-
sense KG comprising 799,273 nodes and 2,487,810 edges.
Each node represents a concept, and edges capture common-
sense relations such as AtLocation, and UsedFor.
B. Inference Models
All experiments are conducted with Flan–T5–small(80M
parameters) [34], selected due to its effective balance of com-
putational efficiency and performance. We adopt and extend a
knowledge integration strategy introduced in prior work [16],
in which knowledge statements are incorporated into the input
prompt to guide answer selection. Given a set of knowledge
statements G = {s1, s2, . . . , su}, an original question q, and
answer choices C = {a1, . . . , an}, we construct augmented
prompts by concatenating knowledge with the question and
choices:

The model independently scores each answer candidate us-
ing each prompt. For each prompt pi and candidate answer
a ∈ C, the model computes a score sc(a | pi), and these are
aggregated across all prompts.
The model operates in a teacher-forcing mode during infer-
ence, meaning that for each candidate answer, we compute the
conditional log-likelihood of the target sequence by providing
the ground-truth tokens as decoder inputs. In this deterministic
setup, there is no autoregressive decoding (e.g., beam search or
sampling), and the loss is solely determined by the discrepancy
between the model’s predicted logits and the given label to-
kens. As a result, inference is reproducible and not sensitive to
random seeds. We use the negative sequence loss as the score
function. These scores are then normalized using softmax to

V. R ESULTS
Lift Metrics Analysis: Figure 3 shows the kernel density
estimates of the maximum lift ∆Pmax (see Equation 4) and the
mean lift ∆Pmean (see Equation 5) across all subgraphs with
one hop. Vertical dashed lines mark their global means. The
distribution of ∆Pmax is skewed toward positive values, with
a long tail reaching up to 1.0 and a mean of approximately
0.25. This shows that many subgraphs contain at least one
statement that increases the model’s confidence in the correct
answer by about 25 percentage points on average. In contrast,
the distribution of ∆Pmean is concentrated near zero, with a
mean of only 0.04 and a large portion of values below zero.
This indicates that averaging over all statements in a subgraph
reduces useful signals and can decrease performance.
The ∆Pmax distribution shows high variance, reflecting
that some statements provide large performance gains. The
∆Pmean distribution shows low variance, indicating that most
individual statements in a subgraph contribute little positive
information. The contrast between these distributions shows
a key finding: effective knowledge graph grounding requires
selecting the most informative statements (those that drive high
∆Pmax) rather than using all one-hop neighbors.
Figure 2 shows the max lift (left) and mean lift (right) as
functions of subgraph size. Each scatter point represents an
individual subgraph, and trend lines show the general patterns.
The max lift increases as subgraph size grows from small
to moderate, indicating that adding neighbors helps capture
more relevant statements. However, this improvement stops
at larger sizes, showing diminishing returns beyond a certain
neighborhood size. The mean lift remains nearly constant
across all subgraph sizes, with the trend line close to zero. This
indicates that aggregating all statements in a subgraph does
not improve model performance and may reduce effectiveness
by including irrelevant information. Both panels show wide
distributions. Small subgraphs are highly variable, with lift
values ranging from beneficial to harmful. Larger subgraphs
are less variable for max lift, suggesting they contain some
useful information, but the mean lift shows they still include
substantial noise. The difference between max lift and mean
lift trends demonstrates the importance of selecting high-
value statements rather than including entire neighborhoods.
Moderate-sized subgraphs provide the best balance.
Subgraph Consistency Analysis: Figure 4 shows the his-
togram of per-subgraph consistency κ (see Equation 6) across
all one-hop subgraphs. The distribution has a bimodal shape
with two dominant peaks: one at κ = 1 (all statements equally
supportive) and one close to κ = 0 (only one useful statement).
This reveals that subgraphs fall into two categories: Perfectly
consistent subgraphs almost always arise from subgraphs of
size one, where mean lift equals max lift by definition.
Highly spiky subgraphs (κ ≈ 0) are larger, multi-statements
subgraphs where only few statements contributes positive lift
and the rest do not contribute positively to the model. Very
few subgraphs achieve intermediate κ values in the 0.2-0.8
range. This indicates that neighborhoods rarely offer uniformly
helpful facts beyond size 1 cases.

VI. C ONCLUSION
This study investigates how KG statements extracted from
concept-centered ego-subgraphs influence LLM performance
on MCQA through ICL. Our results show that knowledge
selection is more important than knowledge volume. Using
the single most relevant statement from concept-specific sub-
graphs leads to significant performance gains. However, using
larger subgraphs with multiple statements often introduces
irrelevant or contradictory information, which increases re-
sponse variance and reduces accuracy. Margin analysis shows
that well-selected KG-based prompts help models better distin-
guish between correct and incorrect answers. However, these
benefits depend heavily on statement quality and relevance.
Performance drops quickly when noisy or unrelated content is
included. These findings demonstrate that precision matters
more than comprehensiveness when integrating KGs with
LLMs. Optimal performance requires effective filtering and
relevance scoring to identify useful statements while excluding
misleading information. Future work should focus on de-
veloping sophisticated knowledge selection mechanisms that
can identify and extract the most relevant information while
minimizing misleading or noisy content.
