Abstract— This pilot study develops and evaluates an agentic 
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system for hea rt 
failure management, integrating an LLM-as-a-Judge ( LLM-J) 
to autonomously critique and refine a primary chatb ot's 
outputs. The LLM-J evaluates responses on five dime nsions—
accuracy, safety, robustness, empathy, and clarity— within a 
two-stage feedback loop. Testing on 25 synthetic HF  question–
answer (QA) pairs demonstrated significant improvem ent in the 
robustness of responses (+0.39 mean Δ) by adding cr itical safety 
thresholds and escalation guidance, while maintaini ng high 
accuracy, safety, and empathy. A small subset (4.3% ) failed the 
second safety gate due to contextual drift, trigger ing a reliable 
fallback message. The system operated with a mean t otal cycle 
time of 15.74 seconds and a mean cost of $0.00312 p er query, 
confirming its scalability for real-time clinical s upport. This 
work demonstrates the feasibility of a lightweight,  self-
correcting AI governance layer to enhance the safet y and 
reliability of patient-facing digital health tools.  
Keywords— Heart failure, large language models, RAG, 
evaluation, AI safety, digital health. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Heart failure (HF) is “one of the fastest growing 
cardiovascular conditions in the world” [1]. In Can ada, HF 
patients at the University Health Network (UHN) hav e access 
to the Medly program, a digital therapeutic that mo nitors 
parameters such as weight, blood pressure, heart ra te, and 
self-reported symptoms to provide actionable feedba ck [2]. 
Medly has demonstrated a 50% reduction in HF-relate d 
hospitalizations and a 24% decrease in all-cause 
hospitalizations [3]. While patients can communicat e with 
nurses asynchronously within Medly, they lack synch ronous 
and after-hours support, affecting the quality of a nd access to 
care. Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLM s) 
can address this gap. Their natural language genera tion and 
knowledge synthesis capabilities allow LLMs to serv e as a 
“nurse chatbot” when in-person experts are not avai lable. 
However, LLMs are still subject to limitations such  as 
hallucinations, biases, and stochasticity [4]. To m itigate such 
hallucinations, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG ) has 
become increasingly popular. RAG is a framework tha t 
enhances language models by combining information 
retrieval with text generation. It first retrieves relevant 
documents or knowledge snippets from external sourc es and 
then uses this retrieved context to guide the model’s response, 
leading to more accurate, grounded, and contextuall y 
informed outputs. Despite these improvements, tradi tional 
RAG still operates as a single-pass generator and c annot 
automatically correct responses that are incomplete , 
inconsistent, or clinically risky. Furthermore, HF 
management often requires conditional guidance, esc alation 
rules, and domain-specific safety boundaries that s tandard 
RAG alone cannot guarantee. 
Therefore, an agentic architecture—where a second m odel 
critiques and iteratively refines the primary model ’s output—
introduces a governance layer that reduces hallucin ations, 
strengthens safety, and improves reliability for pa tient-facing 
use. In particular, an agentic RAG system that incl udes an 
LLM-as-a-Judge (LLM-J), a secondary model that eval uates 
responses using predefined metrics [5], provides co ntinuous 
quality control and prevents the release of unsafe or low-
quality outputs.

This work describes a prototype LLM-based agentic R AG 
system for HF management (HeartWise), containing a main 
chatbot that provides HF support and an LLM-J that provides 
guardrails through iterative evaluation rounds. In this pilot 
study, we assessed HeartWise on synthetic questions  and 
performed a scalability analysis to determine speed  and cost. 
If successful, HeartWise can be integrated into pro grams such 
as Medly to ensure patients can access the high-qua lity care 
they need when they need it.  
II. METHODS 
A. System Overview 
HeartWise is a prototype agentic RAG system for HF 
management that combines a patient-facing chatbot w ith an 
autonomous evaluator (LLM-J) to ensure safety and c linical 
accuracy through iterative review cycles. The patie nt-facing 
chatbot operates within a RAG framework that ground s every 
response in evidence-based HF content. The evidence  of 
HeartWise was scraped from the Our Heart Hub websit e [6], 
which includes symptom explanations, lifestyle guidance, and 
medication adherence.  
The LLM-J scores HeartWise responses on accuracy, s afety, 
robustness, empathy, and clarity (1-3 scale) [5], r eturning 
concise rationales and edit suggestions for automat ic 
refinement before user release. The 1–3 scale was s elected to 
improve clarity and consistency based on previous 
experiments. Two rounds of evaluation were performe d with 
the LLM-J (Figure 1).  
In the initial round, after the model drafts a repl y, the LLM-J 
assigns scores across all metrics. If all scores ar e equal to 3, 
the response is released without further evaluation . If any 
score is below 3, the system automatically triggers  a 
refinement using LLM-J edit suggestions. The edit 
suggestions include adding advice for ‘if symptoms worsen’, 
or adding more emotional support, e.g., ‘it’s norma l to feel 
worried.’ Using the edit suggestions, HeartWise gen erates a 
new output. Following answer generation, the LLM-J scores 
the new response. If accuracy and safety are equal to 3, the 
message is communicated to the user; otherwise, a f allback is 
activated, which returns a concise verified stateme nt (e.g., “I 
can’t provide specific instructions for this situat ion. Please 
contact your care team for guidance. If your sympto ms are 
severe or getting worse, seek urgent or emergency c are 
now.”) instead of free-text to ensure reliability and 
protection.  Only accuracy and safety scores trigge r the 
fallback response, a design choice made to reduce l atency 
while prioritizing reliability. 
B. Judge Persona and Scoring  
The LLM-J’s prompt emulates a U.S. cardiologist and HF 
specialist¹ guided by a structured system prompt that defines 
its clinical role, evaluation rigor, and safety constraints. It 
operates using GPT-4.0 with deterministic decoding 
(temperature = 0) to ensure reproducibility across evaluation 
rounds. The rewriter prompt enforces plain-language 
phrasing, explicit safety cues (e.g., “contact your provider if 
symptoms worsen”), and defer-to-care messages when 
uncertainty is detected, ensuring revisions remain safe and 
patient-appropriate. Scores (1-3, 0 = crisis) follow five 
dimensions (Table 1): accuracy, safety, robustness, empathy, 
and clarity, with detailed internal checklists.

Robustness Coverage of edge cases, thresholds, and fallbacks. 
Empathy Warmth and emotional validation 
Clarity Readability, plain-language phrasing, and s tructure. 
A score of 0 (crisis) is reserved for situations wh ere the user’s 
input indicates suicidal ideation, self-harm, or ac ute 
emotional distress (e.g., “I want to give up” ). This 
automatically sets a crisis trigger, which redirect s the system 
to a safety response, directing the user to seek em ergency 
help. Finally, the system records each evaluation—n umeric 
scores, short rationales, and up to two actionable edit 
suggestions—in a CSV file, ensuring consistent logg ing and 
reproducible analysis.

The LLM-J applies structured accuracy, safety, robu stness,

penalize missing escalation cues, absent fallback p lans, vague 
phrasing, or unhandled ‘what-if’ scenarios. The rew riter adds 
a final guardrail layer by revising only through ju dge hints 
while maintaining clinical meaning, adding threshol ds or 
fallbacks when needed, and reinstating missing safe ty or 
empathy elements. Together, these guardrails ensure  that 
outputs remain grounded, safe, consistent, and suitable for HF 
patients. 
D. Evaluation Dataset  
We developed a set of 25 synthetic HF question–answer (QA) 
pairs designed to reflect educational patient-style  queries [7] 
while avoiding any PHI. Questions covered five core domains 
of HF self-management: symptom recognition, medicat ion 
education, lifestyle and activity guidance, daily m onitoring 
(e.g., weight changes), and escalation scenarios re quiring 
thresholds or fallback steps. This structure allowed systematic 
evaluation of judge scoring across all dimensions. All 
evaluations used deterministic decoding (temperatur e = 0), 
fixed prompts, and a uniform two-stage judgment pro tocol to 
ensure reproducibility. Because the dataset is synt hetic and 
PHI-free, it can be shared publicly for independent  
replication. 
III. RESULTS 
A. Quantitative Performance  
Of the 25 evaluated QA pairs, 23 (92%) required ret ries, and 
95.7% successfully passed the Round 2 safety gate. 
Robustness demonstrated the largest improvement (+0 .39 
mean Δ), reflecting more comprehensive coverage of edge 
cases and fallback guidance. Other metrics remained  stable 
across iterations, indicating that the iterative pr ocess 
enhanced response reliability without compromising 
factuality or readability.

IV. DISCUSSION 
HeartWise’s two-stage evaluation loop improved resp onse 
robustness and reliability while maintaining accura cy, safety, 
and empathy. Of 25 evaluated QA pairs, most that fa iled 
Round 1 were successfully corrected in Round 2 thro ugh 
targeted rewrite instructions. Revisions added miss ing 
thresholds, escalation cues, and fallback logic, de monstrating 
the effectiveness of the LLM-J in enhancing the completeness 
and clinical safety of patient-facing responses.  
As shown in Table II, accuracy dropped in Round 2. This 
highlights an important observation: improvements i n one 
dimension can unintentionally degrade another. This  finding 
highlights the importance of evaluating all dimensi ons 
jointly, rather than assessing them in isolation. I n the future, 
we will refine and rewrite rules to penalize unsupp orted 
expansions and favor concise, evidence-aligned revi sions. 
Introducing retrieval verification and controlled l exical edits 
will further reduce regressions and ensure factual 
consistency. 
From a scalability standpoint, the mean cycle time (15.74s) 
and cost ($0.00312 per QA) indicate that the system  is 
lightweight for real-time clinical applications. Wi th retries 
contributing only 0.52% of total cost, the framewor k can 
scale to large datasets or longitudinal monitoring without 
heavy compute demand. 
This study has several limitations. The dataset was  small (25 
items) and synthetic, which restricts generalizabil ity to real 
HF patient queries. Given hardware limitations, GPT-4.0 was 
used for both response generation and evaluation, w hich may 
introduce evaluator–model alignment effects. The re liance on 
fixed prompts with deterministic decoding could als o 
underestimate the variability seen in real-world se ttings. 
Future versions will address these issues by incorp orating 
more efficient, modular evaluator models such as LL aMA 
and by testing the system on real-world HF conversa tions to  
better capture natural variability.  
Although accuracy scores remained near-perfect, cli nician 
validation is needed to verify that the accuracy me tric aligns 
with expert expectations and real-world clinical re asoning. 
Future work will incorporate clinician-validated da tasets and 
broader co-design with patients and providers to en sure the 
system’s clinical relevance and safety. 
Ultimately, the HeartWise judge-and-rewrite framewo rk 
shows that multi-round, self-correcting evaluation loops are 
both feasible and scalable. With continued refineme nt 
through co-design, grounding verification, and ligh tweight 
evaluator models, the system can evolve into a reli able 
governance layer for digital health tools such as M edly, 
supporting safe and accessible HF care.
