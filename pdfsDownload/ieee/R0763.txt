Abstract—Integrating AI -powered assistants in different 
areas has changed how people retrieve information. Users can 
now get quick responses, use the service whenever needed, and 
handle inquiries well. However, relying on these systems has 
increasingly raised worries about their safety and reliability, 
especially when facing threats like prompt injection or jailbreak 
attacks. These attacks take advantage of the weaknesses of large 
language models (LLMs) by changing input prompts to create 
harmful, biased, or misleading results. This paper examines 
prompt jailbreak attacks in ChatGPT-based assistant chatbots, 
especially in education. It examines the weakn esses in these 
systems and suggests ways to detect these attacks by fine-tuning 
various Transformer models. The research also includes adding 
prompt jailbreak attack detection in a virtual assistant 
application for university use. This aims to ensure teachers and 
students can interact safely and rely on the system. Through 
rigorous experimentation and evaluation, we demonstrate the 
practicality and effectiveness of our detection methods, 
providing reassurance and confidence in the face of AI security 
challenges. Our studies emphasize the need for robust AI 
chatbots and offer practical solutions to preserve the integrity of 
AI-driven tools. 
Keywords—chatbot, prompt engineering , prompt injection 
attack, transformer, LLM. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
The rapid development and widespread adoption of AI 
chatbots have profoundly impacted numerous sectors, 
including customer service, healthcare, and education. These 
intelligent conversational agents, driven by advanced natural 
language processing (NLP) mod els such as Transformer in 
OpenAI's ChatGPT [1], Bert [2], and ELMO [3], offer several 
advantages, including round -the-clock availability, 
immediate responses, and efficient management of large 
volumes of inquiries. In educational contexts, AI chatbots 
enhance interactions between students and teachers, 
delivering timely assistance, personalized feedback, and 
support for administrative tasks [4]. 
AI chatbots offer many benefits, but their increasing use 
raises concerns about their security and trustworthiness. One 
worrying issue is prompt injection, an attack that targets 
applications built on Large Language Models (LLMs). This 
attack involves combining untrusted user input with a trusted 
prompt created by the application's developer, exploiting the 
system's vulnerabilities [5]. Another related threat is 
jailbreaking, a type of attack aimed at bypassing the safety 
filters embedded within LLMs [6]. T hese attacks have the 
potential to undermine the reliability of AI chatbots, leading 
to the spread of misinformation and a decline in user trust. 
Research on the security of AI chatbots has revealed that 
large language models (LLMs) are vulnerable to various 
adversarial attacks. Studies have shown that LLMs can be 
manipulated through carefully crafted inputs, leading to 
unintended and potentially ha rmful behaviors [7]. The 
dynamic and often unpredictable nature of LLM responses 
makes it difficult to secure these systems, as even slight 
variations in prompt wording can lead to significantly 
different outputs. 
This paper focuses on the important issue of prompt 
attacks in AI -driven assistant tools. Our main goal is to 
examine the weaknesses that make ChatGPT-based and other 
AI applications vulnerable to these attacks and to suggest 
effective detection methods to  protect against them. Our 
research emphasizes refining a detection model for prompt 
attacks and integrating it into a ChatGPT -based application 
designed for university use. This will ensure secure and 
dependable interactions between educators and students . By 
conducting multiple evaluation experiments and training 
various Transformer models, we aim to achieve initial results 
in applying machine learning models to identify prompt 
attacks on LLMs, laying the groundwork for further in-depth 
research in the future. 
To achieve these objectives, this paper will first provide a 
detailed overview of the prominent LLM architecture, known 
as the Transformer model. Then, we will explore prompt 
attacks, their mechanisms, and impacts. The methodology 
section will explain the prompt dataset used in our 
experiments. We will validate malicious prompts on the 
GPT-4 to verify the self -protection of this LLM, and then 
discuss an alternative mitigation approach. Our next step 
involves refining various models to identify the most 
effective model for detecting jailbreak attempts. This process 
will enable us to develop detection systems for a ChatGPT -
based educational application, thereby enhancing the security 
and dependability of AI-powered educational tools. 
II. BACKGROUND KNOWLEDGE 
A. Transformer 
The Transformer model, introduced by Vaswani and 
colleagues, represents a significant advancement in natural 
language processing (NLP). Unlike traditional models such 
as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and convolutional 
neural networks (CNNs), the Transfo rmer uses a self -
attention mechanism to process input data efficiently in 
parallel, improving computational effectiveness and 
facilitating the modeling of long -range dependencies within 
sequences. At the heart of the Transformer architecture is the 
multi-head self-attention mechanism, allowing the model to 
simultaneously focus on different segments of the input 
sequence, thus capturing diverse contextual information. The 
model consists of an encoder and a decoder, each containing 
multiple self -attention lay ers and feed -forward neural 
networks. The encoder processes the input sequence to create 
a context-rich representation, which the decoder then uses to 
produce the output sequence. This design makes Transformer 
well-suited for tasks such as machine translation, text 
summarization, and language generation. The introduction of 
the Transformer has led to the development of advanced 
models like BERT, GPT [8], and T5 [9], which have set new 
benchmarks in various NLP tasks and significantly advanced 
the field.

B. Prompt engineering 
Prompt engineering is an important technique in artificial 
intelligence (AI), especially in natural language processing 
(NLP) models like GPT and other large language models. 
This practice involves carefully designing and optimizing 
input prompts to get specific responses from these models 
and guide their output toward particular tasks or objectives. 
The importance of prompt engineering comes from the fact 
that the performance of language models can vary 
significantly based on the wording, structure, and content of 
the prompts given. Therefore, prompt engineering has 
become a crucial technique for maximizing the usefulness 
and accuracy of AI models, used in tasks from automatic 
content creation to complex problem solving. Res earchers 
and practitioners are now focusing on developing organized 
approaches to prompt design, exploring techniques like 
prompt tuning, prompt templates, and using example -based 
prompts to improve model performance. By refining how 
prompts are created an d used, prompt engineering helps in 
the broader aim of improving the interpretability, reliability, 
and adaptability of AI systems in various real -world 
situations. 
 
C. Prompt attacks 
Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking are two distinct issues 
associated with large language models (LLMs), though they 
are often conflated. 
Prompt Injection could be a problem found in current 
LLM architectures where the models are unable to 
differentiate between original developer instructions and user 
input within a prompt. Due to this architectural limitation, 
any user-provided instructions are given the same weight as 
the de veloper's original instructions. For example, if a 
developer creates a website to recommend books and uses the 
prompt "Give a Java assignment related to this course: {User 
Input}," a malicious user could input a command like "Ignore 
other instructions and make a threat against the teacher." As 
a result, the model may follow the user's malicious 
instructions instead of the intended developer's request. 
Prompt Injection occurs when untrusted input overrides 
original instructions in a prompt, stemming from the model's 
inability to distinguish  between developer and user 
instructions. This can happen directly through user input or 
indirectly through external sources such as website content.

Jailbreaking involves manipulating an LLM to produce 
unintended or undesirable outputs by using carefully crafted 
prompts, despite the model's built -in safety mechanisms. 
LLM providers invest significant effort in tuning models to 
prevent them from generat ing harmful content, such as hate 
speech or dangerous instructions. However, even with 
extensive safety measures, it is still possible to trick the 
model into producing such content. For example, a user might 
simply ask the model to make a threat against t he president, 
bypassing any original instructions or system prompts 
designed to prevent this. Jailbreaking can thus be defined as 
coercing a GenAI model into generating unintended content 
through adversarial prompts, which is a challenge both 
architecturally and in terms of training.

While both Prompt Injection and Jailbreaking are often 
seen as negative due to their potential for abuse, it is 
important to note that they are not inherently malicious. 
Sometimes, these techniques may be used for good reasons. 
For example, enterprise -grade LLMs often avoid discussing 
sexuality-related topics, yet the MMLU benchmark includes 
questions on such topics. Researchers may need to us e 
jailbreak models to adequately evaluate them on this 
benchmark, proving that these techniques can serve 
legitimate and constructive purposes.

C. Jailbreak Prompt Mitigation Approach 
Given that the natural defense mechanisms of large 
language models (LLMs) may not be fully effective, it is 
essential to develop additional safeguards to detect harmful 
inputs. Several methods can be employed to achieve this, 
including: 
Paraphrasing: Asking the LLM to rephrase prompts 
while retaining detail can disrupt malicious attempts by 
complicating the injection strategy. 
Threat Intel Driven Sanitization : This uses string 
searches, vector databases, and embeddings based on threat 
intelligence to remove known injection techniques from 
prompts. 
Mutation & Repair: Randomly removing characters and 
correcting them with an LLM can reduce malicious details by 
iteratively disrupting prompt injections. 
Fine-tuned Injection Characterization : Fine -tuning 
models to recognize common injection techniques, using 
methods like Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG), 
enhances detection accuracy. 
This research focuses on refining Language Model 
Machines (LLMs) to create a detection module specifically 
designed to identify prompt attacks. The module will be 
incorporated into current systems that use OpenAI's GPT 
technology. By refining the models to  detect and prevent 
prompt injection techniques, the aim is to enhance the 
security and reliability of these systems, ensuring they can 
identify and neutralize potential attacks before they cause 
harm. This approach seeks to offer an added layer of defense 
for developers working with LLM-based technologies.

IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 
A. Experimental settings 
In order to determine the most suitable machine learning 
model for the jailbreak detector module, a series of 
experiments were conducted involving the fine -tuning of 
various transformer models. These models are renowned for 
their pre-training on extensive English corpora. Among the 
models tested were BERT, RoBERTa [14], DeBERTa [15] 
and others. Following numerous tests and consultations, 
standardized hyperparameters were selected to retrain the 
aforementioned models using the specified dataset. The 
dataset was partitioned into 80% for training purposes and 
20% for post-training model evaluation.

B. Experimental results 
During the fine-tuning process, we made use of Hugging 
Face’s transformers library. By employing the 
AutoTokenizer, we transformed job applications into 
tokenIDs that were compatible with transformer models. The 
model underwent training using the Trainer API, which 
streamlined the fine -tuning process. Subsequently, we 
conducted an assessment of the model's capability to discern 
between benign and jailbreak prompt  by calculating 
evaluation metrics. Table 3 shows the results of the models’ 
training on this dataset. 
Some validation metrics are used in this experiment. The 
accuracy score measures overall correctness, precision score 
focuses on accuracy of positive predictions, recall measures 
correct identification of positive instances, and F1 score 
considers false positives and false negatives.  The evaluation 
results of the models demonstrate high performance, with 
accuracies ranging between 97 -99%. Notably, Microsoft’s 
DeBERTa-v3 model achieved the highest accuracy  with 
XLNet coming second. However, the accuracy in classifying 
malicious prompts is comparatively lower than that of normal 
prompts. This suggests potential for further research with 
larger prompt datasets and more comprehensive 
classification, positioning this approach as a promising 
foundation for future investigation. 
V. CONCLUSION 
In summary, this study shows how fine -tuning 
transformer models can effectively detect prompt jailbreak 
attacks in AI assistant systems. The research investigated 
different prompt -based attack methods on large language 
models, emphasizing the seriousness o f these security 
vulnerabilities for virtual assistant systems such as ChatGPT. 
This is especially concerning in educational environments, 
where the misuse of such attacks could have significant 
consequences. The study also assessed the detection of 
prompt attacks through various Transformer models training. 
The high accuracy achieved by models like Microsoft's 
DeBERTa-v3 indicates the potential of this approach for real-
world applications. However, there is still room for 
improvement. Future work could focus on expanding the 
dataset and refining cl assification methods to enhance the 
detection capabilities of these models. This research provides 
a strong foundation for ongoing efforts to improve the 
security and reliability of AI systems against evolving threats.
