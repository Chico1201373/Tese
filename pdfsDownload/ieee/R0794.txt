Benchmarking and Error Taxonomy on RadQA

Abstract—Radiology reports are a critical source of clinical
information but are often lengthy, variable in structure, and dif-
ficult for non-radiologists to navigate. Question answering (QA)
systems can help clinicians rapidly retrieve relevant findings, yet
existing evaluations of extractive QA on radiology reports rely
heavily on aggregate metrics such as exact match (EM) and
F1, which obscure important failure modes. In this work, we
benchmark modern open large language models (LLMs), such
as LLaMA 3.1-8B, LLaMA 3.2-3B, and Mistral-Small-22B, on
the RadQA dataset under zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned
settings. We report performance using EM, F1, and a sentence-
level measure (SM), and introduce a structured error taxonomy
that disaggregates incorrect predictions into eight categories,
including hallucination, superset, subset, and abstention errors.
Our results show that fine-tuning substantially outperforms in-
context prompting, with LLaMA 3.1-8B achieving 61% EM, 77%
F1, and 85% SM, surpassing previously reported baselines on
RadQA. The taxonomy reveals that fine-tuning not only raises
accuracy but also eliminates hallucinations and reduces false
positive and false negative abstentions, concentrating residual
errors in boundary-level categories such as superset and subset.
These findings demonstrate the value of combining standard met-
rics with taxonomy-driven analysis to better understand model
behavior and highlight future directions, including reasoning-
oriented LLMs and hybrid extractive–generative approaches, for
building reliable clinical QA systems.
Index Terms —large language model, question answering, ra-
diology, EHR, error taxonomy
I. I NTRODUCTION
Radiology studies inform decisions that affect diagnosis,
treatment planning, and patient outcomes. Each year, millions
of imaging studies are conducted, and their findings are
documented in narrative radiology reports. These reports serve
as the primary means of communication between radiologists
and other clinicians. However, their length, variable structure,
and use of highly specialized terminology often make it
difficult for non-radiologist physicians to quickly locate the
information most relevant to their patients. As a result, critical
insights may be overlooked [1]–[4] or take longer to retrieve
[1], [2], slowing clinical workflows [1]–[3] and increasing
cognitive burden on healthcare providers [1], [2]. Recent
advances in natural language processing (NLP) have created
new opportunities to support clinicians in navigating unstruc-
tured electronic health records (EHRs), including radiology
reports [5]–[10]. Among these methods, question answering
has emerged as a particularly intuitive approach: it allows a
clinician to pose a targeted question and receive a precise
answer. Such a system could help physicians quickly confirm
or rule out findings, improving efficiency and potentially
enhancing patient care. To facilitate research in this area,
Soni et al. [11] introduced the RadQA dataset, a collection of
3,074 question–answer pairs (6,148 including multiple spans
for the same answer) grounded in radiology reports. Uniquely,
RadQA’s questions were crafted from the clinical referral
indications of the exams – i.e. the actual information needs
of physicians who ordered the imaging. This design ensured
that the questions target real-world clinical queries (e.g. “Is
there evidence of pulmonary embolism?”), and it organically
produced unanswerable questions in cases where the report
did not address the query. Each question is answered with
a specific span from the radiologist’s Findings or Impression
section, providing a concise yet complete answer phrase (often
more than a single entity) that can span multiple lines of text.
By framing radiology report understanding as an extractive
QA task, RadQA directly links to clinical decision-making: a
robust QA system could help clinicians rapidly retrieve key
findings (or determine that the report does not contain an
answer), thereby improving workflow and patient care.
Extractive QA in the radiology domain poses considerable
challenges and holds significant clinical importance. Radi-
ology reports are a form of unstructured EHR data known
for their diverse, semantically complex content. They contain
domain-specific language (e.g. medical abbreviations, anatom-
ical terms) and often require expert interpretation. Questions
about a report frequently demand integration of information
across sentences and the application of medical knowledge or
reasoning beyond the text itself. In an analysis of RadQA,
for example, a large majority of the questions were found to
require substantial medical background knowledge to answer.
This makes the task challenging even for advanced NLP
models. Moreover, the inclusion of unanswerable questions
introduces a critical real-world scenario: a system must recog-
nize when a report does not contain the requested information.
Handling such cases correctly is as important as finding
answers, since producing an unwarranted answer can mislead
clinicians. Notably, RadQA was the first clinical QA dataset to
explicitly include unanswerable questions – a feature inspired

characteristics (specialized language, cross-sentence reason-

both the difficulty and the clinical relevance of the extractive
QA task in radiology.
Despite the clear need and the introduction of RadQA as
a benchmark, there remain significant gaps in the literature.
Initial evaluations on RadQA reported only moderate success:
the best baseline model (a transformer fine-tuned on MIMIC-
III radiology reports) achieved an F1 score of 63.6%, while
expert human performance was over 90.0% [11]. This large
performance gap highlights the difficulty of the task and the
inadequacy of early methods. Subsequent research has im-
proved performance by leveraging more powerful and domain-
tuned models. For instance, Lehman et al. [12] conduct a
comprehensive study of foundation models for radiology QA
and show that a fine-tuned BioClinicalRoBERTa model can
reach roughly 75.0% F1 on RadQA, representing the state-
of-the-art for this dataset. This result reinforces a trend:
domain-specific encoder models (e.g. RoBERTa-based) tend
to outperform generic large language models (LLMs) on
extractive clinical QA tasks [13]. However, even with 75%
F1, the machine performance still lags notably behind human-
level comprehension, and the RadQA challenge is far from
solved. Moreover, many of these efforts have focused on
traditional fine-tuning of encoder models, without exploring
the capabilities of the latest generative LLMs in this domain.
There is a lack of systematic benchmarking of how modern
large language models – such as the recent LLaMA and
Mistral models – perform on radiology QA, especially under
zero-shot or few-shot prompting conditions. It remains an open
question whether advanced LLMs can close the gap on a task
that demands both domain knowledge and precise extraction.
Another under-addressed aspect of prior work is the han-
dling of unanswerable questions and the analysis of model
failure modes. Most existing studies report only aggregate
metrics like EM and F1 to summarize QA performance. While
these metrics are standard and useful, they do not tell the
full story of how models fail. Indeed, recent evaluations have
noted that token overlap metrics like F1, though reasonable
for span-based QA, may not be suitable for all extractive QA
scenarios and answer types [14]. A model’s answer can be
wrong in different ways: in some cases, the model produces an
answer where the true answer was “no answer”, or, conversely,
fails to produce any answer when one was present. It might
include too much irrelevant text beyond the gold answer (token
superset error), omit part of the correct answer (token subset
error), or overlap only partially with the gold span (token
overlap error). Without a detailed analysis, these failure modes
are conflated into the same drop in EM/F1. Furthermore, the
challenge of unanswerable questions is often glossed over
– many models will attempt to answer every question, a
tendency that was explicitly addressed in open-domain QA by
introducing unanswerable questions in datasets like SQuAD
2.0 [15], [16], but in the clinical domain this behavior has
not been thoroughly analyzed. To build reliable radiology QA
systems, we need to understand not just how often models
are wrong, but how they are wrong. In particular, identi-
fying whether errors come from inability to locate relevant
facts, misunderstanding the question, or not handling the “no-
answer” case can guide more targeted improvements.
In this paper, we aim to advance the state of radiology report
QA by evaluating a range of modern LLMs on the RadQA
dataset and by performing a fine-grained error analysis of their
performance. Specifically, our contributions are as follows:
1)Comprehensive Evaluation of Diverse LLMs : We
conduct an extensive evaluation of several state-of-the-
art large language models – including LLaMA 3.1-
8B, LLaMA 3.2-3B, and Mistral-small-22B on RadQA.
These models span different sizes and architectures,
allowing us to assess the impact of model scale and
design. We evaluate each model under both zero-shot
and few-shot prompting conditions, as well as in a fine-
tuned setting where the model is specialized on the
RadQA training set. This comprehensive experiment is,
to our knowledge, the first to benchmark the newest
generation of open LLMs on radiology QA, and it
enables direct comparisons between in-context learning
and fine-tuning approaches.
2)Standard Metric Results and Unanswerable Question
Handling: We report model performance using standard
QA metrics (EM and token-level F1) on both answerable
and unanswerable questions. This includes a rigorous
comparison to prior published results on RadQA. In
particular, we examine how well these models handle
questions with no answer. By measuring performance
on unanswerable cases separately (e.g. the frequency
of correct “no answer” predictions), we shed light on
the models’ ability (or inability) to abstain when appro-
priate. This analysis addresses a gap in the literature,
as previous studies have rarely isolated performance
on unanswerable questions or explored strategies to
improve it.
3)Structured Error Analysis with a Detailed Taxon-
omy: We introduce a novel error taxonomy to categorize
the types of mistakes models make on this extractive QA
task. Each non-Exact-Match prediction is assigned ex-
actly one of seven error types; Gold No-Answer (model
predicts an answer when it should not), Prediction No-
Answer (the ground-truth has an answer span, but the
model gave no answer), Hallucination (prediction is not
in the clinical note.), No Overlap (the prediction has no
overlap with the gold answer at all), Token Superset
(the prediction strictly contains the gold answer text
plus additional tokens), Token Subset (the prediction
is contained inside the gold answer, but incomplete),
or Token Overlap (the prediction intersects with the
gold answer but neither is a subset of the other). By

II. R ELATED WORK
The task of clinical QA has received increasing attention in
biomedical NLP due to its potential to improve access to in-
formation in unstructured EHRs. Among various subdomains,
radiology report comprehension has emerged as a critical focus
area because of the high density of clinically relevant infor-
mation and the complexity of interpreting narrative radiology
findings. While several QA datasets have been developed in
the clinical domain, such as emrQA [17] and PubMedQA
[18], the RadQA dataset stands out for its emphasis on real-
world, patient-specific questions derived from radiology report
referral indications and its explicit inclusion of unanswerable
questions.
A. BERT-based Clinical QA Models on RadQA:
Early work on RadQA by Soni et al. [11] benchmarked
a range of transformer-based models fine-tuned on clinical
and biomedical corpora, including ClinicalBERT [19] and
BioBERT [20]. These models achieved moderate performance,
with Bio+Discharge Summary BERT reaching an F1 score of
63.6%, highlighting the difficulty of the extractive QA task
in this domain. ClinicalBERT, though pretrained on discharge
summaries, struggled to generalize well to radiology language,
which is often more concise and fragmented.
Subsequent work by Lehman et al. [12] explored the use of
larger and more specialized encoder-based models, including
BioClinicalRoBERTa and ClinicalLongformer, showing that
domain-specific pretraining combined with extended input
contexts can significantly improve extractive QA performance.
In particular, a Longformer model fine-tuned on RadQA
achieved a new state-of-the-art F1 score of 75.0%, demon-
strating the value of extended context modeling and domain
adaptation. Despite these gains, these models still rely heavily
on large-scale supervision and are limited by their fixed
context size and architecture constraints.
B. Large Language Models for Clinical Question Answering:
The advent of large language models (LLMs) such as
GPT-3 [21], GPT-4 [22], PaLM [23], and LLaMA [24] has
transformed open-domain question answering. These models,
with billions of parameters and broad pretraining, demon-
strate strong zero- and few-shot reasoning capabilities across
tasks. However, their applicability to domain-specific extrac-
tive QA—particularly in radiology—remains underexplored.
In many cases, LLMs exhibit hallucinations or overly general
responses when applied to structured medical data, especially
when the answer must be tied precisely to a span within a
source document.
Lehman et al. [12] conducted a comprehensive study eval-
uating several foundation models—including GPT-3, GPT-4,
and ChatGPT—on clinical QA tasks. Their findings show
that while general-purpose LLMs perform surprisingly well
in low-data settings, smaller, fine-tuned clinical models can
still outperform them on extractive QA benchmarks like
RadQA. For instance, they report that fine-tuned domain
models consistently deliver more precise span predictions and
handle answerability with greater reliability, even as GPT-
based models struggle with unanswerable questions or over-
generate responses.
While models like Med-PaLM [25] have pushed the bound-
aries of generative QA in medicine, there is a notable lack
of systematic evaluation of the latest open models (such
as LLaMA 3.1/3.2, and Mistral-small) on extractive clinical
datasets like RadQA. Moreover, most prior studies have not
examined how these LLMs perform under different prompting
strategies (zero-shot vs. few-shot) or compared their effective-
ness to fine-tuned variants. This gap motivates our present
study.
C. Evaluation and Error Analysis in Extractive QA:
Evaluation in clinical QA has primarily relied on traditional
metrics such as EM and token-level F1 [15]. While useful,
these metrics can obscure the nuanced ways in which a model
may succeed or fail. For example, a model might provide a
partial but clinically relevant answer and still be penalized as
incorrect. Few works have gone beyond aggregate metrics to
conduct detailed error analysis in extractive QA tasks.
In the context of RadQA, annotators have previously used
qualitative labels such as “missed relevant info” or “insuffi-
cient specificity” to categorize inter-annotator disagreements
[11]. However, no study to date has applied a structured error
taxonomy to model predictions. This leaves open the question
of how LLMs fail on extractive clinical QA, especially when
faced with unanswerable questions or ambiguous spans. Cat-
egorizing these errors (such as identifying when a predicted
span is a token superset, token subset, contains no overlap with
the reference, or is even in the right “neighborhood” of the
correct answer) can yield deeper insights into model behavior
and reliability.

III. M ETHODS
A. Dataset
RadQA [11] consists of 1,009 radiology reports paired
with 6,148 clinically motivated questions and span-based gold
answers. Each question is derived from the clinical referral
indication associated with the imaging study and is answered,
when possible, by a contiguous span from the report text.
Importantly, a portion of the questions (1,754, 28.5%) are
explicitly labeled as unanswerable, meaning the radiology
report contains no information relevant to the physician’s
query. This feature reflects real-world clinical practice, where
a clinician’s information need is not always addressed in the
radiologist’s interpretation.
In addition to overall dataset size, we further characterize
RadQA in terms of answerability and answer span length, as
these properties directly influence task difficulty and model
behavior. For answerable questions, gold answer spans vary
substantially in length, ranging from short atomic phrases to
multi-clause clinical descriptions. The average answer span
length is 16.2 tokens, with a median of 7 tokens, indicating
a skewed distribution with a long tail of longer spans. By
definition, unanswerable questions have an answer length of
zero. This variability highlights the dual challenge of correctly
determining answerability while also extracting precise bound-
aries for answers of heterogeneous length.
For all experiments, we followed the official RadQA split:
the training set was used for fine-tuning, and the test set was
held out for evaluation. The same test set was used consistently
across the zero-shot, few-shot (5-, 10-, 15-shot), and fine-tuned
model evaluations to ensure comparability of results.
In the few-shot prompting setting, we manually curated a set
of 15 representative examples from the training split (Table I)
to serve as in-context demonstrations. Selection was guided by
coverage across answerability (both answerable and unanswer-
able cases), span length (short atomic spans and longer clause-
level answers), and clinical content (device/line placement,
interval change, negative findings, anatomy variety).
To enable controlled comparisons across different few-shot
sizes, we used a nested selection protocol: the 5-shot subset is
contained within the 10-shot subset, which is contained within
the 15-shot subset. This approach allows us to isolate the
incremental effect of adding demonstrations, while keeping
the composition of smaller sets consistent.
We did not apply additional preprocessing or filtering to
the dataset. Each report was presented to the models in its
entirety, including Findings, Impression, and any additional
sections. This design preserved all potentially relevant context
for answering the question and mirrored the natural input
length encountered in clinical practice.
B. Task F ormulation
We frame the problem as an extractive QA task over
radiology reports. Given the full text of a clinical note R
and a clinician-posed question Q, the goal is to predict an
answer span A that is a contiguous sequence of tokens from
R that fully answers Q. If no such span exists in the report, the
correct output is an empty string, indicating that the question
is unanswerable based on the report’s content.
A ⊆ R ∨ A = ∅. (1)
During inference, we instructed the LLMs to return exactly
the string “None” when they determined that no answer
was present. For evaluation purposes, these “None” predic-
tions were mapped to empty strings so they matched the
gold-standard representation of unanswerable questions in the
RadQA dataset.
Our evaluation explicitly considers both answerable and
unanswerable cases. When both the gold answer and the
model prediction are empty, this is treated as an Exact Match
category, since the model correctly abstained from answering.
In contrast, misclassified unanswerable cases fall into two error
categories in our taxonomy (Section III-D):
• Gold No Answer (GNA): the gold answer is empty but
the model predicts a span.
• Pred No Answer (PNA): the gold answer contains a
span but the model outputs no answer.
This design ensures that models are assessed not only on
their ability to extract correct spans when an answer exists but
also on their ability to abstain appropriately when an answer
is absent.
C. Model Setup
We evaluated three LLMs: LLaMA 3.1-8B, LLaMA 3.2-
3B, and Mistral-Small-22B. All evaluated models are general-
purpose open LLMs rather than radiology- or clinically spe-
cialized language models. This choice allows us to assess how
far broadly trained, reproducible models can be adapted to
extractive radiology QA through prompting and task-specific
fine-tuning, independent of domain-specific pretraining. We
used the base variants rather than instruction-tuned (chat)
versions, in order to directly assess their raw extractive ca-
pabilities. Each model was evaluated under three paradigms:
• Zero-shot prompting: the model is given only the task
description and the test instance without any examples.
• Few-shot prompting: the model was given k = 5, 10, 15
representative examples from the RadQA training set.
• Fine-tuning: the models were trained on the RadQA
training set using LoRA applied to both attention and
feed-forward layers.
For inference, we used the Ollama framework [26], which
provided a standardized interface for model loading and execu-
tion. This allowed us to run zero-shot and few-shot prompting
experiments reproducibly across all model families.
For fine-tuning, we employed the Unsloth [27] and Hugging
Face Transformers [28] libraries. Hugging Face tokenizers
were used to ensure consistent text preprocessing, while Un-
sloth enabled efficient parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA)
with 4-bit quantization. This hybrid setup ensured both re-
source efficiency and comparability across models.

D. Evaluation
Model outputs were normalized and evaluated using the
official SQuAD v2.0 evaluation protocol, which extends the
original SQuAD metric formulation [15] to explicitly support
unanswerable questions [16]. We used the publicly released
evaluation script ( evaluate-v2.0.py) 1 provided by the
SQuAD authors, which applies standard text normalization
including lowercasing, punctuation removal, article removal,
and whitespace normalization. EM and token-level F1 were
computed following this protocol, with correct abstentions
(both gold and prediction empty) treated as exact matches.
The normalization steps applied were:
1) Lowercasing all text.
2) Removing punctuation characters.
3) Removing the English articles “a”, “an”, and “the”.
4) Collapsing multiple spaces into a single space.
Unanswerable questions are represented in the gold data as
an empty string. To ensure consistency, any model output of
“None” was replaced with “” prior to evaluation. This aligns
with the gold standard for unanswerable cases, ensuring that
correct abstentions are rewarded.
Evaluation Metrics: We evaluated models using three
complementary metrics:
• EM: The percentage of predictions that exactly match a
gold answer based on SQuAD matching policy.
• Token-level F1: The harmonic mean of precision and
recall computed over tokens in the normalized prediction
and gold answer. Both EM and F1 are computed per
question and micro-averaged over the dataset.
• SM: A sentence-level measure of alignment between gold
and predicted spans. Each clinical note was segmented
into sentences and each gold/pred span mapped to the
set of sentence indices it covered. SM is defined as:
1) If both gold and prediction are empty:
SM = 100% (2)
2) Else:
SM = |G ∩ P |max(|G|,| P| ) ×100 ,(3)
whereG is the gold sentence set and P the predicted sentence
set. One could imagine a QA system that returns the sentences
containing the answer instead of the answer span itself. SM
then measures the accuracy of such (relaxed) approach. It also
helps to identify if the LLM is “close” to the right answer
within the document.
To account for minor lexical variation, we introduced a
token-difference threshold when assigning predictions to
sentences. If the token difference between a predicted span and
a strict subset of a sentence was equal or less than threshold,
the sentence index was included in P . The threshold was
further adjusted for very short predictions: if |pred tokens|4 <1 ,
the threshold was reduced stepwise until it reached zero.
This ensured that small lexical differences (e.g., “situation”
vs. “sutuation”) did not prevent correct sentence alignment.
Together, SQuAD EM and F1 scores capture exact and
token-level accuracy, while SM provides a complementary

For questions with multiple gold answers, the score for

In addition to overall scores, we further categorized each
prediction into one of eight answer types to provide a
fine-grained breakdown of model performance on answerable
versus unanswerable questions.
Error Taxonomy: To obtain fine-grained insights into
model behavior beyond EM and F1, we designed a structured
error taxonomy. Each prediction is deterministically assigned
to exactly one of eight mutually exclusive categories.
Because each question in RadQA may have two gold
answers (one each in the Findings and Impressions sections),
we compared the prediction against all gold spans and selected
the gold yielding the higher F1 score.
Formally, let G denote the selected gold span, P the pre-
dicted span, and R the source report. Set operations ( ∩, ⊂, ⊃)
are defined over tokenized spans. The taxonomy is defined as
follows:
1) Exact Match: Prediction exactly matches the gold span
(including the case where both are empty).
P = G, (4)
2) Gold No Answer (GNA): Gold is empty but the model
outputs a non-empty span.
(G = ∅) ∧ (P ̸= ∅), (5)
3) Pred No Answer (PNA): Gold span is non-empty but
the model predicts nothing.
(G ̸= ∅) ∧ (P = ∅), (6)
4) Hallucination: Predicted span is not present in the
source report (after thresholded comparison).
P ̸⊆ R, (7)
5) No Overlap: Prediction and gold share no tokens.
(G ̸= ∅) ∧ (P ̸= ∅) ∧ (P ∩ G = ∅), (8)
6) Token Superset: Prediction strictly contains the gold
span plus additional tokens.
(G ̸= ∅) ∧ (P ̸= ∅) ∧ (G ⊂ P ), (9)
7) Token Subset: Prediction is strictly contained within the
gold span.
(G ̸= ∅) ∧ (P ̸= ∅) ∧ (P ⊂ G), (10)
8) Token Overlap: Prediction and gold overlap partially,
but neither fully contains the other.
(P ∩ G ̸= ∅) ∧ (P ̸⊂ G) ∧ (G ̸⊂ P ). (11)
This taxonomy ensures that every prediction falls into ex-
actly one category, capturing both boundary errors (superset,
subset, overlap), answerability errors (GNA, PNA), and
severe failures (no overlap, hallucination), while rewarding
correct abstentions as Exact Match .
While EM and token-level F1 are standard for extractive
QA, they do not fully capture semantic or clinical correct-
ness. A prediction may be clinically acceptable despite minor
boundary deviations, or clinically misleading despite token
overlap. We therefore complement these metrics with SM
and taxonomy-based error analysis to better reflect practical
reliability.
IV. R ESULTS
Table III summarizes aggregate performance for the eval-
uated models, with the best-performing scores per metric
highlighted in bold for ease of comparison. Across all settings,
fine-tuning provides the largest improvements. For LLaMA
3.1-8B, performance increases from 25.7% EM, 55.4% F1,
and 63.4% SM in the zero-shot setting to 33.9% EM, 63.6%
F1, and 71.9% SM with 15-shot prompting, and further to
almost 61% EM, 77% F1, and 85% SM after fine-tuning.
LLaMA 3.2-3B follows the same trend, with fine-tuned results
of 55.7% EM, 74.3% F1, and 82.2% SM. These outcomes con-
firm that task-specific supervision yields substantially stronger
extractive QA performance than in-context prompting alone.
These results, while still below human performance on RadQA
(estimated above 90.0% F1), substantially narrow the gap
compared to prior automated systems. In particular, the fine-
tuned LLaMA 3.1-8B model achieves 77.0% F1, surpassing
the previously reported best performance of approximately
75.0% F1 from BioClinicalRoBERTa on RadQA. Few-shot
prompting improves over zero-shot, but the effect is modest
and insufficient to close the gap with fine-tuned models.
Mistral-Small-22B behaved differently and underperformed
relative to both LLaMA models. In zero- and few-shot con-
ditions, EM and F1 scores are very low (e.g., 4.9% EM
and 13.0% F1 in zero-shot), though SM is higher (around
33–40%), indicating that the model often identifies the correct
region of the report but fails to extract accurate spans. After
fine-tuning, SM rises substantially to 75.3%, but EM and F1
remain low relative to the LLaMA models. This suggests that
Mistral benefits from supervision at the sentence level yet
still struggles with precise boundary extraction. In summary,
Mistral consistently underperforms relative to the LLaMA
models, with weak EM and F1 scores and a persistent tendency
to abstain, even after fine-tuning.

V. D ISCUSSION
The results highlight several key observations. Fine-tuning
consistently outperforms zero-shot and few-shot prompting
across all models. The largest gains are seen for LLaMA 3.1-
8B, which achieves state-of-the-art performance on RadQA
with 77% F1, indicating that task-specific supervision re-
mains crucial for extractive radiology QA. Few-shot prompting
improves performance compared to zero-shot, but its effect
is limited and insufficient to close the gap with fine-tuned
models. These findings reinforce the importance of domain
adaptation when applying large language models to clinical
QA.
The error taxonomy in Table IV provides additional insights
into how models fail and how their errors evolve with su-
pervision. Fine-tuning shifts the distribution of errors toward
more favorable categories such as Exact Match, Superset, and
Subset, while reducing severe errors including Hallucination,
GNA, and PNA. Hallucinations are particularly concerning in
clinical settings because they introduce content not present
in the source note; their elimination after fine-tuning is an
encouraging sign of improved reliability. Among GNA and
PNA errors, the relative risk is context-dependent: GNA rep-
resents a false positive where the model invents an answer,
which can mislead clinicians, while PNA represents a false
negative where the model abstains despite an available answer,
which reduces utility but is less dangerous. Importantly, these
two error types have different clinical implications. GNA
represents a false positive, where the model produces an
answer despite the report not containing relevant information;
such errors pose higher clinical risk because they may mislead
downstream decision-making. In contrast, PNA represents a
false negative, where the model abstains despite an available
answer; while undesirable, this behavior is generally less
harmful clinically, as it reflects uncertainty rather than fabri-
cation [29]. Our results show that fine-tuned LLaMA models
reduce both error types substantially, while hallucinations—the
most severe failure mode—are entirely eliminated after fine-
tuning. These findings underscore the importance of jointly
evaluating answerability and extraction accuracy in clinical
QA. The LLaMA models show balanced reductions in both
categories, whereas Mistral initially exhibits very high PNA,
indicating a strong tendency to abstain. Although fine-tuning
mitigates this behavior, its performance remains lower than
the LLaMA family. Our error taxonomy further highlights
distinct patterns of model behavior. Superset errors indicate
that models often err on the side of verbosity, producing
answers that include the gold span but extend beyond it. Subset
errors, by contrast, reflect under-extraction, where models
identify only part of the correct span. These complementary
tendencies show that models are frequently close to the correct
answer but struggle with precise boundary alignment. Overlap
errors capture partial correctness when the prediction intersects
the gold span without fully containing it, while No Over-
lap represents a more severe mislocalization. Hallucination
remains the most dangerous category clinically, as it intro-
duces content not present in the source report. Hallucinated
answers were observed primarily in zero-shot and few-shot
settings, where models attempted to respond to clinically
plausible questions despite the absence of explicit supporting
evidence in the report. In some cases the hallucination was
relatively harmless, reflecting information in the report but
with terminological or word order differences. However, this
is still not ideal: answers should be grounded in the report

Our error taxonomy further helps uncover failure modes that
aggregate metrics obscure. By disaggregating errors into cate-
gories such as superset, subset, no overlap, and hallucination,
we were able to identify concrete patterns of model weak-
nesses. For example, the prevalence of superset errors suggests
that models often err on the side of verbosity, whereas subset
errors highlight a tendency to under-extract. Such insights can
inform targeted interventions, including modifying prompts,
refining training objectives, or designing hybrid pipelines that
integrate extractive and generative approaches.
SM acts as a complementary measure of correctness by
rewarding models that at least identify the correct sentence
containing the gold answer. Its close correspondence to the
sum of Exact Match, Superset, Subset, and Overlap in Table IV
suggests that many errors arise from boundary misalignment
rather than failures of sentence-level localization. This obser-
vation highlights practical opportunities for improvement: con-
straining predicted spans to sentence boundaries, or integrating
sentence-level retrieval as a pre-filter, could reduce superset
and overlap errors and improve Exact Match accuracy.
The structured error patterns revealed by our taxonomy
suggest several concrete algorithmic directions for improving
extractive radiology QA. First, persistent boundary errors
indicate the potential benefit of sentence-level localization
followed by constrained span extraction, where models are
first trained or prompted to identify the most relevant sentence
before predicting a token-level span. Second, boundary over-
and under-extraction could be mitigated through substring-
constrained decoding or post-hoc span normalization, restrict-
ing outputs to exact substrings of the source report. Third,
remaining abstention errors suggest the need for explicit
abstention calibration, for example by thresholding model
confidence or likelihood ratios when deciding whether to
produce an answer versus abstain. While evaluating these
strategies is beyond the scope of this benchmark study, our
error taxonomy provides a principled framework for guiding
and assessing such future algorithmic improvements.
This study is not without limitations. First, we focused
on a select set of open LLaMA and Mistral models. We
acknowledge that language models pretrained or specialized
on medical or radiology-specific corpora may ultimately be
better suited for extractive radiology QA, particularly for pre-
cise boundary detection and domain-sensitive interpretation.
Broader comparisons across diverse model families, including
encoder-based clinical transformers and larger closed-source
models, would provide a more comprehensive landscape of
performance. Second, although RadQA captures clinically
motivated questions, it remains limited in size relative to
other open-domain QA datasets. Larger and more diverse
training corpora may yield further improvements. In addition,
RadQA reflects a limited range of radiology report styles and
clinical settings. The dataset is derived primarily from a single
institution and focuses on specific imaging modalities and
report formats, which may limit generalizability to other radi-
ology genres such as impression-only reports, operative notes,
or longer and more ambiguous report narratives. Evaluating
whether the proposed error taxonomy and observed failure
patterns transfer to such heterogeneous report types remains an
important direction for future work. Another limitation of this
study is the absence of human expert evaluation. While EM,
F1, and SM provide objective measures of extraction accuracy
and localization, they do not fully capture whether minor
boundary deviations alter clinical interpretation or diagnostic
meaning. Future work should incorporate radiologist review to
assess the clinical acceptability of near-miss predictions, par-
ticularly for superset and subset errors. Finally, our evaluation
centered on span extraction accuracy; future work should also
assess the clinical utility of model outputs, including whether
predicted spans support accurate downstream decision-making
by physicians. Additionally, while fine-tuning improved per-
formance for the non-reasoning extractive models evaluated
here, we note that emerging reasoning-oriented LLMs (often
accessible only through few-shot or zero-shot prompting) may
exhibit different trends. Evaluating such models was outside

VI. C ONCLUSION
This work presented a comprehensive benchmark of open
large language models for extractive question answering on
radiology reports using the RadQA dataset. We evaluated
LLaMA 3.1-8B, LLaMA 3.2-3B, and Mistral-Small-22B un-
der zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned settings, and intro-
duced a structured error taxonomy to characterize model
behavior. Fine-tuning yielded the largest improvements, with
LLaMA 3.1-8B achieving the strongest overall performance
at 61% EM, 77% F1, and 85% SM. The taxonomy analysis
showed that fine-tuning not only raised accuracy but also
reduced severe errors such as hallucination and abstention
mistakes, concentrating residual failures in near-miss boundary
categories. SM further complemented span-level metrics by
highlighting when models located the correct sentence despite
imperfect span boundaries.
While fine-tuned extractive LLMs achieve strong results,
limitations remain. Residual errors, particularly superset, sub-
set, and overlap cases, suggest the need for better boundary
alignment strategies. In addition, reasoning-oriented LLMs,
which are often accessible only through few-shot or zero-
shot prompting, may exhibit different performance trends.
Evaluating such models, along with extending this analysis
to other clinical QA datasets and instruction-tuned variants,
represents an important direction for future work.
Overall, our results demonstrate that fine-tuned open LLMs
can provide accurate and reliable support for radiology report
comprehension, moving closer to clinically useful QA systems
while also revealing concrete areas for further improvement.
