Abstract
Understaffed universities and limited funding for tutors risk stu-
dents’ disengagement due to missing assistance. HCI research in-
creasingly focuses on conversational agents for scalable educational
support. This paper presents a course-specific AI chatbot designed
to answer students’ questions and reduce tutor workload by address-
ing frequent organizational and content-related questions. Devel-
oped as a pilot case study for an introductory Software Technology
course, ST-Buddy combines modular LLM-based dialogue with a
flexible course-grounded knowledge integration to enable adaptive,
context-aware responses grounded in relevant course material and
logistical information. A formative evaluation of ST-Buddy (n=32)
showed good usability (SUS=77.81), perceived helpfulness particu-
larly when tutors were unavailable, and rated response quality as
relevant and understandable. Additionally, students’ self-reported
everyday academic AI use and lessons learned from the evaluation
settings are discussed. These findings outline implications for im-
proving future evaluations and highlight the potential of modular
chatbot frameworks to scale personalized support in large courses.

1 Introduction
Across higher education, many students require timely and individ-
ualized support to navigate academic and organizational demands,
particularly during the early stages of their studies and in large in-
troductory courses with heterogeneous cohorts [14, 30]. This need
is especially pronounced for specific student groups with diverse
backgrounds and expectations [5]. While study success is closely
linked to students’ ability to regulate their learning processes and
access relevant information when needed [ 23, 32], institutional
support structures often struggle to meet this demand at scale. As
a result, students may experience uncertainty, delayed feedback,
and fragmented access to information, which can negatively af-
fect engagement and persistence [14]. Peer learning, tutorials, and
office hours are widely regarded as effective, diversity-sensitive,
and inclusive forms of support [25]. However, they are inherently
resource-bound and frequently constrained by staffing shortages,
financial limitations, and increasing student numbers. These lim-
itations have motivated Human-Computer Interaction ( HCI) re-
searchers and educators to increasingly explore Conversational

Agents (CAs) as scalable tools for on-demand assistance in educa-
tional contexts [1, 30]. Recent research further highlights the poten-
tial of Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based tools to foster inclusion [19],
better support students in large courses [ 18], promote problem-
solving skills [27], improve formative feedback processes [31], or
enable conversational, adaptive, and personalized interactions [21].
However, deploying such systems in higher education introduces
new challenges related to interaction design, trust, pedagogical
alignment, and data privacy. Without careful user-centered design
and transparent implementation, AI-based support risks remaining
generic, opaque, or misaligned with students or course-specific
needs [5].
This paper addresses these challenges through the design and
evaluation of a course-specific Large Language Model (LLM)-based
chatbot, called ST-Buddy. ST-Buddy is explicitly not designed as an
intelligent tutor or coach, but as a course-specific support agent
complementing existing human assistance. More specifically, ST-
Buddy provides scalable learning and organizational support to com-
plement human assistance by offloading recurring questions, and
is employed in an introductory Software Technology (ST) course.
Addressing limited design and implementation reporting in prior
educational chatbot research [4], ST-Buddy’s technical architecture
is documented transparently and released as open source.1
This research provides contextual insights into students self-
reported everyday AI use and addresses the following research
question (RQ): How do students perceive and evaluate the course-
specific chatbot ST-Buddy as support for learning and organizational
questions with regard to usability, usefulness, and response quality?
To answer this RQ, the paper presents (1) the learner-centered de-
sign of ST-Buddy and its underlying system decisions (modular bot
routing, privacy-aware deployment, and course-aligned Retrieval
Augmented Generation (RAG)); (2) findings from a formative in-
class evaluation on usability and perceived usefulness; and (3) prac-
tical lessons learned for designing and evaluating course-specific
educational chatbots in authentic learning contexts.
2 Background and Related Work
2.1 Conversational Agents in Education
Educational chatbots have been studied for tutoring, practice, moti-
vation, and administrative support. Wollny et al. [30] synthesized
74 studies and distinguished three pedagogical roles: (i) learning-
oriented tutoring and content practice (e.g., language learning [3, 12]
and learn about laws [8]), (ii) assisting course-related and organi-
zational support [26], and (iii) mentoring systems supporting scaf-
folding and Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) [13]. While results are
generally positive, the review highlights persistent limitations, such
as weak integration into teaching practice and limited evidence
from realistic deployments [30]. Recent work further illustrates the
potential of LLM-based tutors: Wang et al. co-designed a clinical
reasoning tutor powered by Med-PaLM 2 2 and evaluated it with
medical learners (N=8), showing that interactive AI tutoring can
support practice and feedback when it emulates effective instructor
traits such as scaffolding and reflection [28].

2.2 Course-Specific Chatbots and Support for
Computer Science Education
Course-specific assistants are particularly relevant for introductory
computer science education, where learners need timely explana-
tions, structured guidance, and support when human tutors are
unavailable [16, 18]. A recent scoping review identified that most
CAs for novice programmers focus on primary learning support ,
and only a few extend to broader student needs [4]. The reviewed
systems mainly address (i) learning-oriented tutoring and practice
(e.g., concept explanations and quizzes [22] or scaffolding-based
field studies [15]), (ii) task-level programming support such as debug-
ging assistance or feedback in code review activities [10, 20], and
only rarely (iii) secondary support beyond instruction (e.g., holistic
academic/personal support [16] or scalable course-integrated AI
support in large cohorts [18]). Across studies, evaluations often rely
on brief pilot deployment and short-term user reports rather than
longitudinal study design with larger and more diverse samples,
and Barzanji et al. further highlight limited reporting of system
architectures and design decisions, hindering reuse and compari-
son [4].
Despite growing work on course-specific and RAG-based chat-
bots, many systems lack transparent architectural reporting, rely
on externally hosted LLM services, give limited attention to institu-
tional privacy requirements, and focus mainly on learning tasks. ST-
Buddy addresses these gaps through a scalable and privacy-aware
deployment using foundation models hosted within the university
infrastructure. The system is released as open source to support
reproducibility and reuse, and supports multiple course-related
knowledge domains (e.g., different academic contents and admin-
istrative information) through topic-aware routing and retrieval,
which has not yet been explicitly evaluated in the literature.
3 System Design
ST-Buddy is situated on a self-developed chatbot platform to easily
provide CAs tailored to individual contexts - in this case, the intro-
ductory Software Technology (ST) course. The platform hosts the
CA on a homogenized website developed through user-centered
processes and with attention to accessibility and data-privacy. User
data and chat messages are solely saved client-side in the browser’s
local storage, and LLM providers with no-log and no-training poli-
cies are selected. The backend is built using a combination of
FastAPI3 for routing, LangChain [ 7] for structuring LLM calls,
LiteLLM4 for managing available models and routingLLM requests
to providers, and LangFuse [24] to trace LLM generations and keep
a version history of prompts.
ST-Buddy provides students with access to both course content
and organizational information via natural language interaction
in German and English, serving both academic and administra-
tive needs. Course-specific materials are embedded and saved in
a pgvector5 vector database alongside the full texts and metadata.
Through a RAG process, these materials are retrieved based on the
user request. Course data is split into three different categories:
content consisting of reading material accompanying the lecture,

4 ST-Buddy Evaluation
4.1 Study Design
To examine students’ everyday use of AI and their perceptions of
ST-Buddy, a formative mixed-methods evaluation was conducted
with students from the target group in two settings: (i) An in-
class semi-controlled test during exercise sessions to capture initial
reactions and usability issues, and (ii) a four-week online field test,
where students used ST-Buddy independently for exam preparation
and exercise support. Both settings followed the same procedure,
consisting of a pre-survey, ST-Buddy testing phase, and a post-
survey.
Participants. The study involved students enrolled in the intro-
ductory ST course at Dresden University of Technology across
different study programs. Participation in the study was volun-
tary and independent of course requirements, grading, or academic
evaluation. The chatbot was provided as an optional support tool
and was not part of mandatory course activities or assessment. All
participants provided informed consent and signed a privacy policy
in line with data protection regulations. Survey data were collected
anonymously and were not accessible to course instructors at the
individual level.
In-Class Testing Procedure. The in-class evaluation was conducted
as a semi-controlled test of the implemented CA-based prototype.
After a short introduction, participants accessed the chatbot web-
site and completed a pre-survey hosted on a separate online survey
platform, capturing demographics and prior AI use, measuring the
standardized Affinity for Technology Interaction (ATI) scale [11].
Survey links contained an embedded anonymous ID that was auto-
matically pre-filled, enabling matching of responses across surveys.
After submitting the pre-survey, participants freely interacted with
ST-Buddy. To lower the entry barrier, course-related conversation-
starter questions were provided (e.g., "How can the State Pattern in
the BankAccount exercise be implemented?" and "I am sick. How
can I withdraw from the exam?"). Finally, participants completed a
post-survey including System Usability Scale (SUS) [6] to assess per-
ceived system usability and Partner Model Questionnaire (PMQ) [9]
to evaluate theCA as a conversation partner. Participants also rated
whether the chatbot would be useful when no tutor is available,
and evaluated response quality (relevance, correctness, clarity, com-
plexity, length, and linguistic naturalness) on 5-point Likert scales
from "not at all" to "completely". To complement quantitative mea-
sures with qualitative input, participants could optionally provide
open-ended feedback on what they liked and disliked about the
system.
Online Field Test Procedure. The online field test followed the
same survey design and instruments. Participants received the
chatbot link and project information via the course newsletter. To
support self-directed use, the pre-survey remained accessible during
the first two weeks of the test period, while the post-survey was
activated only at the end of the study and distributed via a follow-up
newsletter.
4.2 Results
4.2.1 Participation. A total of 103 participants started the pre-
survey, including 45 in the semi-controlled in-class evaluation and
60 in the online field test. In the in-class setting, 43 participants
completed the pre-survey; 38 started the post-survey, and 32 com-
pleted it. Of the 32 participants, about 20% (n=6) were female and
about 80% (n=26) male. The majority (65.6%; n=21) were between
18 and 20 years old, while the remaining 11 (34.4%) were between
21 and 29 years old. Most students (68.75%; n=22) were enrolled in
(Media) Computer Science, with the rest being split betweenBusiness
Information Systems, Engineering, and Mathematics. In the online
field test, which was sent to over 300 course members, 22 partic-
ipants completed the pre-survey, 12 started the post-survey, and
2 completed it, of which one contained invalid responses. Since
the two study setups were different and the online field test had a
low response rate, it was excluded from analysis, and the results
reported below are based on the 32 valid responses from the in-class
evaluation.

The open-ended responses on what students liked and disliked
about ST-Buddy were analyzed using thematic coding, resulting in
nine themes. The most frequent theme was relevance and course-
specificity (n=11), with students emphasizing that the chatbot was
grounded in lecture material. Further prominent themes concerned
positive answer quality and clarity (n=9) and answer accuracy and
completeness (n=9). Students also valued the efficiency and ease of
use (n=8) and practical examples (n=6) that ST-Buddy gave. On the
limitations side, participants highlighted technical issues (n=10),
such as duplicated or aborted responses, and issues with response
length and style (n=7), e.g. long and complex answers. Furthermore,
missing features and usability gaps (n=6), such as an “option to abort
answers” (P29), and lack of visual support (n=5) were identified,
explicitly requesting diagrams for programming contexts.
5 Discussion
5.1 How Students Self-report Current AI Use
Pre-survey results indicate that AI tools are embedded in students’
study routines, with most participants reporting frequent (often
daily) use, primarily for explanations, programming support, and
exam preparation, while summarization was less common. This
aligns with broader trends in higher education [15, 18], and reflects
the learning and assisting roles of educational chatbots described by
Wollny et al. [30], but also reflects ambivalence between efficiency
benefits and accuracy concerns [ 29], indicating the need for AI
literacy and transparent support tools.
5.2 Course-Specific Value and Design
Requirements
Given students’ frequent use of AI for academic tasks, the posi-
tive evaluation of ST-Buddy suggests that course-specific AI assis-
tance for internal learning and organizational needs, which general-
purpose chatbots typically do not cover, is valued, aligning with
prior research on scalable support in large courses [5, 18]. Given
the prototype status and short exposure time, usability was rated
positively (SUS=77.8), and 75% of students perceived ST-Buddy as
a useful alternative when tutors are unavailable. However, this
result may have been influenced by the cohort’s high technology
affinity (ATI=4.32) and suggests low interaction barriers rather
than long-term acceptance. Student feedback further highlighted
key HCI requirements for sustained use, including support beyond
text-only output and robust mobile usability, as mobile learning is
increasingly common [17].
5.3 Study Design Insights - Implications for
Empirical Research
While the online field test was intended to complement the in-
class study, its primary contribution lies in methodological insights
rather than quantitative results. The strong contrast between high
in-class participation and low online completion likely reflects con-
text, technical constraints, and study design. Open-ended feedback
indicated that technical issues occurred for 6/32 participants, and
online participation may have been further hindered by access
restrictions (university network/VPN). Conducting the field test
prior to and during the exam period and using a multi-stage survey
setup with lacking incentives may have additionally reduced en-
gagement and increased dropout. This underscores the importance
of timing and low-friction designs when evaluating educational
CAs, and motivates combining semi-controlled formative studies
with longitudinal field evaluations.
5.4 Limitations
As a first-iteration prototype,ST-Buddy occasionally produced tech-
nical issues (e.g., duplicated or incomplete responses), which may
have affected perceived reliability. Generalizability is limited due
to participants predominantly being male, German, and computer

science students. Moreover, the privacy-focused local-first design
led to mismatched pre-/post-survey data when participants used
multiple devices. Although the online study invitation reached over
300 students, only one participant completed both surveys; there-
fore, comparisons between online and in-class settings were not
feasible. Future studies should include a post-study step, asking
online participants about reasons for dropout to inform improve-
ments in study design and recruitment. Overall, the results rely
on the in-class sample (𝑛 = 32), limiting subgroup analyses. Due
to the short (30-minute) test phase and the absence of a control
group, conclusions about learning efficacy and causal effects are
restricted. A study setup testing ST-Buddy against a group using
a general-purpose AI chatbot would cast a light on the efficacy of
course-specific knowledge integration. Future work should involve
larger and more diverse samples, include control groups, and inte-
grate objective learning measures. While the evaluation focused
on perceived usability and usefulness, future work should also em-
pirically compare architectural variants (e.g., RAG vs. non-RAG) to
isolate design effects.
6 Conclusion
This paper presented ST-Buddy, a course-specific AI-based chatbot
designed to provide grounded learning and organizational support
in a large introductory Software Technology course, addressing
students’ recurring struggle to identify relevant and reliable course
information at the moment of need. The reusable system design
of ST-Buddy builds on modular LLM-based dialogue with contex-
tually relevant information injected using RAG and is available
as open-source software. A formative in-class evaluation with 32
participants showed good perceived usability (SUS=77.8), positive
usefulness when tutors are unavailable, and largely favorable rat-
ings of response relevance, clarity, and correctness. Beyond system
design and performance, insights into students’ everyday AI use
and expectations, and valuable lessons learned for in-class versus
field-test evaluations with student target groups are discussed.
Taken together, combining grounded AI architectures with HCI-
based evaluation and student feedback is critical for moving toward
robust, course-grounded educational support systems in real-world
teaching contexts.
