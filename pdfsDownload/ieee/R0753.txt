Abstract— While LLMs like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini
have proven effective at handling common, everyday tasks, they
often lack the domain-specific expertise necessary for more
specialized consultations. This limitation makes it essential to
integrate a targeted knowledge base when developing chatbots for
specific domains. In this study, we investigated the performance of
chatbots utilizing large language models (LLMs) within the
context of a mid-scale knowledge base for a regional R2 university
i n t h e U . S . U s i n g a w e b - c r a w l e d k n o w l e d g e b a s e , w e c r e a t e d
chatbots across multiple platforms, testing them against a set of
predefined questions to evaluate correctness and
comprehensiveness. Our findings indicate that while ChatGPT
models, particularly those augmented with both a knowledge base
and real-time search capabilities, provide the most balanced
responses on objective questions, hallucinations remain an issue.
In the subjective testing questions, no single chatbot emerged as
the best performer, with different models excelling in various areas
based on the personas and questions. The study suggests that while
LLMs are valuable tools in education, careful consideration of
their limitations is necessary for improving their application in
domain-specific consulting.

I. I NTRODUCTION
With the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI),
Large Language Models (LLMs), as a major breakthrough in the
field of AI, are gradually changing the way people acquire,
understand, and use information. These models, such as
ChatGPT [1], Claude [2], Gemini [3] etc., greatly enhance the
efficiency and convenience of users obtaining information
through the internet due to their powerful natural language
processing and knowledge generation capabilities. In the field
that requires domain knowledge and understanding of the
context/scenario, such as consultation, LLMs have demonstrated
“remarkable potential” [4]. In education settings, LLMs are also
“promising tools for open education” as they can provide
customized and interactive assistance to students and thereby
improve the independence and autonomy of the learners [1].
These types of “domain-specific” LLMs [5] can not only quickly
respond to the diverse needs of students, but also provide
personalized advice and information based on massive data,
greatly reducing the burden of traditional
consultation/supporting/feedback methods.
However, despite the enormous potential demonstrated by
LLMs, their reliability and accuracy issues in practical
applications are becoming increasingly prominent. Due to the
complexity, diversity, and potential bias of model training data,
LLMs may experience misunderstandings, erroneous reasoning,
and even fabricated information during the understanding and
inference process [6] (sometimes referred to as “hallucination”).
This uncertainty undoubtedly poses potential risks for users who
rely on model recommendations to make important decisions.
Therefore, an in-depth exploration of the accuracy and reliability
of LLMs in providing consulting information is of great
significance for promoting healthy and safe applications in
different domains.
This study aims to systematically evaluate the performance
of different major LLMs in answering a common set of questions.
Specifically, we utilized web crawling technology to extensively
collect information on University of North Carolina Wilmington
(UNCW), a regional R2 university on the east coast of the United
States as our knowledge base. The evaluation is done based on
both objective questions (about new student enrollment, such as
tuition fees, accommodation, and food) and subjective questions
(using given scenarios/personas). Subsequently, we input this
data into multiple commercialized large language modeling
platforms (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot) to create
Chatbots that can simulate answering questions in real-life
scenarios based on the given knowledge base.
By comparing and analyzing the answers provided by
different LLMs, this study aims to achieve the following
objectives: 1) demonstrate our framework of LLM evaluations
using a knowledge base and a set of questions, both objective
and subjective; 2) explore the impact of a reliable knowledge
base versus/with online search in building a domain-specific
chatbot, and analyze the role and limitations of them in
improving the quality of answers; 3) based on the research
results, strategies and suggestions for improving the application
of LLMs in the field of educational consulting are proposed,

II. R ELATED WORK
Chatbots are an important research method in the field of
natural language processing (NLP). In the early field of machine
translation which is a subdomain of NLP, BLEU [7] and
METEOR [8] were two common evaluation metrics. BLEU
evaluates the accuracy of the translated text by calculating the
similarity between the machine-generated translation and a set
of reference translations. METEOR improves the BLEU
algorithm by introducing an alignment algorithm to evaluate the
similarity between the generated translation and the reference
translation, which can better handle synonyms and word order
differences. However, these evaluation metrics are ineffective
for evaluating chatbots. In 2020, Mehri and Eskenazi proposed
the FED measurement metric [9], which can measure fine-
grained dialogue quality at both the dialogue turn (defined as “a
dialog context and a system response (from chatbot)” [9]) level
and the entire dialogue level. FED was reported to achieve a
moderate to strong correlation with human judgments at both
levels. However, with the advancement of chatbot technology,
especially after the introduction of large language models
(LLMs), people have put forward higher requirements for
chatbots. People's focus on dialogue has shifted from fluency to
multi-dimensional evaluations of chatbots' reading
comprehension, reasoning capability, math, and other technical
measures [10].
Researchers utilized specific datasets to evaluate the fluency,
naturalness, and other aspects of dialogue response, testing
whether the dialogue level of chatbots can reach human levels.
For example, Mendonça et. al introduced a new evaluation
benchmark dataset SODA-EVAL [11], it used more than 120000
turn-level assessments for training, covering 10000
conversations. Human validation and annotation tasks were also
conducted to confirm the quality of automatic annotation. A
rating system ranging from 1 to 5 was used to evaluate the
quality of dialogue responses. The evaluation focuses on the
effectiveness of dialogue annotations, including whether they
contradict the information in the dialogue history, whether the
model can cover all relevant information in the conversation, and
the fluency and naturalness of dialogue response, which includes
evaluating the common sense, participation, and repetition in the
conversation. This research found that although GPT-4 performs
well in many aspects, there is still room for improvement in
coherence and common-sense reasoning. This indicates that
although large language models have made significant progress
in generating fluent and relevant responses, they still face
challenges in simulating the complexity of human dialogue.
Researchers have reported successes in creating domain-
specific chatbots that can support domain experts in reading and
making decisions. The progress of domain-specific chatbots also
entices research in domain-specific chatbot evaluations. In the
work by Song et al., the authors developed a questionnaire of 21
questions and two clinical scenarios related to urolithiasis [4].
They tested four LLMs (Claude, Bard, ChatGPT4, and Bing).
Their domain experts then evaluated the model responses
regarding accuracy, comprehensiveness, legibility, human care,
and clinical case analysis ability based on a 5-point Likert scale.
They reported that Claude and GPT-4 were the top-performing
LLMs in their evaluation matrices. However, they only
evaluated urolithiasis-related dialogs, which is a relatively small
domain.
In the field of education, Hwang et. al [12] used an AI-driven
approach to create and assess multiple-choice questions in the
fields of chemistry and biology. They evaluated the quality of
the questions by using the Item Writing Flaw (IWF) criteria [13]
and combined machine learning models with human
assessments to verify the alignment of the questions with
Bloom's Taxonomy. The study utilized the RoBERTa model, a
machine learning model, to validate 120 generated questions. A
domain expert with over 28 years of experience in STEM
education assessed 57 of those questions. The research indicated
that GPT-3.5 can generate questions aligned with Bloom's
Taxonomy levels. However, there are differences in the quality
assessment of the questions between human and machine
evaluations. This suggests that there is still a discrepancy
between machine learning models and human assessments when
evaluating AI-generated content. Thus, a carefully considered
set of standards is necessary for evaluating the quality of AI-
generated materials.

IV. E VALUATION FRAMEWORK AND RESULTS
We developed a structured framework to assess chatbot
performance with two test sets. The first set, comprising 20
objective questions, was used in single-turn interactions (where
the user asks a single question and receives a single response
without any follow-up or context retention) to evaluate the
performances of the chatbots. The second set introduced three
fictional personas; for each, a chatbot will answer 13 subjective
questions through multi-turn dialogues (where multiple
exchanges occur between the user and the chatbot, allowing for
context retention and follow-up questions within a coherent
conversation).
A. Evaluation framework
We created two test sets, both covering various aspects of the
university. The first set of 20 objective questions focused on
factual aspects such as tuition/fees, housing, and campus safety.
The second set included three fictional personas and 13
subjective questions that were related to personal preferences,
such as “What food options align with my taste does the on-
campus dining offer?” and “Are there any on-campus housing
options that will fit my lifestyle and preferences?” To create
these personas, we developed profiles of three virtual incoming
college students, detailing their preferred majors, food
preferences, personal hobbies, and other individualized
characteristics.
When using the first set of questions to test the chatbot, we
conducted single-turn conversations to obtain and evaluate
responses directly. In contrast, for the second set, we employed
a multi-turn dialogue approach. First, we provided the fictional
persona’s background information to the chatbot to provide
context for the interaction. We then asked subjective questions
from the test set, expecting the chatbot to respond based on both
the provided knowledge base and the specific details of the
persona. This approach allowed us to assess the chatbot’s ability
to handle both straightforward questions and more complex,
context-driven conversations by integrating its knowledge with
personalized responses, offering a comprehensive view of its
adaptability and effectiveness.
When we evaluated the dialogue with the chatbots, we
focused on two aspects,correctness andcomprehensiveness. For
each response from the chatbots, we rate the correctness and
comprehensiveness using a 3-point Likert scale. The Likert
scales were defined as follows:
Correctness -
∂ 0 (Poor) - wrong information (hallucination) or refusal to
answer when the knowledge base has included this
information.
∂ 1 (Fair) - partially wrong/inaccurate or misleading
information provided or refuse to answer when the
knowledge base does not provide adequate information
to answer.
∂ 2 (Good) - accurate information, or the information is
very close to the information provided (considering some
numbers, like student population, will change every year).
Comprehensiveness -
∂ 0 (Poor) - not enough information is provided to cover all
the aspects of the question.
∂ 1 (Fair) - some information related to the question but
inadequate, or too much additional and unrelated
information.
∂ 2 (Good) - good coverage of all the aspects, no or limited
unrelated information provided.
The last two authors of this paper rated all the responses
written by our chatbots. Both of them have been teaching,
studying, or working at UNCW for at least three years.
B. Experimental results
Objective question:
We created three different ChatGPT-based chatbots:
ChatGPT4o without knowledge base ( GPT-4o), ChatGPT4o
with knowledgebase but no online search ( GPT-4o+KB), and
ChatGPT4o with knowledge base and search (GPT4o+KB+S).
GPT-4o is the baseline model in our test, and without the
knowledge base, it used its built-in knowledge and real-time
searches in our tests. We found thatGPT-4o was able to produce
reasonably accurate and comprehensive answers that are
comparable toGPT-4o+KB and better than Gemini and Copilot

the second highest, mainly due to unrelated information.

separate our knowledge base files and produce four chatbots on
Claude before they could provide answers to all test questions.
We found that our chatbot based on Gemini missed
important information that was actually provided in the
knowledge base. It may also not provide a comprehensive
answer from all the possible perspectives. For example, when
we tested it using a question about the tuition, it only provided
answers for in-state tuition but missed the out-of-state tuition. In
addition, Gemini did not take tabular data (such as Excel files or

the core course list for each major) could not be taken by Gemini,
leaving several “n/as” on the experiment results.
The chatbot based on Copilot Studio received the greatest
number of 0s, signifying the lack of capability to provide the
answer even if it was included in the knowledge base. Compared
to other chatbots, Copilot also provides shorter and less
comprehensive responses. Considering that Copilot Studio
allows searches on up to four hyperlink domains, we expected it
to perform better when using both the given knowledge base and
real-time searches, but it was the weakest model in terms of both
correctness and comprehensiveness. However, Copilot Studio
had the most extensive features, such as customizing
conversational flow for different topics. It still has the potential
to handle more complex tasks in the future.

Subjective questions:
We tested the performance of the chatbots on three fictional
personas. For the first one, UNCW, the university involved, was
not a good fit, considering the intended major, preferred location,
and city setting. The second one, by contrast, was clearly a good
fit for UNCW, considering the academic plan and personal
hobbies specified. The last fictional persona may find UNCW as
a “borderline fit” for his academic plan. We created those three
functional personas with the purpose of testing the chatbots in
answering subjective questions related to each one’s specific
needs and interests.
Overall, the ChatGPT-based models, GPT4o+KB and
GPT4o+KB+S, had good but sometimes unstable performances
on the test of three functional personas. The main source of the
0 ratings comes from hallucinations - they make up information
that the knowledge base did not include. For example,
GPT4o+KB+S suggested the third person come for the
“Bachelor of Science in Civil Engineering” degree, which was
not on the major list that was part of the knowledge base. We
found a similar model that relied on the knowledge base only,
GPT4o+KB, was less likely to hallucinate.
Claude was the most straightforward one in providing
information like “this university is not a good fit” to the first
person and the third person, making it the top performer for the
first persona. However, we also noticed that sometimes this
chatbot was not able to find personal suggestions due to the lack
of information (Claude did not support real-time searches
online), then, it may provide more general suggestions instead.
For example, when answering the question, “Is it easy to rent an
off-campus apartment near the school that is aligned to my
personal needs?” Claude did not find rich enough information
from our knowledge base; therefore, it only provided more
general answers about the off-campus housing availability and
cost.
Gemini may answer our testing questions while not
considering some of the specific person’s attributes. For
example, when calculating the student loans, the loan estimate
was provided based on in-state tuition, while the second person
was an out-of-state student. Another concern with
comprehensiveness is that it provided long responses to most of
the answers that present facts but are not necessarily related to
this person’s needs. However, our Gemini-based chatbot
performed better than GPT4o and Claude-based models on the
third fictional person. In addition, we were not able to use all our
testing questions because the tabular data could not be added to
Gemini, leaving part of the “n/as” in Tables III-V.
The chatbot based on Copilot Studio tended to provide
answers that were consistent with the knowledge base in the
subjective question testing; however, we found that the answers
may not be consistent with the needs of the fictional person. For
example, the first persona preferred housing options with “her
own apartment/suite with a private bedroom and bathroom.”
However, Copilot Studio still presented the option of “double-
occupancy, pod-style rooms,” which was clearly not the option
that this person preferred. We also found that the answers written
by Copilot Studio were not as comprehensive as other chatbots.
When we asked the chatbots to help the fictional persons with
estimating the student loan and the payments after graduation,
most of the chatbots helped the students with calculations and
provided estimated numbers, but Copilot Studio responded –
“You can estimate your total student loan payment by
multiplying the annual loan amount by the number of years and
adding any interest accrued.” While this answer is not mistaken,
this is not a helpful answer.

objective questions, which is consistent with Rydzewski’s
findings [15]. However, on the subjective questions based on
different fictional personas, we could not find a consistent model
with the highest performance - ChatGPT4o, Clause, and Gemini

Below are our qualitative findings on different commercialized
LLM models -
∂ ChatGPT 4o - providing the customized knowledge base
and utilizing the online search option tends to provide
better responses for objective questions, but hallucination
is still a concern. Keeping the knowledge base and
turning off the online search can reduce the
hallucinations.
∂ Claude - there is no option to utilize real-time searches.
The answers are more “faithful” to the knowledge base,
but the allowed knowledge base size is smaller than
ChatGPT, making it challenging to build a domain-
specific model on Claude if the knowledge base is larger
than the online textual data from an R2 university.
∂ Gemini - as a model that does not support real-time
searches, it is generally good at providing general
answers and considering personal needs. However, it
does not support structured input (such as Excel files) as
part of the knowledge base in the current version.
∂ Copilot Studio - is currently weak in both correctness and
comprehensiveness and is especially weak in answering
subjective questions that relate to the needs of a specific
persona. However, we see the ambition of the developer
- we are hopeful for stronger future versions.
Although this study aimed to create an assessment
framework and evaluate the current state-of-the-art LLM models,
the pace of innovation in the field will inevitably limit the
generalizability of our results and observations. However, our
assessment framework has a longer impact compared to our
findings. Our experiments were done between August and
October 2024, and the new OpenAI o1 was not in the scope of
this project.
In addition, there will be cases in which the knowledge base
cannot be online (either uploaded or shared) due to
institutional/cooperation regulations. In this case, the technical
paths discussed in this paper will not be applicable. For those
researchers/working professionals, the best option will be to
create an in-house LLM server.
