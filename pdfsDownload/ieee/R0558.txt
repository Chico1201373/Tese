Abstract —Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly 
applied in healthcare for clinical question answeri ng and 
information support, yet their deployment is constr ained by 
risks of hallucinated content and embedded bias tha t can 
compromise patient safety. This study proposes an i ntegrated 
mitigation framework that combines advanced prompt 
engineering with knowledge-grounded approaches, spe cifically 
Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) and the Model Context 
Protocol (MCP), without modifying underlying model 
parameters. Using a mixed-methods design, parallel RAG and 
MCP pipelines were implemented with shared retrieva l and 
prompting logic and evaluated on NIH-derived MedQuA D 
subsets (CancerQA and NIDDKQA). Structured promptin g 
strategies including role-based prompts, chain-of-t hought, and 
chain-of-verification were applied consistently acr oss both 
pipelines. Quantitative evaluation using accuracy, semantic 
similarity, evidence-backed rate, abstention, recal l, and 
demographic drift metrics shows that MCP consistent ly 
outperforms or matches conventional RAG, achieving higher F1 
and ROUGE-L scores, stronger evidence grounding, an d lower 
abstention while maintaining minimal demographic dr ift. 
Expert review further confirmed improved clinical r elevance 
and reduced unsupported claims. These findings demo nstrate 
that coupling structured prompting with knowledge g rounding 
produces more reliable, safer, and fairer healthcar e LLM 
outputs, offering a practical and scalable pathway for 
trustworthy AI deployment in high-stakes clinical settings.

I.  INTRODUCTION  
Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI), especially 
in Natural Language Processing (NLP), have enabled Large 
Language Models (LLMs) with billions of parameters to 
perform a wide range of tasks such as translation, 
summarization, content generation, and code creation [1], [2]. 
Prominent models like GPT-3, GPT-4 and Bard are trained on 
diverse corpora, including books, research articles, and online 
resources, allowing them to produce coherent, context-aware 
responses across many domains [3]. In healthcare, LLMs are 
increasingly used to analyze medical texts, generat e clinical 
reports, support research writing, and summarize le ngthy 
patient records, thereby assisting clinicians and researchers in 
decision support, documentation, and education [4], [5], [6]. 
LLMs are now being explored more broadly in high-stakes 
domains such as law and finance for drafting docume nts, 
triaging information, and supporting complex profes sional 
tasks, further increasing their impact and the need  for 
trustworthy behaviour [5], [7], [8]. 
Alongside these benefits, LLMs exhibit two critical failure 
modes: bias and hallucination. Bias arises from imb alances 
and stereotypes in training data that are learned and sometimes 
amplified in model outputs [9]. Hallucinations refe r to 
fabricated or incorrect content and can be factual,  logical, or 
contextual, such as contradictions of established m edical 
knowledge or answers that ignore prior dialogue [10], [11]. 
In healthcare, such errors can be severe. Biased da tasets 
risk worsening disparities; for example, models tra ined on 
predominantly fair-skinned populations may underdia gnose 
conditions in darker-skinned patients [7], [9]. Hal lucinated 
references, diagnoses, or treatment suggestions thr eaten 
patient safety and erode trust, as shown in studies where LLMs 
fabricated plausible but non-existent citations and  clinical 
details [12], [13]. These concerns make accuracy, f airness, 
and transparency non-negotiable requirements for he althcare 
LLM deployment [6]. 
Most current mitigation strategies rely on retraini ng or 
fine-tuning LLMs, which is computationally expensive, data-
intensive, and often infeasible for closed-weight models used 
in practice. These approaches also tend to address bias and 
hallucination separately rather than through an int egrated 
solution. 
Prompt engineering, designing and refining instructions to 
steer model behaviour offers a lightweight alternat ive that 
requires no changes to core model parameters and ca n be 
adapted to new tasks with minimal labelled data [14], [15]. In 
parallel, knowledge-grounding methods such as Retri eval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) and the Model Context 
Protocol (MCP) constrain generation to trusted exte rnal 
sources and have emerged as primary strategies for reducing 
hallucinations in LLMs. However, the combined use o f 
advanced prompting and these grounding methods rema ins 
underexplored, particularly in domain-specific, hig h-stakes 
healthcare applications. 
This study aims to address this gap by developing a nd 
evaluating an integrated approach that couples adva nced 
prompt engineering with RAG and MCP to mitigate bot h 
hallucinations and bias in healthcare LLM outputs. The 
objectives are to (i) investigate causes of bias an d 
hallucination in AI-generated healthcare responses; (ii) review 
and synthesize existing prompt-based and grounding 
techniques; (iii) design a unified framework combin ing 
prompt engineering with RAG and MCP; (iv) implement this 
framework on widely used text-based models such as 
ChatGPT; and (v) assess its effectiveness using NIH -derived

II.  BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK  
A.  Large Language Models in Healthcare 
Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-3, PaLM, 
and LLaMA scale to billions of parameters and achieve strong 
zero- and few-shot performance across diverse NLP tasks [6]. 
Domain-adapted variants like BioBERT and ClinicalBE RT, 
trained on biomedical corpora, outperform general models on 
clinical NLP tasks and question answering [6]. In healthcare, 
LLMs are used to summarize medical literature and p atient 
records, assist diagnosis and treatment planning, a utomate 
documentation, and support patient communication vi a 
question-answering and educational content [5], [6] . 
However, these benefits are accompanied by persiste nt 
concerns about factual errors, embedded bias, and p rivacy 
risks, motivating the need for domain-specific safeguards and 
rigorous validation before deployment in clinical w orkflows 
[5], [6]. 
B.  Biases and Hallucinations in Large Language Models 
LLMs inherit social and statistical biases from the ir 
training data, which can manifest as gender, racial , or 
demographic stereotypes and may be amplified in gen erated 
outputs [9]. Hallucinations refer to fabricated or incorrect 
content that appears fluent and plausible. [10] dis tinguish 
factual hallucinations (contradicting objective fac ts), logical 
hallucinations (violating internal reasoning), and contextual 
hallucinations (ignoring prior context), alongside domain-
specific phenomena such as object hallucination in vision-
language models [10], [11]. In practice, hallucinations include 
invented references, erroneous numerical claims, or  
unsupported clinical statements. For example, [12] show that 
ChatGPT can generate plausible but non-existent sch olarly 
citations, illustrating how factual hallucinations undermine 
evidence-based practice. Bias and hallucination are therefore 
intertwined reliability failures with direct conseq uences in 
high-stakes decision-making [9]. 
C.  Impacts of Hallucinations and Biases in Healthcare 
The consequences of biased and hallucinated outputs  are 
especially severe in medicine, where incorrect info rmation 
can directly affect patient safety and clinical out comes. 
Hallucinated clinical summaries or treatment 
recommendations based on incomplete or misinterpreted data 
can lead to harmful decisions, delayed diagnosis, o r 
inappropriate therapy [1], [16]. LLMs may also inadvertently 
expose memorized sensitive data such as names or locations, 
posing privacy and confidentiality risks [17]. 
 
Beyond direct harm, hallucinations can generate fictitious 
studies or misrepresent existing evidence in clinic al and 
research contexts, contributing to misinformation a nd 
eroding the integrity of scientific literature [9].  In medical 
education, misleading but confident explanations ca n distort 
trainees’ mental models and impair long-term clinic al 
reasoning [18]. Patient-facing chatbots that provid e 
inaccurate or myth-reinforcing advice further risk confusion 
and loss of trust in healthcare providers [18].

D.  Prompt Engineering and Knowledge Grounding 
Recent work explores prompt-level interventions to 
reduce hallucinations without retraining models. Gr ammar-
aware prompt engineering constrains syntax and logi c to 
reduce ambiguity and has been argued to “greatly di minish 
LLM hallucinations” conceptually [1]. Chain-of-thou ght 
prompting decomposes complex questions into interme diate 
reasoning steps, which can reduce factual errors, w hile 
iterative prompting and role-based instructions hel p refine 
responses through feedback loops [19]. Reinforcemen t 
learning combined with prompt design further tunes LLM 
behavior using user-feedback rewards, improving 
controllability but at the cost of additional compl exity [20], 
[21]. 
 
Complementary knowledge-grounding approaches 
anchor generation in external, trusted sources. Ret rieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) retrieves relevant docum ents 
and injects them into the prompt, improving factual accuracy 
and verifiability while introducing latency and dep endency 
on retrieval quality [21]. Knowledge-graph-based me thods 
such as RHO embed entities in subgraphs and re-rank  
responses, yielding substantial reductions in hallu cination 
rates on factual QA benchmarks [16]. Prompt-based M onte 
Carlo Tree Search explores multiple candidate reaso ning 
branches to select less hallucinated outputs but is  
computationally intensive [22].

E.  Remaining Gaps 
Despite rapid progress, existing studies typically address 
either bias or hallucination in isolation and rarel y provide 
unified, scalable frameworks that combine prompt 
engineering with knowledge-grounding for healthcare LLMs. 
Evidence on domain-specific prompt effectiveness, 
especially under realistic clinical QA workloads and datasets, 
remains limited, and few works jointly evaluate RAG  and 
emerging protocols such as the Model Context Protoc ol 
(MCP) in medical settings. Moreover, practical depl oyment 
guidelines, toolchains, and evaluation recipes tail ored for 
healthcare institutions are still scarce, motivatin g the 
integrated framework and empirical study presented in this 
work. 
III.  METHODOLOGY  
A.  Research Design 
This study employed a mixed-methods design to 
investigate how prompt engineering and knowledge 
grounding can mitigate hallucinations and bias in c linical 
question answering. The quantitative component 
implemented and compared two technical pipelines - a batch 
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system and a Model 
Context Protocol (MCP) application on medical QA ta sks. 
Both pipelines shared the same retrieval stack and prompting 
logic so that differences in performance could be attributed to 
invocation context rather than architecture. The qu alitative 
component incorporated medical-domain expertise to guide 
prompt construction, interpret common error modes, and 
contextualize metric differences. 
B.  Datasets and Preprocessing 
The evaluation used two MedQuAD clinical question–
answering subsets obtained from Kaggle: CancerQA an d 
Diabetes_and_Digestive_and_Kidney_DiseasesQA(NIDDK 
QA). In line with the original MedQuAD documentatio n, 
CancerQA content originates from the National Cance r 
Institute (cancer.gov), while the NIDDKQA subset is derived 
from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digesti ve and 
Kidney Diseases (niddk.nih.gov). Both datasets consist of real 
patient-oriented questions paired with verified ans wers from 
NIH patient-facing materials. 
CSV files were validated and lightly cleaned (e.g.,  
normalizing case and whitespace, fixing obvious for matting 
artefacts) while preserving the atomicity of QA pai rs. The 
cleaned QA pairs served both as gold references for  scoring 
model outputs and as part of the retrieval corpus o nce 
embedded.

C.  Prompting Conditions 
The study compared four prompting conditions applie d 
consistently across RAG and MCP: 
 
1.  Ungrounded baseline – the model answered directly 
from its internal knowledge, with a minimal system 
prompt, and no retrieval or citations. 
2.  Role-based prompt – the model was instructed to act  
as a careful healthcare explainer, answer concisely in 
2–6 sentences, and adhere to clinical tone and scope. 
3.  Chain-of-Thought (CoT) – the prompt encouraged 
step-by-step reasoning before summarizing the final  
answer. 
4.  Chain-of-Verification (CoVe) – the model first 
produced a candidate answer and then cross-checked 
key claims against retrieved evidence, revising or 
abstaining where necessary. 
 
For grounded modes (Role, CoT, CoVe), a shared 
citation-aware template instructed the model to use  only the 
provided context and to abstain with the fixed stri ng 
“Insufficient evidence in provided context.” whenev er 
evidence was missing or conflicting. Answers were r equired 
to end with Citations which were mapped to ordered context 
blocks in the retrieved evidence. 
 
In all experiments, generation was performed using the 
open-source 20B-parameter model GPT-OSS-20B served via 
Groq, with deterministic decoding (temperature = 0). 
D.  Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Pipeline 
The RAG pipeline grounded answers in authoritative NIH 
text through a four-stage architecture (Fig. 1): mu lti-query 
expansion, vector retrieval, cross-encoder re-ranki ng, and 
citation-aware generation.

original question and these variants were embedded

2.  Vector retrieval : Each query embedding was issued 
against the Chroma index, returning an initial pool of 
candidate passages. A simple per-source cap limited  
how many chunks could come from the same URL to 
maintain diversity in the retrieved context. 
3.  Cross-encoder re-ranking : A lightweight cross-
encoder then rescored the (question, passage) pairs  
for semantic relevance. The top-k passages (typically 
6–8) after re-ranking formed the final evidence set  
used for answer generation. 
4.  Citation-aware generation : The selected evidence 
was concatenated with the question and a strict 
grounded prompt. The LLM generated answers 
deterministically (temperature = 0), was required t o 
abstain when context was insufficient. 
E.  Model Context Protocol (MCP) Pipeline 
The MCP pipeline exposed the same RAG core through a 
standardized tool interface. An MCP server, impleme nted in 
Python using FastMCP, registered tools such as 
retrieve_context, answer_grounded, answer_ungrounde d, 
and batch_answer. These tools delegated directly to  the 
shared retrieval and prompting code used in the RAG  
pipeline, ensuring metric-compatible outputs. 
 
retrieve_context returned the top-k evidence blocks  and 
the assembled prompt; answer_grounded produced 
grounded, citation-aware answers using the selected  prompt 
technique (Role, CoT, CoVe) while preserving the st rict 
abstention rule; answer_ungrounded generated baseli ne 
answers without retrieval; and batch_answer execute d 
chunked, resumable runs over CSV datasets. Each too l 
returned structured JSON, enabling interactive insp ection of 
context blocks and citations in clients such as the  MCP 
Inspector. 
 
Crucially, MCP functioned as a thin orchestration l ayer 
rather than a new reasoning method: it read the sam e 
configuration, accessed the same Chroma index, and 
enforced identical grounding, abstention, and citation rules as 
the RAG batch pipeline. 
F.  Evaluation Procedure and Metrics 
Both pipelines were run on the curated development split 
of CancerQA and NIDDKQA. For each combination of 
dataset, pipeline (RAG or MCP), grounding mode (grounded 
vs ungrounded), and prompting technique, batch answ er 
generation produced CSV files containing the questi on, gold 
answer, model answer, citations, retrieved sources,  number 
of context chunks, latency, and a configuration has h for 
reproducibility. A single scoring script processed all runs to 
avoid metric drift across pipelines. 
 
Quantitative evaluation covered two dimensions: 
 
• Utility / answer quality: 
o Exact Match (EM) 
o Token-level F1 (overall and restricted to non-
abstained answers) 
o ROUGE-L (overall and non-abstained) 
o SBERT cosine similarity between model and 
gold answers (non-abstained) 
• Safety and evidence (hallucination/bias proxies): 
o Abstention rate – fraction of questions 
answered with the fixed abstention string 
o Evidence-Backed Rate (EBR) – fraction of non-
abstained answers with sufficient term-level 
support in cited context 
o Unsupported Answer Rate – complement of 
EBR within grounded runs 
o Recall@k – whether key gold-answer terms 
appeared in retrieved context 
o Demographic drift – fraction of answers 
introducing unsupported demographic 
attributes, used as a simple bias proxy 
 
Automated metrics were complemented by expert review 
of sampled outputs from each configuration. Domain experts 
assessed clinical correctness, completeness, approp riateness 
of tone, and the validity of cited evidence, helping to interpret 
subtle metric differences and identify residual hal lucination 
or bias patterns. 
IV.  RESULTS AND DISCUSSION  
A.  Overall Evaluation 
The two QA pipelines - RAG and MCP were evaluated 
on multiple dimensions: Exact Match, F1, ROUGE-L, a nd 
their non-abstained variants (F1_non_abstained, 
ROUGE_L_non_abstained), SBERT semantic similarity, 
Evidence-Backed Rate (EBR), abstention rate, recall@k, and 
demographic drift.  
 
Across both CancerQA and NIDDKQA, MCP 
consistently outperformed or matched RAG on the mai n 
answer-quality metrics while maintaining strong saf ety 
characteristics. Ungrounded baselines (no retrieval , minimal 
prompting) performed worst in all settings, confirm ing that 
grounding and prompt design are essential for reliable clinical 
QA.

Answer quality also varied by prompting technique, but 
MCP retained its advantage within each mode. The Ro le-

prompting gave MCP F1_non_abstained = 0.3587 versus  
0.3310 for RAG, showing a clear lead in the configu ration 
that best balances structure and conciseness.

RAG’s in each prompt mode. In CancerQA CoT, SBERT

that, even when exact token overlap is modest, MCP’ s

D.  Retrieval Effectiveness (Recall@k) 
Retrieval performance in grounded modes was near-
perfect for both pipelines, ensuring that answer-ge neration 
differences are not simply due to missing context. In 
NIDDKQA, all grounded configurations achieved recal l@k 
= 1.0. For CancerQA, most grounded modes reached re call 
values of 0.99 or higher; for example, CoVe recall was 
0.9873 for RAG and 1.0 for MCP. 
 
Ungrounded baselines, by design, had recall = 0 (no  
retrieval). The slightly higher recall for MCP in some settings 
(e.g., 1.0 vs 0.9873) suggests marginally more reli able 
retrieval, but in practice both pipelines had suffi cient access 
to relevant supporting documents.  
E.  Evidence Support and Hallucination Reduction 
MCP showed stronger evidence-backed behavior than 
RAG. In grounded CancerQA tasks, MCP’s EBR ranged 
from 0.71 to 0.794, while RAG’s ranged from 0.6456 to 
0.7975. MCP clearly outperformed RAG in CoVe (0.71 vs 
0.6456) and Role (0.71 vs 0.6835) prompts. In NIDDK QA, 
EBR values were high for both pipelines ( ≥0.8), but MCP 
again was slightly ahead in most configurations (e. g., Role 
0.92 vs 0.9155). 
F.  Bias Mitigation and Safety (Demographic Drift) 
Bias, measured via demographic drift, was low for b oth 
pipelines across all grounded configurations (drift  < 0.15). 
MCP typically achieved slightly lower drift, suggesting more 
consistent performance across demographic groups. F or 
CancerQA, MCP’s CoVe drift was 0.10 vs RAG’s 0.1392 , 
and under Role 0.08 vs 0.1266. In NIDDKQA, MCP drif t 
under CoVe was 0.047 vs 0.0704 for RAG, and under R ole 
0.0388 vs 0.0423. 
 
The main exceptions were CoT prompts, where MCP’s 
drift was marginally higher in some cases (e.g., 0. 14 vs 
0.1139 in CancerQA CoT), but even there the absolut e drift 
remained small. Overall, both pipelines demonstrate d 
minimal demographic drift, with MCP holding a small  but 
consistent edge.

V.  CONCLUSION & FUTURE WORK  
A.  Summary of Contributions 
This study set out to address a central challenge i n 
healthcare AI: how to reduce hallucinations and bias in Large 
Language Model (LLM) outputs without retraining or fine-
tuning base models. Building on prior work in promp t 
engineering and knowledge grounding, the research proposed 
and evaluated an integrated framework that combines  
advanced prompting strategies with Retrieval-Augmen ted 
Generation (RAG) and the Model Context Protocol (MC P) 
over authoritative NIH-derived clinical QA datasets. 
 
Using MedQuAD-based Cancer and NIDDK subsets, the 
study implemented parallel RAG and MCP pipelines th at 
shared the same retrieval and grounding stack, and 
systematically compared Role, Chain-of-Thought, and  
Chain-of-Verification prompting. Across configurati ons, 
MCP consistently achieved higher or comparable F1, 
ROUGE-L, and SBERT similarity, alongside stronger 
Evidence-Backed Rates, lower abstention in grounded  
modes, and minimal demographic drift. These results suggest 
that the proposed MCP-based orchestration with stru ctured 
prompting yields more reliable, better grounded, and slightly 
fairer clinical QA than a conventional batch RAG pi peline 
using the same knowledge base. 
B.  Limitations and Directions for Future Research 
The study has several limitations. It focuses on En glish, 
text-only QA tasks using two MedQuAD subsets and a single 
LLM (Groq - GPT-OSS-20B ) family and evaluates bias  via 
a lightweight demographic drift proxy rather than f ull-scale 
fairness audits. The experiments do not cover multi modal 
inputs, longitudinal reasoning, or complex multi-step clinical 
workflows. 
 
Future research could extend the framework to larger and 
more diverse medical corpora, multiple LLM architec tures,
