Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) chatbots are increasingly
deployed for academic advising, yet it remains unclear whether
these systems can provide reliable, hallucination-free guidance. We
investigate this question by evaluating a production course advising
chatbot using LLM-as-a-judge methodology with Gemini 3 Flash.
To enable systematic assessment, we develop AdvisingBench, a
benchmark comprising 55 expert-evaluated questions across three
complexity categories (common, specific and edge cases). We eval-
uate the system on four metrics: answer relevancy, faithfulness,
context relevancy and answer correctness. Our results reveal perfor-
mance variation across query categories, with edge cases showing
6% higher overall scores than common queries despite lower con-
text relevancy. We find that context relevancy is the metric most
sensitive to query complexity, while answer relevancy remains con-
sistently high (>0.95) across all categories. Notably, hallucination
failures were rare (3 of 53 cases), suggesting that RAG architectures
substantially constrain models to retrieved content in academic
advising contexts, though they do not eliminate fabrication risk.
This work provides initial empirical evidence on whether AI can
support course advising, and offers a methodology and benchmark
for future validation studies.

1 Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) integrated with Retrieval-Augmented
Generation (RAG) architectures are transforming academic sup-
port services in higher education [8]. These systems promise 24/7
availability, consistent responses, and reduced burden on human
advisors. However, deploying AI for academic advising carries sig-
nificant responsibility as incorrect course recommendations can
delay graduation, increase costs and cause students stress. A crit-
ical question therefore arises: can RAG-based chatbots provide
reliable, hallucination-free academic guidance? Despite growing
adoption, standardized evaluation frameworks for academic advis-
ing chatbots remain notably absent. A recent comprehensive survey
of RAG chatbots in education reported that there is currently no
widely accepted standard for evaluating such systems and high-
lighted this as a major weakness in the field [11]. Existing systems
report heterogeneous metrics that prevent cross-system compar-
ison: AdvisingWise achieved high-accuracy responses on 84.2%
(16/19) of evaluable advising queries in an expert-validated sample
of 20 queries [7], NileAdvisor reported 0.93 query accuracy using
knowledge graphs [1], while CourseQ focused more on usability
and user-perceived accuracy than on formally measuring factual
correctness of its recommendations [10]. This heterogeneity leaves
practitioners without guidance for selecting among competing ap-
proaches or understanding how system performance varies across
different types of student queries. The RAGAS framework [3] has
emerged as a de facto standard for RAG evaluation, offering metrics
for faithfulness, answer relevancy and context quality. However,
practical validation reveals concerning limitations: the TREC 2024
RAG Track evaluation found GPT-4o and human judgments match
perfectly only 56% of the time in manual assessments, rising to
72% with post-editing [ 13]. These gaps suggest that automated
evaluation, while useful for scale, requires careful validation. This
paper addresses these gaps by evaluating a production RAG-based
course advising chatbot to provide initial empirical characteriza-
tion of system behavior across query types. We develop Advising-
Bench, a 55-question benchmark with expert-evaluated reference
answers across three complexity categories, and apply LLM-as-a-
judge methodology to assess the system across four established
metrics. We contribute: (1) initial empirical characterization of how
a RAG-based academic advising system performs across query com-
plexity categories; (2) analysis of how evaluation metrics behave
differently across these categories; and (3) a reusable benchmark
and methodology that can be validated against human advisor as-
sessments in future work.

2 Related Work
To contextualize our evaluation approach, we review three relevant
areas: RAG evaluation frameworks, educational AI chatbots, and
LLM-as-a-judge methodology.
2.1 RAG Evaluation Frameworks
The RAGAS framework introduced reference-free metrics for RAG
systems, including faithfulness (factual consistency with retrieved
context), answer relevancy (appropriateness to query), and con-
text precision/recall [3]. While widely adopted, practitioners report
variable reliability. The TREC 2024 RAG Track evaluation found
GPT-4o and human judgments match only 56% perfectly in manual
assessments, rising to 72% with post-editing [ 13]. This suggests
automated evaluation, while useful for scale, requires careful vali-
dation. Recent work on the CRAG benchmark reveals that “even
state-of-the-art RAG solutions struggle with accuracy, especially
on questions with higher dynamism, lower popularity, or higher
complexity” [14].
2.2 Educational AI Chatbots
RAG chatbots for education have proliferated across domains. A sys-
tematic review identified common applications including tutoring,
FAQ systems, and administrative support [11]. However, the review
noted that many evaluation methods “lack comprehensiveness or
detailed specification. ” For course advising specifically, published
systems use inconsistent evaluation approaches. The AdvisingWise
system was evaluated using a mixed-methods approach (expert
evaluation, LLM-as-a-judge, and a user study with advisors) and
adopted a conservative strategy of declining to answer when uncer-
tain, which the authors argue helped avoid incorrect responses and
contributed to high observed accuracy [7]. The From Interests to
Insights system applied RAG to course descriptions using embed-
ding similarity, but focused on recommendation relevance rather
than factual accuracy [2]. An academic services chatbot at FPMIPA
UPI achieved 0.91 faithfulness on factual queries but context recall
dropped to 0.59 for reasoning tasks which shows a 35% perfor-
mance gap [6]. These varied approaches underscore the need for
systematic evaluation of whether AI can reliably support academic
advising.
2.3 LLM-as-a-Judge Methodology
The LLM-as-a-judge paradigm uses capable language models to
evaluate other model outputs [ 15]. The foundational MT-Bench
study established that GPT-4 achieves agreement rates comparable
to human-to-human agreement [ 15]. G-Eval extended this with
chain-of-thought prompting, achieving 0.514 Spearman correlation
on summarization tasks [ 9]. However, documented biases affect
reliability. Position bias with GPT-4 exhibits approximately 35%
inconsistency, while Claude-v1 shows approximately 76% as models
may flip decisions when answer order is swapped [15]. Verbosity
bias is more severe: Claude and GPT-3.5 preferred longer responses
over 90% of the time when verbose distractors were added [ 15].
The authors observe that GPT-4, as a judge, gives GPT-4 about
a 10% higher win rate than humans do, but they emphasize that
the evidence for true self-preference bias is inconclusive. A recent
ACM IUI study specifically found “limitations of the LLM-as-a-
Judge approach for evaluating LLM outputs in expert knowledge
tasks” [12]. Recent comprehensive benchmarking of 54 LLM judges
found that model selection significantly impacts evaluation qual-
ity [5]. The Judge’s Verdict study demonstrated that Gemini Flash
variants achieve Tier 1 human-like performance, with gemini-2.5-
flash-lite achieving a z-score of 0.17 on their human-likeness test,
indicating judgment patterns nearly indistinguishable from hu-
man evaluators. Best practices for LLM-as-a-judge include detailed
evaluation rubrics with explicit criterion anchors tied to discrete
scale levels, decomposing evaluation into separable per-metric judg-
ments, and using structured chain-of-thought reasoning to guide
scoring [4, 9]. These approaches can improve consistency and help
mitigate known judge biases such as length preference while im-
proving alignment with human judgments [4, 9]. We incorporate
these practices in our evaluation methodology.
3 Methodology
Building on the evaluation approaches reviewed above, we now de-
scribe our methodology for assessing whether a RAG-based course
advising chatbot can provide reliable guidance.

3.1 System Under Test
We evaluate a production RAG-based course advising chatbot de-
ployed at a university in North America. The system assists students
in Computer Science, Engineering, Mathematics, and Data Science
programs with course planning queries. The architecture integrates:
• Retrieval: Vector similarity search over course catalog doc-
uments using PostgreSQL with pgvector extension
• Generation: Gemini 2.5 Flash for response synthesis
• Knowledge Base: Official university course descriptions,
prerequisites, restrictions, and program requirements
The chatbot is instructed to answer strictly from retrieved context
and politely decline out-of-scope queries.

3.2 Benchmark Dataset
To systematically assess the chatbot across different query types, we
developed AdvisingBench, a dataset of 55 questions organized into
three complexity categories based on expected retrieval and reason-
ing difficulty: Common queries (n=29, 53%): Frequent questions
with straightforward answers directly stated in course descriptions.
Examples include course descriptions, credit values, prerequisites,
term offerings, and program information. These test baseline system
performance on typical student queries. Example: “What are the
prerequisites for CS 320?”Specific queries (n=19, 35%): Questions
requiring interpretation of course policies or evaluation of eligibility.
Examples include prerequisite chain analysis, credit exclusion sce-
narios, grade threshold evaluation, and co-requisite interpretation.
These test the system’s ability to reason about policy implications.
Example: “If I scored 65% in CS 111, can I take CS 121? What if
I scored 55%?” Edge cases (n=7, 13%): Adversarial, ambiguous,
or out-of-scope queries testing system boundaries. Examples in-
clude requests for system prompt exfiltration, questions outside
course advising scope, and ambiguous course references. These
test appropriate refusal and escalation behavior. Example: “What
are the prerequisites for 304?” Each question includes an expert-
evaluated reference answer validated against official university
academic calendar. Table 1 summarizes the category distribution
and subcategories.

3.3 Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate four metrics using an LLM-as-a-judge approach: An-
swer Relevancy: Does the answer appropriately address the ques-
tion (or appropriately refuse/redirect if out-of-scope)? Scored 0.0-
1.0. Faithfulness: Are all factual claims in the answer supported
by the retrieved context? Penalizes hallucinations. Scored 0.0-1.0.
Context Relevancy: Is the retrieved context relevant to the ques-
tion? Evaluates retrieval quality independent of generation. Scored
0.0-1.0. Answer Correctness: How factually correct is the answer
compared to the expert-evaluated reference answer? Score 1.0 if
all key facts match, 0.5 if partially correct, 0.0 if incorrect. This
reference-based metric follows standard QA evaluation practice [9].
Overall: Aggregate score computed by the judge model. The judge
prompt suggests using a weighted average prioritizing faithfulness,
answer correctness, and answer relevancy, but the specific weights
are determined dynamically by the judge rather than fixed a prior-
ity. This reflects the relative importance of avoiding hallucinations
and providing accurate information in academic advising contexts.
3.4 Judge Configuration
Following best practices for LLM-as-a-judge [9, 15], we configure
the judge model with:
• Model: Gemini 3 Flash Preview with high thinking level
• Temperature: 0 for deterministic evaluation
• Prompt Structure: Detailed rubric with scoring criteria,
followed by question, reference answer, system answer, and
retrieved context
• Output Format: Structured JSON with scores and brief
explanatory notes
We selected Gemini 3 Flash as the judge model based on recent
benchmarking showing Gemini Flash variants achieve Tier 1 human-
like judgment, with gemini-2.5-flash-lite exhibiting agreement pat-
terns nearly indistinguishable from human evaluators [5]. While
the benchmarking validated earlier Gemini Flash versions rather
than 3 Flash Preview specifically, we extrapolate that architectural
continuity within the Gemini Flash family preserves these eval-
uation characteristics. Temperature 0 ensures reproducible eval-
uation by eliminating sampling randomness, following standard
practice for automated assessment [9]. We enabled the model’s ex-
tended reasoning mode (high thinking level) to allow deliberation
before scoring, as chain-of-thought approaches have been shown
to improve correlation with human judgments [9]. The judge is in-
structed that out-of-scope queries should be appropriately refused,
and that answers must be grounded in retrieved context to score
well on faithfulness.
3.5 Experimental Procedure
For each test case, we: (1) submit the question to the course advising
API; (2) capture the system response and retrieved context; (3) pass
question, response, context, and reference answer to the judge;
and (4) record all scores, latencies, and judge notes. The evaluation
completed successfully on 53 of 55 test cases (96.4% completion
rate).
4 Results
We now present the evaluation results, organized to address our
research questions about metric variation across query categories
and patterns that inform system reliability. We begin with aggregate
performance, then examine category-level differences, and conclude
with failure mode analysis.
4.1 Overall Performance
Table 2 presents aggregate performance across all successful test
cases. The system achieves strong answer relevancy (0.979) and
respectable answer correctness (0.758), with faithfulness (0.764)
and context relevancy (0.702) showing more variation. These aggre-
gate scores suggest reasonable overall performance, but examining
results by query category reveals important variation.

4.3 Metric Correlation Analysis
To understand metric relationships, we examined score distribu-
tions across categories. Answer relevancy shows minimal variation
(range: 0.032), remaining consistently high regardless of query com-
plexity. This suggests it may be less discriminative for identifying
system weaknesses. Context relevancy shows the widest variation
by category (range: 0.096), with specific queries retrieving more
relevant context than both common and edge cases. This metric
appears most sensitive to query-retrieval alignment. Faithfulness
shows moderate variation (range: 0.093), with highest scores on
edge cases where the system correctly declines to answer based on
irrelevant retrieved context.
4.4 Failure Mode Analysis
Examining cases with overall scores below 0.6, we identified four
primary failure patterns: Retrieval misalignment (n=6): Ques-
tions retrieved tangentially related courses rather than the specific
course queried. Example: asking about CS 328 prerequisites re-
trieved generic CS course listings without the specific course. Other
instances included queries about CS 441, DATA 311, MATH 221, and
MATH 200 where the retrieved context lacked the specific course in-
formation needed to answer the question. Hallucination failures
(n=3): The model fabricated information not present in the retrieved
context, and the fabricated information was factually incorrect. Ex-
amples include: hallucinating a quote from CS 111’s description;
providing prerequisite requirements for CS 221 that weren’t in the
context; and claiming prerequisite information was present when
it wasn’t. Notably, these cases had low answer_correctness scores
(0.0-0.5), indicating the fabricated information was incorrect. This
5.7% hallucination rate (3 of 53 cases) includes all evaluated re-
sponses, both substantive answers and appropriate refusals, in the
denominator. Incomplete answers (n=1): Responses omitted key
information or provided partial answers when complete informa-
tion was available. Example: when asked “When is CS 304 offered?”,
the system provided year-level information (third year) instead of
the specific term offerings (Winter/Summer) that were requested,
even though the context did not contain term scheduling infor-
mation. Over-generalization (n=1): Responses provided generic
guidance rather than specific policy interpretation. Example: when
asked to “Tell me about CS”, the system provided an extensive
generic overview of Computer Science programs instead of ask-
ing for clarification about what specific aspect was needed. While
hallucination failures occurred, they were relatively rare (3 cases)
compared to retrieval issues. Most failures involved the model cor-
rectly using information from its training data that wasn’t in the
retrieved context (faithfulness issues) rather than fabricating incor-
rect information. This suggests the RAG architecture provides some
constraint, though not complete protection against hallucination.
5 Discussion
Our evaluation provides initial characterization of how a RAG-
based chatbot behaves across different types of academic advising
queries. We discuss the implications for understanding system be-
havior, metric properties, and practical deployment, while acknowl-
edging the limitations of automated evaluation.
5.1 How Reliable is AI Academic Advising?
Hallucination failures were rare but not absent: 3 of 53 test cases
(5.7%) involved the model fabricating information not present in the
retrieved context. In academic advising, where incorrect informa-
tion about prerequisites or requirements could delay graduation or
increase costs, even a low hallucination rate carries meaningful risk.
The RAG architecture, which instructs the model to answer strictly
from retrieved context, appears to provide substantial constraint
but does not eliminate fabrication entirely. However, reliability is
not solely about avoiding hallucinations. The failure mode analysis
reveals that retrieval misalignment was actually the most common
issue (6 cases), followed by hallucinations (3 cases). A student re-
ceiving an incomplete answer about credit exclusions could make
suboptimal course decisions even without the system hallucinating.
These findings indicate that while RAG reduces hallucination risk,
retrieval quality remains the primary reliability challenge.
5.2 Metric Behavior Across Complexity
Our results reveal that RAGAS-style metrics behave differently
across query complexity categories. Answer relevancy remains
a weak discriminator as the system produces relevant responses
nearly always (0.957-0.989). This ceiling effect limits its utility for
identifying improvement areas. Context relevancy proves most in-
formative for diagnosing retrieval quality. The 0.096 range across
categories suggests retrieval performance varies meaningfully with

query type. Practitioners should prioritize this metric when optimiz-
ing RAG pipelines. The inverse relationship between faithfulness
and context relevancy for edge cases merits attention. When re-
trieved context is irrelevant (0.643), the system can still achieve
high faithfulness (0.843) by appropriately declining to answer. This
demonstrates that faithfulness captures response grounding rather
than retrieval quality, which is an important distinction for eval-
uation design. However, this also means that a system which fre-
quently refuses to answer could achieve artificially high faithful-
ness scores while providing limited practical value. Hence, practi-
tioners should interpret faithfulness alongside answer correctness
and relevancy. Answer correctness, which compares against ex-
pert references, shows consistent discrimination across categories
(0.739-0.786). This reference-based metric may be most valuable for
assessing end-to-end system quality when ground truth is available.
5.3 LLM-as-a-Judge Validity
Several observations inform the validity of LLM-as-a-judge for
educational RAG evaluation. The judge successfully identified ap-
propriate refusal behavior on edge cases, awarding high relevancy
and faithfulness scores for responses that politely declined out-
of-scope queries. This suggests the rubric design adequately cap-
tures domain-appropriate behavior beyond simple factual matching.
The detailed notes provided by the judge (required in our prompt
design) offer interpretable explanations for scores. For example,
one edge case received the note: “The assistant correctly identi-
fied the out-of-scope question and refused to answer, though the
retrieved context was entirely irrelevant to the query. ” Such trans-
parency enables manual review and rubric refinement. However,
there are potential limitations. Using Gemini for both generation
(2.5 Flash) and evaluation (3 Flash) may introduce family-specific
or self-enhancement biases. Prior work on LLM -as-a-judge has
observed possible self-enhancement effects and other biases (e.g.,
position, verbosity), though these are generally smaller than the
overall agreement between strong judges like GPT-4 and human
raters [15]. Future work should validate scores against human aca-
demic advisor assessments.
5.4 Implications for Practice
Our findings suggest that practitioners building educational RAG
systems should prioritize retrieval quality above generation tun-
ing. Context relevancy showed the most variation across query
categories, indicating that the retrieval component represents the
primary bottleneck. Embedding model selection and chunk design
likely offer greater returns than prompt engineering for response
generation. The strong performance on edge cases also highlights
the value of designing systems for appropriate refusal. Our chatbot
maintained high faithfulness scores precisely because it declined to
answer when retrieved context was irrelevant. Clear scope defini-
tion in system prompts enables this behavior and prevents the sys-
tem from overreaching into areas where it lacks grounding. Finally,
our results underscore the importance of structured evaluation.
Aggregate metrics obscured meaningful category-level variation
that only became apparent when we examined common, specific,
and edge cases separately. Practitioners should invest in expert-
validated reference answers and organize test cases by expected
complexity to surface actionable insights about system weaknesses.
5.5 Limitations
Several limitations scope our findings: Sample size: With 55 ques-
tions (53 successful), statistical power for fine-grained analysis is
limited. Effect sizes and patterns should be interpreted cautiously.
Single system: We evaluated one RAG configuration at one institu-
tion. Results may not generalize to other architectures or academic
contexts. No human validation: We did not validate LLM-as-a-
judge scores against human academic advisor assessments. The
reported metrics reflect automated evaluation only. Judge model
selection: Using Gemini Flash for both generation and evaluation
may introduce systematic biases not present with other judge mod-
els. Temporal snapshot: Evaluation occurred at a single point in
time. System performance may vary with course catalog updates
or model changes.
6 Conclusion
We evaluated a production RAG-based course advising chatbot to
provide initial empirical characterization of system behavior across
query types. Using AdvisingBench, a 55-question benchmark across
three complexity categories, and LLM-as-a-judge methodology, we
observed that:
(1) Hallucination failures were rare (3 of 53 cases, 5.7%), suggest-
ing RAG architectures may meaningfully constrain models to
retrieved content, though they do not eliminate fabrication
risk entirely.
(2) RAGAS-style metrics vary meaningfully across query com-
plexity, with context relevancy most discriminative and an-
swer relevancy showing ceiling effects.
(3) Edge cases achieve high faithfulness through appropriate
refusal despite low retrieval relevancy, demonstrating the
system knows when not to answer.
(4) Retrieval misalignment was the most common failure mode
(6 cases), indicating that retrieval quality remains the pri-
mary reliability challenge.
These findings suggest that RAG-based chatbots substantially con-
strain hallucination in academic advising contexts, though the 5.7%
hallucination rate and 11.3% retrieval failure rate indicate that full
reliability remains elusive. Practitioners should focus on retrieval
quality and use structured evaluation across query categories to
identify weaknesses. Future work should: validate LLM-as-a-judge
scores against human academic advisor assessments to establish
metric reliability in this domain; expand the benchmark to addi-
tional institutions and programs; investigate failure mode taxonomy
across query categories; and develop educational-domain-specific
evaluation metrics capturing advisory appropriateness beyond fac-
tual correctness. AdvisingBench and evaluation code will be made
available upon publication.
