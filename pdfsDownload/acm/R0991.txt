Abstract
While Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as promising
methods for automated student question-answering, guaranteeing
consistent instructional effectiveness of the response remains a key
challenge. Therefore, there is a need for fine-grained analysis of
State-Of-The-Art (SOTA) LLM-powered educational assistants.
This work evaluates Edison: a Retrieval Augmented Generation
(RAG) pipeline based on GPT-4. We determine the pedagogical effec-
tiveness of Edison’s responses through expert Teaching Assistant
(TA) evaluation of the answers. After the TA edits and improves the
response, we analyze the original LLM response, the TA-assigned
ratings, and the TA’s edits to ascertain the essential characteristics
of a high-quality response. Some key insights of our evaluation are
as follows: (1) Edison can give relevant and factual answers in an
educational style for conceptual and assignment questions, (2) Most
TA edits are deletions made to improve the style of the response,
and finally (3) Our analysis indicates that Edison improves TAs’
efficiency by reducing the effort required to respond to student
questions.

1 Introduction
Interest in Computer Science (CS) has grown at a rapid rate [ 3],
driving automated question-answering with Large Language Mod-
els (LLMs) [1, 11] due to their demonstrated strong performance in
answering subject-specific questions [14] and generating code [5].
Despite the increased use of LLM-based assistants for fast and per-
sonalized responses to student questions, challenges remain in eval-
uating the instructional effectiveness of LLM-generated responses,
especially for course-specific questions.
When evaluating LLM tools [25, 34, 43] for coding assistance or
answering questions in CS courses, the key question is how to eval-
uate the instructional effectiveness of responses. Beyond metrics
like relevance and factuality [17, 25, 31], recent work highlights the
importance of interactivity, time-saved, and withholding of direct
solutions in automated responses [8, 18, 43] as key factors impact-
ing response quality in educational settings. Most works address
this by using statistical analysis, student and educator surveys, and
human evaluations [8, 18, 41].
Our work evaluates the pedagogical quality and efficiency of
LLM responses through expert TA feedback. To achieve this, we first
present our LLM assistant, Edison, a GPT-4-based method that lever-
ages Optical Character Recognition (OCR), retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG)[23, 35], and prompt engineering (including expert
pre-conditioning and few-shot examples) to answer course-specific
natural language and coding questions consisting of text and/or
images. We deploy Edison with TAs in the loop in a large-scale
upper-division undergraduate data science course, Data 100, at UC
Berkeley.
Our evaluation uses expert TA feedback to assess the relevance,
factuality, and style of Edison responses. We analyze TA edits to
Edison responses to answer the following research questions:
RQ1: How do TAs evaluate Edison’s responses on factuality, rele-
vance, and style?
RQ2: What are the main attributes of TAs’ edits to Edison’s re-
sponses?
RQ3: What are the determining factors of Edison’s responses that
lead TAs to completely rewrite the answers from scratch?
RQ4: Are there efficiency gains for TAs from the use of Edison?

Our analysis shows that Edison gives relevant and factual re-

and weaknesses of an LLM-based educational assistant can guide
future work in the development and evaluation of these systems.
2 Related Work
2.1 LLM-powered Virtual Assistants in
Education
Logistical inefficiencies and repeated questions are major challenges
of scaling office hours for large classes [27]. A study on CS instruc-
tor pain points found that much time is spent assisting students
with individual or group queries [29], and Oberman notes a link
between student motivation and help-seeking in CS courses [ 6],
highlighting the value of digital tools for reducing help-seeking bar-
riers [2, 4]. To address these challenges, research has focused on the
adoption of LLMs such as GPT-4 [1], LLaMA [10], and Gemini [11]
as educational assistants [12, 18, 25, 31, 42, 43].
One LLM application is answering student questions [ 15, 31],
including use in discussion forums and virtual office hours. Other
educational applications of LLMs include code generation [36, 38,
40], student submission feedback [17, 21, 28], student modeling [9,
26], and course content development [33, 37, 39].
2.2 Pedagogical Evaluation of LLMs
The continual improvement and adaptation of intelligent tutor sys-
tems via data-driven approaches also guides LLM educational as-
sistant development [19, 32]. While many benchmarks exist for
general LLM tasks like question-answering [ 14] and conversa-
tion [7], educational assistants require higher standards for evalu-
ating factuality, relevance, and response style. Automated metrics
like ROGUE [24], and similarity-based embedding methods [ 35]
provide quick insight into the similarity of LLM-generated and ex-
pert responses but lack depth in assessing factuality or pedagogical
style and often overfit to the structure and template of the text
rather than the content. As such, these methods struggle to eval-
uate syntactically similar but semantically or structurally diverse
answers to questions [16].
The best existing method for pedagogical evaluation is to as-
sess LLM assistants on real student questions from courses [ 18,
31, 41, 43], leveraging human evaluation [ 13, 18, 25, 30, 31], sur-
veys [18, 25], and interviews [8, 13, 18] from students and educators
regarding LLM assistant interactions. These methods allow for fine-
grained analysis of instructional effectiveness.
3 Method
3.1 Deployment Settings
Our team deployed Edison in a large-scale upper-division under-
graduate data science course, Data 1001, at the University of Califor-
nia, Berkeley, during the Spring 2024 semester. The course served
1133 undergraduate and 71 graduate students and was operated

https://ds100.org/sp24/
by 59 staff members. The course was chosen in part due to the
diversity of topics discussed (data processing, machine learning,
probabilistic modeling, etc.) and the different types of questions
that arise as a consequence (logistics, mathematics, programming,
conceptual, etc.)
Edison was integrated into the course discussion forum — Ed-
STEM2 — for one month 3. During this timeframe, for each new
question, students were asked to provide consent if they permitted
the use of Edison. Upon consent, a TA would determine whether
the question was appropriate to send to Edison; factors for this
determination included the presence of personally identifiable in-
formation and extension requests. If determined to be appropriate,
the TA triggered Edison, which returned a TA-visible response to
the student question. The TA was subsequently prompted to rate
the generated response on a scale from 1 to 5 for the following three
metrics — factuality, relevance, and style (see metric definitions in
Table 1). We measure the pedagogical quality of a response as a
combination of these three metrics. The TA then decided whether
to directly use, edit, or completely rewrite Edison’s response.
Students provided their consent for 199 out of 435 questions for
which we have asked for consent. TAs activated Edison for 110
questions (after removing miscellaneous activations). There were
15 unique users (i.e., TAs), with the majority of the calls (~65%)
made by three experienced TAs; all the TAs have previously taken
the course (Data 100) and were either enrolled in or had completed
a pedagogy course at that time.
3.2 Edison’s Pipeline
Edison utilizes historical Q&A pairs and course documents to an-
swer new student questions with RAG. The system is also aug-
mented with a few additional features (the details of the base
pipeline are presented in [31]). Images attached as part of the con-
text are parsed using OCR and appended to each question. Based on
student-tagged metadata, incoming questions about assignments
are also augmented with the solutions from the corresponding
assignment. Syllabus text and course notes were used to create
logistics and conceptual vector databases, respectively. As a prepro-
cessing step, an incoming question is first passed to GPT-4 to deter-
mine its category - logistics, assignment, or conceptual. Within each
of these categories, only appropriate vector databases were queried,
and different category-based prompts, curated with in-category
hand-engineered few-shot examples, were used to generate an an-
swer. Finally, answers generated for assignment questions were
passed to an additional GPT-4 call to make the answer concise and
ensure it does not contain solution-revealing hints. Based on quali-
tative observations, the additional features - OCR, category-based
conditional prompting, few-shot examples, and additional LLM
solution filtering - improved the performance of the base pipeline
[31]; a thorough evaluation of the adapted system is left for future
work.

3.3 Data Processing
There were a total of 110 calls made to Edison; 65 were for as-
signment, 41 for conceptual, and 4 for logistical questions. Due to
the limited sample size, we did not use the responses to logistical
questions in this paper. The initial Edison response, TA ratings (see
subsection 3.1), and the final answers after TA edits were recorded.
We categorize TA edits based on their intent as well as based on
their mechanistic implementation.
First, sentence-level edits between the initial Edison response
and the edited TA answer were extracted4. The three co-authors
of this study, two of which have extensive experience as TAs for
large-scale CS or data science courses, reviewed a sample of Edison
responses alongside corresponding TA edits and tagged the intent
of each edit. Afterward, the annotators discussed and arrived at
the final four categories — hints and solutions, style, content, and
relevance. Descriptions and representative examples of each intent
category are shown in Table 2. All three annotators labeled a subset
of 10 edited responses with a total of 35 edits; the initial inter-rater
reliability rate was 71%. After discussing and resolving inconsisten-
cies, the final agreement rate was 85%. Then, the rest of the data
was divided equally among the researchers and labeled according
to the discussed criteria.
We also consider the mechanistic implementation of the edits
(different from the intent categories described above) — additions,
deletions, and modifications. Mechanistic categories are defined on
sentence level (e.g., modification is when a sentence is changed,
and addition is when a new sentence is added). Furthermore, the
number of edited units on a sentence/paragraph level was counted
(e.g., deleting multiple sequential sentences was considered one
deleted unit).
4We used difflib python library to automatically extract sentence-level differ-
ences between two strings.
In addition to sentence-level analysis, we looked at edits on the
word level as well. For modification type edits, word-level Lev-
enshtein edit distance (referred to hereafter as edit distance) was
calculated [22]. For deletions and additions, we used the total num-
ber of words deleted or added, respectively.
We also analyze the difference in the effort it takes for TAs
to answer student questions with and without using Edison; with
Edison, they start with the initial response and either use it verbatim,
modify it, or rewrite it. To quantify TA effort without using Edison,
we use TA answers to questions where Edison was not triggered.
We classify responses into three categories: Edison-only, where the
TA uses the response without modification; TA+Edison, where the
TA edits or rewrites the response; and TA-only, where Edison is not
triggered. We filtered assignment-related and conceptual questions
posted during the deployment timeframe to minimize the potential
biases between the two datasets. The number of words in the TA-
only answer was used to approximate their effort, factored as an
addition-type edit. To calculate the TA effort it takes to produce
TA+Edison answers, we use three proxies:
• Naive cost: We weigh additions, deletions, and modifica-
tions equally, and the total effort is the summation of the
corresponding edit distances on a word level: a+m+d, where
a, m, and d are the edit distances of additions, modifications,
and deletions, respectively.
• Adjusted weights cost: Removing existing text takes less
cognitive strain and time than writing new text [ 20]. As
deletions take less effort, we weigh deletions as half the
effort of additions and modifications: a+m+d/2.
• Modified units cost: We also define cost in terms of modi-
fication units. In this case, the total effort is calculated as the
edit distance of additions and modifications plus the number
of deleted units (e.g., sentences, paragraphs):a+m+units deleted .
The rationale for this metric is that TAs often remove a sub-
stantial portion of the response to make it less verbose, and

4.2 RQ2: Characteristics of TA Edits
4.2.1 Length Analysis. Figure 2 shows the lengths of TA-only,
Edison-only, and TA+Edison responses. The final response after
the TA edits is shorter on average compared to the initial response
generated by Edison. Furthermore, on average, TAs shorten an-
swers to assignment questions more than conceptual questions,
as seen by the gap between the solid and the dashed lines in the
left (red) and right (blue) panels. The TA-only responses tend to
be shorter than the TA+Edison responses for both assignment and
conceptual questions (gap between solid black and colored curves).
When we look at examples, TA+Edison responses often include
supporting examples with step-by-step explanations, which makes
them longer. We will investigate why TA responses are shorter and
the implications of this in future work.

4.3 RQ3: TA Rewrite Analysis
To analyze TAs’ decisions to use or rewrite Edison’s responses,
we report the pairwise correlations between the three evaluation
metrics and the rewrite indicator variable (1 if it was a rewrite, 0
otherwise) in Figure 3. The coefficients are -0.06, -0.5, and -0.004 for
factuality, relevance, and style metrics, respectively. Only the met-
ric corresponding to relevance is statistically significant, indicating
that relevance is most correlated to the TA’s decision to completely
rewrite the answer. While most edits focus on improving the re-
sponse’s style, style is not strongly correlated with the decision to
rewrite. This finding highlights opportunities for tuning LLMs to
better align with the stylistic needs of educational settings.

4.4 RQ4: Efficiency Gain Analysis
To analyze the comparative effort it takes TAs to answer questions
with and without the use of Edison, we use multiple methods for
calculating effort (see subsection 3.3); the results are shown in
Table 4.
• Naive cost (a+m+d): The effort to respond without Edison
(TA-only) is lower for assignment questions (𝑝 < 0.05), but
not statistically significant for conceptual questions.
• Adjusted weights cost (a+m+d/2): The effort to respond
with Edison is lower for conceptual questions (𝑝 < 0.05), but
not statistically significant for assignment questions.
• Modified units cost (a+m+units deleted ): The estimated
effort is 48.47 for the TA and 21.66 + 1.37 units deleted for
TA+Edison.
It should be mentioned that we do not account for the cognitive
effort required by TAs to mentally process the question and formu-
late a response. Edison can potentially reduce this cognitive load
by providing accurate responses for quick edits and verification.
5 Limitations & Threats to Validity
One limitation of our work is evaluating our method in just one
course. The nuances of Edison’s performance on other courses
should be further investigated. Furthermore, while evaluation by
expert TAs offers high-quality insights, it also limits the scope of
our analysis to a small number of samples. If we were to use an
LLM-based judge (appropriately trained) we could potentially scale
this evaluation further. Beyond considerations of scope and scale,
several external factors may influence the validity of our results.
One such factor is the extent to which students independently use
LLMs6. Another factor is the fluctuation in the volume of student
forum posts over time, which could impact the consistency and
representativeness of the data collected during the study. Lastly, the
efficiency analysis does not account for the cognitive load needed
to write an answer when not using Edison or reading through and
editing the initial answer when using Edison.
6 Conclusion
This paper analyzes how expert TAs evaluate responses from an
LLM educational assistant. We further explore how TA edits to LLM-
generated responses provide insights for enhancing their quality

to better align with teaching goals. Our work features several key
insights: (1) Edison, while highly factual, can be improved to provide
more relevant responses to assignment questions and less verbose
responses overall; (2) On average, Edison generates longer answers
than TAs. While this is preferable for conceptual questions, shorter
answers are better for assignment questions; (3) We find that most
edits by TAs are made to improve response style; (4) Only 16% of
responses were rewritten, and the relevance of the response is the
most strongly correlated factor to the TA’s decision to rewrite the
response; and (5) Edison answers save TAs time when answering
content questions.
These findings offer valuable insights into the differences in
instructional effectiveness between LLM and high-quality TA re-
sponses, informing the efficacy and future improvements of educa-
tional LLM assistants in real-world deployment.
