Abstract— This study introduces Sanity-LLM, an 
advanced Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) 
framework for mental health chatbots that aims to 
produce responses that are accurate, empathetic, and 
safe. The proposed architecture integrates hybrid 
semantic–keyword search, step-back questioning, and a 
reranking mechanism to improve the relevance and 
grounding of generated responses. The knowledge base 
was built from publicly available mental health dialogues 
and Q&A data, embedded and indexed using FAISS. 
Evaluation using RAGAS metrics demonstrates notable 
performance improvements, achieving 0.9391 context 
precision, 0.7077 context recall, 0.8754 faithfulness, and 
0.6297 answer correctness, surpassing naïve RAG 
baselines. Ablation results further confirm that each 
module: hybrid retrieval, step-back prompting, and 
reranking contributes significantly to the overall system 
effectiveness. While not a substitute for professional care, 
Sanity-LLM offers a scalable and accessible first-line 
support tool for encouraging help-seeking behavior. 
Future work will focus on broadening verified knowledge 
sources and validating effectiveness through expert and 
user-based evaluation. 
Keywords—Advanced RAG, Mental Health Chatbot, Hybrid 
Search, FAISS, Large Language Model 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Mental health issues have become a growing global 
concern, affecting individuals across diverse demographics 
and socioeconomic backgrounds. According to the World 
Health Organization (WHO), mental health disorders 
contribute substantially to the global burden of disease, with 
affected individuals having a life expectancy that is 10 to 20 
years shorter than the general population [1]. Despite this 
alarming reality, many people still lack access to timely, 
affordable, and effective mental health support services.   
Although awareness of mental health has increased in 
recent years, societal stereotypes and stigma continue to 
influence how it is perceived. Many individuals refrain from 
seeking professional help due to fears of being labeled as 
“weak” or “unstable” [2], while others may suppress their 
struggles in cultures that value emotional restraint and 
resilience. Such misconceptions hinder access to proper 
mental health support, often resulting in misdiagnosis or 
inadequate treatment. To help address this challenge, this 
research explores the use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) to 
provide more accessible mental health consultation [2].   
The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has 
transformed natural language processing, enabling more 
advanced human-computer interaction and deeper semantic 
understanding of text [3]. However, applying general-purpose 
LLMs in mental health contexts poses significant challenges. 
These models often lack domain-specific knowledge essential 
for accurately understanding and responding to mental health 
concerns. Without enrichment from reliable, expert-curated 
datasets, LLMs risk producing responses that may be 
inaccurate, insensitive, or even potentially harmful.  
Previous studies have investigated the application of 
machine learning techniques for mental health detection. For 
instance, a systematic review [4] examined various machine 
learning techniques employed in Online Social Networks 
(OSNs) to identify mental health conditions, highlighting both 
the potential and the limitations of these approaches. 
However, the integration of domain-specific enhancements 
into LLMs remains underexplored, creating a gap in their 
effectiveness and applicability for therapeutic or supportive 
interventions.  
Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a 
promising solution to the limitations of traditional chatbots. 
By integrating external knowledge sources with LLMs, RAG 
enables the generation of responses grounded in up-to-date 
medical information and tailored with greater empathy. This 
approach also mitigates common issues associated with 
LLMs, such as hallucinations and outdated knowledge. 
Furthermore, RAG facilitates automation in the healthcare 
domain, where chatbots can assist professionals by addressing 
general patient inquiries. As a result, it has the potential to 
reduce patient waiting times and enhance the overall 
efficiency of healthcare service delivery. 
Previous studies have explored the use of RAG in various 
contexts related to mental health support. One common 
approach involves integrating RAG with question–answer 
(Q&A) datasets to enhance the ability of large language 
models (LLMs) to generate more empathetic and contextually 
appropriate responses, an aspect that is highly important in 
mental healthcare. Another line of research focuses on 
leveraging RAG to incorporate the latest findings and 
knowledge related to mental health treatment and 
interventions. Since LLMs often lack access to up-to-date or 
domain-specific information, the use of RAG becomes

This research focuses on enhancing LLMs with domain-

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
In recent years, LLMs have been increasingly applied to 
solve real-world problems across diverse domains. Common 
approaches include integrating LLMs with RAG frameworks 
to enhance factual accuracy, fine-tuning models for domain 
specialization, and combining them with traditional deep 
learning techniques for better performance. These strategies 
demonstrate the versatility of RAG–LLM systems in various 
contexts.  
RAG has been explored in multiple domains such as 
agriculture, education, and business, highlighting its broad 
applicability. In agriculture, RAG has been combined with 
hybrid deep learning models to identify medicinal plants for 
disease treatment [5], while other works developed RAG-
based virtual assistants to support farmers in improving 
productivity [6,7]. In education, RAG has been utilized for 
quiz generation and virtual tutoring [8-10], demonstrating 
improvements in engagement and personalized learning. 
Similarly, in business and finance, RAG-assisted systems 
have been used for data aggregation, decision support, and 
financial analysis [11-13] 
Focusing specifically on mental health applications, the 
architectures of RAG can generally be categorized into two 
types: Naive RAG and advanced RAG. 
A. Naive RAG 
Research on SentimentCareBot [14] combines RAG with 
sentiment analysis techniques to enhance conversational 
quality and chatbot responses. The retrieval process is based 
on a publicly available mental health counseling dataset. The 
integration of RAG and sentiment analysis demonstrated 
superior performance compared to baseline RAG models. 
However, this improvement comes with a trade-off in 
computational efficiency due to the additional sentiment re-
ranking process. Another study utilized quantized LLMs 
combined with RAG [15] to analyze user documents before 
generating responses or diagnostic suggestions. Quantized 
LLMs offer advantages in terms of computational efficiency, 
allowing them to operate on limited hardware resources. 
However, their accuracy may decrease because of the lack of 
fine-tuning after model size reduction. Moreover, such a 
chatbot system also raises privacy concerns, particularly in 
the handling of sensitive user data, as there remains a 
potential risk of data misuse during model improvement. 
Considering the trade-off between computational cost and 
accuracy, another study develops Medbot [16], which focuses 
on utilizing RAG without combining additional methods. The 
system retrieves data from PubMed sources and uses it to 
provide answers to users’ medical inquiries, including 
potential diagnostic suggestions. However, the study 
provides limited details on chatbot responses from a 
professional evaluation perspective. Allowing the chatbot to 
generate diagnostic outputs directly raises concerns about the 
possibility of false diagnoses, especially given the inherent 
limitations of LLM accuracy. A similar approach was 
adopted in research on LLMs assisting sexual harassment 
victims [17], where the Llama-2 model was used to retrieve 
legal resources. With that method, their LLM managed to 
achieve an accuracy of 95%, demonstrating its ability to 
deliver sensitive and supportive assistance in trauma-related 
contexts. Marco et al. [18], in this recent research, proposed 
the RAG-LLM model based on ICD-11, a new classification 
system for physical and mental disorders. They introduced 
LLMind chat that utilizes Gemma 2 LLM. By leveraging the 
improved classification system, the model aims to better 
assist mental health professionals. The performance of their 
LLM was assessed by the DSM-5-TR Clinical cases manual 
to ensure that the responses from the chatbot were both 
credible and user-friendly. 
B. Advanced RAG 
Recently, more advanced research has been proposed. 
LLMs integrated with RAG are no longer limited to simple 
question-answering tasks but are now being utilized for a 
variety of purposes. In 2024, Qiming et al. [19] proposed an 
application called Soullmate, designed to address gaps in 
existing mental health applications. By combining LLMs, 
prompt engineering, and RAG, they developed a system 
capable of multiple functionalities, including question-
answering, mental health assessment, and risk detection. 
Their model was evaluated using the DAIC-WOZ dataset, 
Reddit suicide posts, and other case studies. With GPT-4 + 
SMMR, they demonstrated an improvement in performance 
across these tasks. Another recent study by Ahsan et al. [20] 
introduced online reinforcement learning to enhance RAG 
utilization. Based on similarity scores, their approach 
dynamically improved model performance in real time. The 
reinforcement learning component determines whether to 
retrieve additional data or refine existing responses based on 
contextual similarity. Their dataset consisted of mental health 
issues collected from college student surveys. Through 
evaluations with GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini 1.5, and 
GPT-3.5, the proposed OnRL-RAG model outperformed all 
baselines in similarity to ground-truth responses, 
demonstrating its ability to generate more personalized and 
contextually relevant outputs.  
Another study, [21] LLM-Therapist explores how fine-
tuned LLMs can analyze patient data and generate responses 
based on the provided information. This approach enables 
more personalized and context-aware responses from the 
chatbot. The researchers evaluated their model using 
relevance and user satisfaction metrics, achieving strong 
results in both aspects. The application of RAG has also been 
extended to text-to-speech systems [22], where the study 
utilized a knowledge graph-based retrieval mechanism. The 
researchers compared three RAG architectures: Ensemble

III. METHODOLOGY 
The system architecture for Sanity-LLM, as shown in Fig. 
1, comprises seven key sections: dataset preparation, query 
optimization, hybrid search, reranking, LLM Selection & 
prompt engineering, test set creation, and lastly RAG 
evaluation. 
A. Dataset Preparation 
To prepare the data, a preprocessing pipeline was applied 
to normalize text by removing extra whitespace, correcting 
spacing around punctuation, and standardizing quotation 
marks. Entries with null or excessively short responses were 
removed, and duplicate (Context, Response)  pairs were 
dropped while retaining multiple valid answers for the same 
context. The resulting dataset was then grouped so that each 
context could include several bullet-point responses, ensuring 
both cleanliness and diversity in the knowledge base. 
Finally, the combined dataset was embedded and indexed 
using Facebook AI Similarity Search (FAISS) to enable dense 
vector retrieval. The context component was also tokenized 
and processed with BM25 to support hybrid search in later 
stages.  
Since the data concerns chats and conversations between 
patients and doctors and may contain sensitive and even 
personal information, we have taken steps to anonymize the 
data to ensure the safety of the individuals who participated in 
the conversations by erasing any names, addresses, phone 
numbers/emails, or other personal details before utilizing the 
data in our system. 
B. Query Optimization 
For query optimization, user queries are processed using a 
stepback prompting technique. This approach transforms the 
original query into a simpler and more fundamental form, 
enabling the model to better understand the underlying intent 
of the question. By generating stepback questions, the system 
enhances the accuracy of document retrieval and ensures that 
the retrieved information is more closely aligned with the 
user’s actual needs. 
C. Hybrid Search 
As illustrated in Fig.2, both the original query and the 
corresponding stepback question undergo a hybrid search on 
the FAISS Database to retrieve the most relevant document. 
This hybrid approach is one of the most widely adopted 
techniques in advanced RAG systems, as it combines 
keyword-based and semantic-based retrieval. On the keyword 
side, the query is matched against tokenized documents using 
the BM25 algorithm, which computes relevance scores based 
on keyword frequency and distribution. Simultaneously, on 
the semantic side, the query is embedded and compared with 
the embedded documents stored in FAISS, where cosine 
similarity is used to measure semantic closeness and generate 
semantic scores. To ensure comparability, both BM25 and 
semantic scores are normalized using Min–Max scaling, after 
which the scores are combined into a single fusion score. 
Finally, documents are ranked according to their fusion 
scores, and the top-N most relevant documents are selected for 
subsequent processing in the RAG system. 
To determine the optimal weighting between the two 
retrieval methods, experiments were conducted across 
different weight ratios ranging from 0.1 to 0.9. The 
performance of each configuration was evaluated using 
RAGAS metrics, and the configuration yielding the best 
performance was selected. Experimental results showed that 
the optimal balance occurred when the BM25 weight was 0.2 
and the FAISS weight was 0.8. This configuration achieved 
the best retrieval accuracy by balancing the strengths of 
keyword and semantic search. 
D. Reranking 
After retrieving the documents relevant to both the original 
query and the stepback question, the next step is to combine 
and rerank them to ens ure the most relevant information is 
prioritized. Before feeding the retrieved documents into the 
LLM, all candidate contexts are reranked based on their re 
levance to the user’s original query. This reranking process 
was implemented using Cohere’s Rerank API with the rerank-

E. LLM Selection & Prompt Engineering 
In this system, the Large Language Model (LLM) plays a 
crucial role. The embedding layer is primarily used during the 
retrieval phase of the RAG process. Before performing a 
search in the vector database, both the user’s query and the 
stored documents are converted into embeddings to enable 
semantic similarity comparison. This embedding process is 
essential for achieving accurate semantic search. For 
embedding, we used the text-embedding-3-small model from 
OpenAI, which produces 1,536-dimensional embeddings for 
all context and query inputs. In this research, we also 
employed GPT-4o-mini, a compact version of GPT-4, to 
balance performance with computational efficiency and cost. 
Another critical component is prompt engineering, which 
ensures that the LLM generates responses strictly based on the 
provided dataset, in this case, the Q&A corpus. When a user 
query falls outside the dataset’s scope, the prompt is designed 
to prevent the model from generating unrelated or fabricated 
content. This strategy helps mitigate hallucinations and 
ensures that the system delivers accurate and contextually 
grounded responses.  
F. Test Set Creation 
First, we selected 14 topics that frequently appeared in the 
dataset. From each topic, we chose two representative 
questions from the database: one short-to-medium question 
(50–150 characters in length) and one long question (more 
than 200 characters in length). Using an LLM, we 
summarized and paraphrased the corresponding original 
answers to produce reference responses, which served as the 
ground truth for evaluating the RAG system using RAGAS 
metrics. This summarization process ensured that the 
evaluation focused solely on the core informational content, 
allowing for a more objective assessment of the system’s 
response quality. 
G. RAG Evaluation 
For the evaluation, we utilized the RAGAS library [24],  
which provides standardized tools for assessing RAG 
systems. The evaluation metrics included faithfulness, 
context recall (recall@k), context precision (precision@k), 
and answer correctness. These metrics were selected to 
comprehensively evaluate both the retrieval and generation 
aspects of the system. Specifically, faithfulness measures the 
consistency of the generated response with the retrieved 
context, while context recall and context precision assess how 
effectively relevant information is retrieved from the 
knowledge base. Answer correctness, on the other hand, 
evaluates the accuracy of the final response compared to the 
reference answer. 
To validate the performance improvements of our 
proposed RAG framework, we also implemented a baseline 
model using a naïve RAG configuration for comparison. The 
naïve RAG employed two retrieval methods: BM25, a 
traditional keyword-based search algorithm, and FAISS, a 
vector-based semantic search method. By comparing the 
advanced RAG against these baselines, we aimed to 
determine the individual and combined contributions of 
stepback prompting, hybrid search, and reranking strategies 
to the overall system performance.  
To determine which part of our advanced RAG system 
was most influential, we also conducted an ablation study by 
evaluating the RAG system with one component removed, 
iterating for each of the components in our system including 
BM25 search, FAISS search, stepback questions, and 
reranking.

might have pruned borderline matches that would have

and FAISS, which achieved 0.8077. The hybrid search 
method helps in retrieving higher-quality documents, while 
the reranking helps highlight the most supportive documents 
and play down distracting documents. This leads to the 
model’s claims being more traceable in the retrieved text and 
an overall higher faithfulness score. The FAISS results might 
also indicate that vector-based retrieval alone could retrieve 
semantically similar documents, but not contextually aligned, 
leading the model to produce answers that might stray from 
the provided evidence. 
Table II presents the RAGAS evaluation metrics 
comparing the advanced RAG to the ablation study 
configuration. The advanced RAG achieved the best balance 
of grounding and answer quality compared to the other 
configurations. It was able to achieve the highest faithfulness 
and answer correctness, while its context precision and recall 
were still near the top. In contrast, the other ablations were 
able to achieve higher raw retrieval metrics, but falls behind 
on response quality. These patterns suggest that the hybrid 
retrieval plus reranking and stepback are able to convert 
retrieval documents into better grounded, more accurate 
answers, even when pure retrieval look marginally stronger 
for the ablations. So, the BM25 and FAISS search methods 
helped with the retrieval part of the system, while the 
reranking and stepback helped with the answer quality and 
groundedness. 
These results collectively suggest that the proposed 
advanced RAG system is able to deliver meaningful 
improvements to naïve RAG techniques for mental health 
chatbot applications. The drastic increase in Answer 
Correctness while maintaining a high Faithfulness score 
indicates that the system can provide more helpful responses 
without sacrificing factual grounding, which is critical when 
handling mental health issues. However, the modest gains in 
Context Precision and Recall suggests that further 
optimization of the retrieval pipeline can still be done to 
ensure a more comprehensive and precisely ranked context 
retrieval. 
While this RAG-based assistant achieves encouraging 
scores on evaluation metrics such as answer correctness, it is 
not and should not be treated as a replacement for licensed 
psychologists, therapists, or other qualified clinicians. In our 
view, it is best understood as a supportive first-line tool, 
handling straightforward questions and encouraging early 
help‑seeking, not to diagnose conditions, deliver therapy, or 
offer personalized medical advice. All outputs should be 
interpreted with human judgement, and users should be 
guided toward clinical pathways that ensure safety, 
accountability, and continuity of care. 
V. CONCLUSION 
In conclusion, this study introduced an advanced RAG 
architecture that integrates hybrid search, step-back 
questioning, and a reranker mechanism. The system was 
implemented in a Sanity-LLM, designed to deliver 
informative, empathetic, and safe responses. Two open-
source datasets from Hugging Face were embedded and 
indexed using FAISS. The retrieval pipeline combined hybrid 
semantic-keyword search, step-back prompting, and Cohere-
based reranking, while prompt engineering guided GPT-4 to 
generate grounded responses with minimal hallucination. 
Evaluation results using RAGAS metrics demonstrate 
clear improvements over baseline methods. The Advanced 
RAG achieved the highest Context Precision  (0.9391), 
Faithfulness (0.8754), and Answer Correctness  (0.6297), 
indicating more precise retrieval and higher factual 
consistency. The Ablation Study further confirmed that each 
module: hybrid search, step-back questioning, and reranking 
contributed meaningfully to overall performance. Notably, 
removing reranking or step-back questions led to decreases 
in answer correctness and faithfulness. These findings 
highlight that the proposed RAG architecture effectively 
balances relevance, accuracy, and grounding, offering a 
robust foundation for trustworthy mental health dialogue 
systems. 
Although the performance of Sanity-LLM is good, 
several limitations remain. First, the current evaluation relied 
on open datasets rather than real patient interactions, which 
may limit ecological validity. Second, while retrieval and 
generation scores are high, empathy and safety were assessed 
indirectly and require validation through expert and user 
feedback. Third, the system depends on static datasets that
