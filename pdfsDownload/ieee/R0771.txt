Abstract—Prompt injection attacks is an important security 
problem in healthcare-specific large language model 
systems(LLMs). The injected prompt can be used to control the 
behavior of the model or to leak sensitive data. This vulnerability 
increases with centralized training pipelines that collect patient 
prompts on a single server, which leads to data leakage and model 
poisoning. In this work, we examine Federated Learning (FL) as a 
privacy-preserving and decentralized prompt injection detection 
defense for medical chatbots. Using the SPML Healthcare Chatbot 
Prompt Injection Dataset, we fine-tune a RoBERTa-base classifier 
in conditions of centralized, IID (Independent and Identically 
Distributed), federated and Non-IID (Non-Independent and Non-
Identically Distributed) federated learning. The experimental 
results show that the federated model has comparable 
performance with centralized training, with accuracy of 0.957 and 
F1-score of 0.964 under IID conditions. The realistic case scenario 
of Non-IID client distribution was evaluated across 3 different 
client configurations, the model is able to maintain stable 
performance with an average performance accuracy of 0.956, 
precision of 0.942, recall of 0.987, F1-Score of 0.964 and AUC of 
0.994. Moreover, the federated setup shows consistent convergence 
between the communication rounds despite the data heterogeneity. 
These results suggest that transformer-based federated learning 
provides an effective and privacy-preserving solution for detecting 
prompt injection attacks in distributed healthcare conversational 
AI systems. This work contributes to Sustainable Development 
Goal (SDG) 3: Good Health and Wellbeing, SDG 9: Industry, 
Innovation and Infrastructure, and SDG 16: Peace, Justice and 
Strong Institutions by enhancing the security and privacy of AI-
driven healthcare technologies. 
Index Terms —Prompt Injection Attacks (PIA), Federated 
Learning (FL), Healthcare Chatbots, RoBERTa, Natural 
Language Processing (NLP), AI Security, Sustainable 
Development Goals (SDG 3, SDG 9, SDG 16). 
 
I. INTRODUCTION 
The progress in LLMs such as as GPT, BERT, and RoBERTa 
has contributed to NLP to a great extent which helped in 
understanding clinical note, to get medical assistance. The 
increasing dependence on prompt-based interaction has 
introduced a security risk known as PIAs. These attacks are 
done by inserting malicious instructions into user prompts to 
override model controls that leads to misdiagnosis, unsafe 
clinical advice or give out sensitive medical information, these 
are harmful threats to medical apps such as telemedicine 
systems, digital triage systems, and clinical chatbots [1], [2]. 
From the recent studies, LLM-based conversations can be 
exploited for social engineering and fraudulent activities, these 
strongly show the need to protect healthcare chatbots against 
adversarial prompt manipulation [3]. LLMs follow instructions, 
prompts are crafted in such a way that this trait is exploited[1]. 
Generative AI increases the risk of harmful content spreading 
across online platforms, which asserts the need to detect and 
prevent malicious prompts before they corrupt the sensitive 
healthcare data and information [4].The attack results in sharing 
protected health information (PHI) or giving untrustworthy 
medical advice, creating serious concerns for healthcare 
privacy regulations [2]. As conversational AI systems are 
widely used in clinical workflows they are prone to prompt 
injection attacks[1]. 
Existing defenses such as input sanitization and context 
isolation struggle to detect semantically complicated attacks 
that bypass the pattern-based detection[2]. Most of the methods 
are based on centralized training, where raw patient data are 
centralized on one server. This makes it easier to poison data, 
manipulate models, and leak sensitive clinical data, making 
healthcare systems more vulnerable to privacy and security 
breaches [5]. These risks are especially crucial in the medical 
environment, where regulations such as HIPAA and GDPR 
emphasize the need to adopt decentralized learning technique 
that will not collect sensitive data, ensuring that the model 
remains intact. 
FL is one of the technologies that can resolve these issues by 
facilitating the joint training of models in a group of healthcare

II. L ITERATURE REVIEW  
A. Prompt Injection Attacks in Healthcare AI 
PIAs utilize how LLMs interpret and execute 
naturallanguage instructions. LLMs can be manipulat ed to 
bypass system constraints, expose confidential data , or behave 
in unintended ways [1]. Advanced attacks, including  
mathematically encoded or function based prompts , can bypass 
simple keyword filtering and context isolation mech anisms. 
These show the limitations of input validation [2]. 
These attacks should not be taken lightly as strict  privacy 
requirements and safe decision making is involved in healthcare 
sector. Multimodal medical vision language models [ 9] can be 
exploited to generate misleading diagnoses. This in dicates that 
PIAs pose serious problems on high-stakes clinical settings. 
B. Defenses Against Prompt Injection in Medical Systems 
Techniques such as filtering, sanitization, and con text 
separation [1], [2], provide security but they stru ggle with 
complex attacks in healthcare chatbots. Hybrid mach ine 
learning approaches can provide improved robustness  and 
anomaly detection capabilities, which can also be r elevant for 
detecting adversarial prompts in healthcare chatbot s[10]. Most 
existing solutions rely on centralized approach whe re patient 
information is processed on a central server which increases the 
risk of information leakage, data poisoning, and un authorized 
access to PHI. 
C. Centralized Learning Vulnerabilities in Healthcare 
Central systems makes use of the PHI for training a nd this 
information provides model updates. Centralized system can be 
attacked by poisoning attacks or to leak informatio n [7], [11]. 
Such attacks not only affect technical systems but also the trust 
on such LLMs[12]. Even simple manipulation of the 
parameters can affect the performance or cause bias [7]. HIPAA 
and GDPR are some of the regulations that limit cen tralized 
management of sensitive information. New AI models focus on 
the decentralized and distributed learning in order  to enhance 
security and scalability [6]. 
D. Federated Learning for Secure Healthcare NLP 
FL provides a decentralized approach, where local m odels 
are trained at clients and the combined model updat es are 
exchanged, which reduces PHI sharing and adversaria l 
propagation. Light and distributed security solutions emphasize 
the significance of efficient and adaptive detectio n of the 
resourceconstrained environment [13]. FL is capable  of 
reaching the performance of centralized learning an d keeping 
privacy intact at the same time [6], [14].It enhanc es the 
resistance against poisoning and inference attacks, also efficient 
transformer updates are used to minimize the commun ication 
overheads[7], [11]. Balancing robustness, latency, and resource 
cost is an issue in distributed healthcare systems [15]. 
E. Research Gap 
PIAs and FL are being studied individually, but little research 
has been done to understand and study how the two c an be 
integrated into healthcare settings. Existing PIA d efenses are 
based on centralized methods, and only few FL resea rch pay 
attention to adversarial prompt detection. In this work, we 
propose a federated transformer-based system powere d by the 
SPML Chatbot Prompt Injection Dataset [8]with a foc us on 
balancing detection performance, privacy preservati on, and 
healthcare regulatory requirements. 
 
III. P ROPOSED METHODOLOGY  
A. System Overview 
The proposed framework is to identify prompt inject ion 
attacks to healthcare-based LLM systems without requiring the 
centralized storage of sensitive clinical data. To achieve this, we 
combine FL with RoBERTa-based text classification m odel. 
Each participating healthcare institutions or clien t devices 
trains the model with their own medical prompts, so  that the 
raw patient data never leaves the client side. Only  model 
updates are shared with the central server, which h elps reduce 
the risk of PHI exposure and related privacy issues. 
This framework follows the basic principles of FL described 
in [6], [7], but adapts them for adversarial prompt  detection in 
clinical dialogue systems. The main focus of the pr oject is to 
have a strong detection performance while reducing the risk of 
data leakage, model poisoning , and privacy violati ons. This 
approach is suitable for healthcare settings where patient data 
confidentiality is highly important.

B. Architectural Design 
The proposed architecture consists of two layers.Th e first 
layer consists of participating client institutions. Each client has 
its own benign and adversarial prompt data and perf orms local 
fine-tuning of the RoBERTa model without sharing raw patient 
text or clinical conversations. The data never leav es the client 
side which address PHI-related privacy concerns and  supports 
and obeys with healthcare privacy laws. 
The second layer is the federated server layer, which manages 
the global learning process. The server collects locally updated 
model parameters from each client and aggregates th em using

C. Data Preprocessing Pipeline 
For experimental evaluation, we make use of SPML Chatbot 
Prompt Injection Dataset [8], this dataset consist of system 
prompts, user prompts, and their corresponding labe ls. As the 
work focuses on healthcare related prompts , the da taset was 
filtered to retain only healthcare entries. 
To preserve conversational semantics, the system an d user 
prompts were combined into a single input sequence before 
tokenization. The merged text was then processed us ing the 
RoBERTa tokenizer based on Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)  [1]. 
This produces token IDs and attention masks, which are later 
converted into PyTorch tensors such as input_ids, 
attention_mask, and labels. This preprocessing step helps retain 
both contextual information and clinical intent, al lowing the 
model to better distinguish normal healthcare conve rsations 
from adversarially manipulated prompts. 
D. Model Configuration 
We chose RoBERTa-base after initial experiments of 
comparison with DistilBERT-base-uncased and DeBERTa -v3-
base. Although all three models demonstrated a similar level of 
performance under a centralized context, RoBERTa 
demonstrated higher levels of stability and robustn ess when 
trained with federated constraints. Each client fin e-tunes the 
RoBERTa model locally using the following configuration: 
• learning rate of 2×10 −5, 
• batch size of 8, 
• three local training epochs per communication round, 
• AdamW optimizer. 
Once the clients have done their local updates, the y transmit 
only the updated model weights to the federated ser ver. The 
server gathers together these updates and sends the  new global 
model for the next communication round. This iterative process 
continues until the global model gets to a stable state.

IV . E XPERIMENTAL SETUP  
A. Dataset Preparation 
All experiments were done using the healthcare subset of the 
SPML Prompt Injection Dataset [8], which has both benign and 
adversarial prompts designed to simulate prompt inj ection 
attacks in medical chatbots. The system prompts and  user 
prompts were combined before preprocessing. Since the dataset 
was imbalanced, paraphrase-based data augmentation was 
applied using a Pegasus-style transformer model (Va msi/T5 
Paraphrase Paws).Beam search (num beam=3) and temperature 
tuning were used to generate semantically similar b ut diverse 
prompt variations. The generated samples were then filtered to 
have only natural and unique prompts.After preproce ssing and 
augmentation, the final dataset was 7,243 samples o f which 
2,949 were benign and 4,294 were malicious. 
B. Client Data Partitioning 
The dataset was allocated to three clients in order to simulate 
distributed healthcare environments. The IID settin g randomly 
shuffled and split samples such that a client recei ved an equal 
amount of benign and malicious prompts. In the case  of Non-
IID experiments, data heterogeneity was added throu gh 
Dirichlet partitioning with concentration parameter s of α =1 .0, 
0.5, and 0 .3, which are mild, moderate and severe skew, 
respectively. The federated training process consis ted of four 
communication rounds where all clients participated  in each 
round. Only the model updates were exchanged with the central 
server, and the results were averaged over three runs. 
C. Training Setup 
The federated experiments used three clients perfor ming 
local fine-tuning followed by FedAvg aggregation at the server. 
RoBERTa-base served as the backbone model. The same  
tokenization and preprocessing rules were applied a cross all 
clients. 
D. Dataset partitioning 
In order to decrease sensitivity to a single client partition, the 
experiment was run several times on different rando m NonIID 
client splits created under varying Dirichlet conce ntration 
parameters and random seeds. This facilitates the assessment of 
the stability of FL performance in various client d ata setups 
instead of a fixed division. 
E. Environment 
All experiments were run in Google Colab on an NVID IA 
Tesla T4 GPU (16GB VRAM). Random seeds were fixed f or 
reproducibility.

V . R ESULTS  
A. Backbone Model Selection 
BERT-base, DistilBERT-base, and RoBERTa-base were 
tested in order to determine model to use in the FL framework. 
All the models were tested on both IID and Non-IID data 
distribution, but there was differences in the fede rated 
environments where there is Non-IID distribution of client data. 
The outcomes mentioned here are an average of three runs that 
involve three clients and four rounds of communication. 
All of the models gave similar outcomes under the I ID 
condition, yet the Non-IID outcomes reveal some cri tical 
differences. BERT is the most degraded and DistilBE RT is 
moderately stable. RoBERTa has almost the same IID and Non-
IID performance, and it is more robust to heterogen eous client 
data. Since healthcare has applications that need r eliability, 
RoBERTa-base is chosen to be the base model. RoBERTa was 
chosen as it offers better contextual information a nd better 
detection results than lightweight models, which is  why it is 
appropriate regardless of its moderate computational cost. 
B. Model Performance Evaluation 
1)  Centralized: The initial training of RoBERTa-base 
was conducted on a centralized basis to determine a n upper 
limit of performance. The model has an accuracy of 0.96, F1-
score of 0.9669, and an AUC of 0.9958 which proves to be 
effective in the event that all the data are centrally available. 
2)  Federated IID: In the case of IID federated training, 
when the clients are given equal data partitions, t he model has 
the closest performance as centralized with an accu racy of 
0.9572 and an F1-score of 0.9644. This implies that  there is 
little degradation in data distributions that are homogeneous. 
3)  Federated Non-IID: In real-life Non-IID conditions 
where the distribution of client data is uneven, th e first 
communication round has a small drop in performance , and 
then the performance gradually improves, which fina lly 
reaches a final accuracy of 0.9545 and an F1-score of 0.9622. 
To further test, FL was tested on three Dirichlet-b ased 
NonIID splits with concentration parameters of alpha = 1.0, 0.5, 
and 0.3 which are mild, moderate, and severe hetero geneity. 
Performance is also similar across all configuratio ns, meaning 
that the model shows limited sensitivity to client partitioning.

VI. D ISCUSSION  
While comparing with a centralized prompt injection  
detection approach[18], decentralization does not c ome at the 
cost of detection performance. Although centralized  
ensemblebased approaches are characterized by high accuracy 
and the ability to discriminate, the suggested RoBE RTa-based 
federated model is equally accurate and slightly better in its F1-
score and AUC in both IID and Non-IID conditions. 
The federated model is stable at different degrees of client 
data heterogeneity, which implies strong resistance  to skewed 
and fragmented healthcare data distributions. An im portant 
aspect of this approach is that we are not storing the raw patient 
data in the central server, which reduces the risk of data leakage 
and poisoning. However, practical deployment of FL can face 
challenges such as communication overhead from larg e model 
parameters, increased latency during aggregation ro unds, and 
coordination difficulties between distributed clien ts. Despite 
these limitations, FL offers a good balance between privacy and 
effective detection, making it a suitable approach for privacy-
preserving healthcare chatbot systems.

VII.  LIMITA TIONS  
The framework has shown a promising performance but there 
are several limitations. The RoBERTa model introduc es 
additional computational and communication overhead , which 
can affect deployment in low-resource settings. The evaluation 
was limited only to a single dataset and controlled experimental 
conditions, which may not completely reflect real-w orld 
federated healthcare scenarios. 
VIII.  CONCLUSION  
This paper has looked into prompt injection defense  in 
healthcare chatbots using a RoBERTa-based model und er 
centralized, IID federated, and Non-IID FL conditio ns. From 
the experiments held, it clearly shows that the per formance is 
comparable to centralized approach while remaining stable 
under heterogeneous client data distributions. 
Since the framework does not store sensitive prompt s on a 
central server it reduces privacy risks while also maintaining 
effective detection of malicious inputs. The findin gs show that 
FL can provide a practical balance between privacy 
preservation and prompt injection detection in heal thcare 
environments. Future work will focus on evaluating the 
framework with larger datasets, more number of part icipating 
clients, and additional defense mechanisms to impro ve 
robustness against evolving prompt injection attack s. 
Improvements can also include integrating secure ag gregation 
and differential privacy techniques to strengthen data protection 
in healthcare AI systems while supporting Sustainab le 
Development Goals such as SDG 3, SDG 9, and SDG 16.
