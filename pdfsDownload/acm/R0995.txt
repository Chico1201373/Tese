Abstract
Providing accurate and efficient user support with the emerging
large language model (LLM) techniques for high-performance com-
puting (HPC) centers is extremely challenging. The major obstacle
lies in the integration of specialized knowledge that is not available
in the base LLMs. Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT), Retrieval Aug-
mented Generation (RAG), and In-Context Learning (ICL), are the
common techniques to empower LLMs with domain knowledge.
However, there is a lack of comprehensive understanding of their
question-answering capabilities in the HPC domain. In this work,
we present a comparative study of eight candidate methods by com-
bining the base model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), SFT, RAG, and ICL
using a ticket database of 60,000 question-answer pairs from Texas
Advanced Computing Center (TACC). We evaluate these methods
with five metrics: correctness, coherence, relevance, fluency, and
cosine similarity, using both human and LLMs as judges. Our re-
sults show that ICL+RAG with the base model achieves the highest
correctness in general, and ICL with the base model outperforms
others for debugging and testing questions. The results suggest that
combining ICL, RAG, and a powerful base model is a promising
solution for LLM-enabled user support in computing centers.

1 Introduction
Modern scientific research relies heavily on large multi-purpose
computers such as High-Performance Computing (HPC) and High-
Throughput Computing (HTC) systems, GPUs, specialized AI-focused
processors, and academic clouds (e.g., Jetstream2 [13]). These sys-
tems are used for many purposes: not just to validate hypotheses
with numerical simulation [22, 26], but also to formulate hypotheses
by analyzing large datasets with statistical and machine learning
methods [8, 11] Computing centers such as the National Center for
Supercomputing Applications (NCSA), Ohio Supercomputer Center
(OSC), San Diego Supercomputer Center (SDSC), Texas Advanced
Computing Center (TACC), and campus computing centers sup-
port tens of thousands of users with widely varying expertise and
experience.
It is challenging to provide high-quality and efficient user support
for these computing centers. The HPC experience among users
is vastly diverse, from novice users to experienced experts. The
software-hardware stacks of modern HPC applications, such as the
PyTorch/NCCL/CUDA/GPU stack, are complicated. The question
types and update frequencies of relevant data vary significantly. For
example, the user data management policy remains unchanged for
years while the system availability status is updated every second.
The data sources of the answers are heterogeneous, as the relevant
information can be in documents, images, or videos.
With the recent breakthroughs in Large Language Models (LLMs)
as conversational assistants [20], computing centers have adopted
PEARC ’26, July 26–30, 2026, Minneapolis, MN, USA Mingkai Zheng, Fangru Linghu, Sikan Li, Marty Charles Kandes, Niall Gaffney, Ian Foster, and Zhao Zhang
LLMs to enable HPC assistants that resolve user inquiries [4]. The
major challenge of exploiting LLMs as HPC assistants is how to
integrate specialized knowledge that is not available in the base
LLMs. Several approaches have been explored, including supervised
fine-tuning (SFT), retrieval augmented generation (RAG), and in-
context learning (ICL). SFT continues to train the pre-trained model
with a labeled domain dataset [ 6, 23]. RAG encodes related data
(e.g., documents) in a vector database and then provides related
documents to a question as context to LLMs [ 16]. ICL provides
related data sources, such as similar question-answer pairs, about
the question to LLMs.
Despite the early deployment of LLM-enabled HPC assistants in
computing centers, there is a lack of a comprehensive understand-
ing of the question-answering capability of the candidate methods
in the HPC community. Although researchers have compared SFT,
RAG, and ICL in other contexts [ 1, 18, 27]. These studies either
covered just a subset of available techniques, targeted closed-ended
questions (i.e., answers with limited options, such as “yes” and “no”),
or specialized in another domain. For example, Mosbach et al. [18]
compared the in-domain and generalization performance of OPT
models [31] with SFT and ICL on downstream tasks, including RTE
[5] and MNLI [29]. Soudani et al. [27] consider only SFT and RAG.
Balaguer et al. [1] consider open-ended questions, which require
descriptive and detailed answers, but only in agriculture and with
just RAG and SFT. Prior work provides limited insights into our
goal of leveraging LLM techniques to enable HPC assistants.
This missing link motivates us to perform a comparative study on
SFT, ICL, and RAG to support HPC users. Specifically, we compare
the performance of these three techniques and their combinations
(e.g., SFT+RAG+ICL) in answering open-ended questions submitted
by HPC users. As a case study, we use a ticket database with 60,000
question-answer pairs from TACC as the SFT dataset, ICL examples,
and evaluation dataset. To build the RAG database, we leverage the
online documents provided by TACC, including general user guides
and software documentation. We use five metrics to evaluate the
quality of the generated answers: correctness, coherence, relevance,
fluency, and the cosine similarity between the generated answer and
the response from TACC staff. During the research, we noticed that
an efficient and accurate evaluation mechanism is another challenge.
We used both human evaluation and LLM-as-a-judge [32] for the
evaluation process.
To evaluate the performance of the candidate methods and their
combinations, we classify user tickets into four categories: policy in-
quiries, debugging and testing, technical suggestions, and machine
status-related questions. We select Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [12] as
the base model and develop the SFT models by fine-tuning it. We
compare eight methods by combining various methods for each
category. Our results show that ICL+RAG with the base model
achieves higher correctness across all categories, while the base
model with ICL shows superior correctness for debugging and test-
ing. Surprisingly, the SFT models are less effective in extracting
relevant information from external data sources retrieved by RAG
and ICL than the base model. Based on the results, we recommend
that computing centers consider combining ICL and RAG with
powerful base models to build their HPC assistants.
2 Background
In this section, we provide the background on supervised fine-
tuning, retrieval augmented generation, and in-context learning.
2.1 Supervised Fine-tuning
LLM training usually starts with unsupervised pre-training on a
large, diverse text corpus, where the LLM can learn the basic lin-
guistic structure across different domains, as it has been pointed out
by many recent works that LLMs mainly learn knowledge through
pre-training [33]. Depending on the size of the pre-training dataset
and the model size, pre-training an LLM can take several months
on tens of thousands of high-end GPUs. For instance, the Llama 3.1
405B model was pre-trained on a corpus of about 15T tokens on
16K H100 GPUs for 54 days [12].
SFT continues to train the pre-trained model without the original
optimizer states on a labeled domain-specific dataset [6, 23]. SFT
dataset usually contains sets of carefully curated question-answer
pairs, and its number of tokens is much smaller than that of the
pre-training corpus. Through SFT, pre-trained models can learn to
follow the desired response patterns and format. Although the SFT
dataset is usually small, the fine-tuned LLM can perform well and
can generalize to unseen tasks. For instance, with an SFT dataset
that contains as few as 1,000 question-answer pairs, the fine-tuned
Llama 65B model can exhibit performance as strong as DaVinci003
on many tasks [33].
2.2 Retrieval Augmented Generation
Unlike post-training, which continues fine-tuning the pre-trained
LLM on customized or private datasets, RAG does not require model
training. Instead, RAG builds an external vector database containing
useful information, such as software documentation, to answer user
questions.
The workflow of RAG is illustrated in Figure 1. The RAG vector
database is constructed using an embedding model. Such an em-
bedding model first converts the tokens in a text into a vector of
numerical values and then stores them in a database, where the
indices are the embedding vectors. Text sources are usually bro-
ken into smaller splits, which helps increase the relevance of the
retrieval result. When a question is submitted to a RAG pipeline,
the embedding model first converts the question into a vector rep-
resentation. Then, it uses some algorithm (e.g., cosine similarity)
to find the most similar vector index in the database and returns
the corresponding text. The question, combined with the retrieved
information, is then fed as input into the LLM
RAG has many advantages over post-training with private data.
First, RAG does not require model training. As the performance
of the state-of-the-art model improves, the performance of RAG
improves as well. Second, RAG is good at solving questions requir-
ing frequent updates from external sources, such as user account
questions. With access to the user account database, RAG can pro-
vide the most accurate and up-to-date information to the LLM to
help solve the problem better. Finally, with the external data source,
RAG helps reduce LLM’s hallucination problem.
RAG also has its own limitations. For instance, RAG cannot
provide accurate answers when the required information is inacces-
sible. The hallucination problem in the generated answers cannot be
A Comparative Study on LLM-enabled HPC User Support PEARC ’26, July 26–30, 2026, Minneapolis, MN, USA
avoided if the provided information includes fabrication. Moreover,
as the external source usually includes irrelevant information, the
number of tokens in such an augmented question might exceed the
model’s maximum context length limit, causing the performance
to degrade or even the out-of-memory issue.
2.3 In-Context Learning
In-context learning (ICL), which is also known as few-shot learning,
is similar to RAG: it does not require training on the model and relies
on external data sources. The difference between them is that ICL’s
data sources are examples similar to the question. By conditioning
the model on the sequence of such demonstrations, the model is
expected to answer the question by using the information in the
examples and following their patterns. According to OpenAI’s work
[3], the number of examples is usually proportional to the quality
and accuracy of the generated answers.
3 Ticket Database Analysis
In this section, we describe the tickets collected in the TACC data-
base, the data cleaning techniques to extract the main content from
tickets, and the categorization strategy.
3.1 Training and Evaluation Dataset
We collect tickets in TACC’s database to construct the training
dataset for supervised fine-tuning, the data source for the ICL ap-
proach, and the evaluation dataset to compare the performance of
different approaches. Specifically, we use conversations between
TACC staff and users to form a multi-round conversation dataset
of more than 60,000 question-answer instances. Then, we use 95%
of these instances as the training dataset and used the remaining
5% for evaluation.
It is worth mentioning that a dataset composed of multi-round
conversations should not be randomly sampled to form training
and evaluation subsets because it is highly likely that a long conver-
sation in the evaluation dataset has a portion of the conversation
leaked into the training dataset, causing the evaluation loss to not
reflect SFT model’s true generalization performance. Therefore, be-
fore forming multi-round conversions, we group the conversions of
the same ticket and assign it a unique ticket number. Then, we ran-
domly sample the ticket numbers into different partitions. Finally,
we construct the multi-round conversion dataset.
We remove the redundant information in the tickets to improve
the overall quality of the dataset. The redundant information in-
cludes: 1) the metadata generated by the ticketing system, which
is mainly auto-generated tags, such as category, queue, subject,
requester, date, time, etc., 2) the metadata generated by the email
website, including the conversation histories attached at the end of
each email, and 3) intermediate emails from TACC: e.g., “Ticket
resolved”, “Marking as resolved ”, and “A ticket has been
created in your Queue. ” As the tickets contain back-and-forth
conversations between a user and TACC staff, they are naturally
suitable for the dataset, which consists of multi-round question-
answering. We exclude images attached to the tickets for two rea-
sons. First, our pre-trained model is not multimodal; it can only
handle text as input. Second, after using Optical Character Recogni-
tion (OCR) to extract the error messages from the screenshots, we
find that the error messages contain thousands of tokens, exceeding
the prompt limit for the supervised fine-tuned model.
3.2 Categorization
We categorize the tickets into four categories: technical questions,
machine status questions, debugging questions, and policy-related
questions. We evaluate the performance of each candidate method
for different categories separately. This categorization is performed
because questions of different categories may require different
sources of information.
The technical questions refer to those that require TACC techni-
cal staff’s help, including issues with software usage and installation
on TACC machines. Some examples areHow to install PyTorch
on TACC’s LS6 , How to accelerate file-transferring speed
when scp is too slow , When will TACC upgrade R to its
latest version , How to properly set up Multi-Factor
Authentication (MFA) on personal device , andHow to acquire
a license for using Matlab on TACC’s machine .
The machine status questions are about network status, the file
system, or a particular machine. These questions are raised when
users experience a significant slowdown in accessing TACC’s file
system or cannot submit jobs to SLURM scheduler. To solve these
questions, we need the real-time machine status. Although TACC
frequently updates machine status and posts maintenance dates on
its website, information about the transient machine unavailability
is missing.
The debugging questions are those seeking help on runtime
errors. These questions are usually attached with a very long error
message, but some users prefer to attach a screenshot or the path
to the log file to their tickets. However, we did not extract the error
messages from the images and error logs because 1) most of the
error messages contain thousands of tokens that can easily exceed
the context limit of the supervised fine-tuned model, and 2) users’
file privacy must be respected.
Most policy-related questions can be solved with reference to
TACC’s documentation. Some examples are How to register a
TACC account , How to reset the password for TACC account ,
How to acquire allocation and machine hours on TACC’s
Frontera, How often does TACC purge files in $SCRATCH ,
and Can TACC help restore accidentally deleted files in
$WORK.
The statistics for the cleaned dataset show that policy-related
questions account for the largest share, and machine status ques-
tions are the least popular. Additionally, we do not evaluate the
performance on machine status questions in this work due to the
infeasibility of reproducibility.

the performance of each method.

ferent LLM methods on open-ended questions submitted by TACC

4.2 RAG Data Collection and Embedding Model
We collect documentation provided by TACC from three base web-
sites, including tacc.utexas.edu, docs.tacc.utex
as.edu, and cvw.cac.cornell.edu. The first URL contains the
most up-to-date notice about system status and news. The second
one contains the user document for all the machines and software,
with examples. The last one contains more commonly used shell
scripts for running software on TACC machines. We recursively
scrape all the contents on the website under these base URLs. For
tables on these websites, directly extracting the text information
breaks their structures. Thus, we convert the scraped website to
Markdown format to preserve the structural information. When
using RAG and ICL, answers generated from context and exam-
ples in Markdown format are easier to display, thereby increasing
readability.
For those use RAG and ICL, we use nomic-embed-text-v1.5 [19]
as the embedding model, and we used SKLearnVectorStore imple-
mented by Scikit-learn [21] to build the vector database. We split
the text document and question-answer pairs into 400-token chunks
to avoid exceeding the maximum context length for LLMs and pre-
serve the information integrity. When generating an answer, the
retriever of the vector database returns the top two text splits that
are most similar to the question.
4.3 Judge Model and Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate the performance of the candidate methods with five
metrics: relevance, coherence, fluency, cosine similarity between the
response from TACC staff and the answer generated by LLMs, and
correctness. To increase the scalability of accessing relevance, coher-
ence, and fluency, we use LLM-as-a-judge withDeepSeek-R1-Distil-
l-Qwen-14B [7] as the judge model and DeepEval framework [14]
to quantitatively measure the scores. The evaluation details are as
follows.
Relevance checks the relevance between the question and the
LLM’s generated answer. We directly adopt the built-in Answer
Relevancy method of DeepEval to evaluate relevance. The answer
is split into several statements. Each statement for its relevance,
categorizing them as yes, idk, or no. The final relevance score is
calculated as the proportion of relevant statements (yes and idk) to
the total number of statements, emphasizing direct and potential
relevance.
Coherence is measured by comparing an LLM’s generated an-
swer and the response from TACC. To evaluate coherence, we
prompt the model to judge whether the actual and expected outputs
are semantically aligned, with an emphasis on meaning rather than
superficial similarity in words or sentence structure. The evaluation
focuses on three key aspects: 1) Factual information: whether the
core facts are consistent across outputs; 2) Content completeness:
whether both outputs cover the same key ideas; and 3) Overall
coherence: whether the outputs convey the same meaning in a
semantically cohesive way. Each aspect is rated as high, medium,
or low, with explanations provided for interpretability. The final
coherence score is computed as a weighted average over all aspect
ratings, where “overall coherence” is given double weight to em-
phasize its importance. Rating weights are: high = 1.0, medium =
0.6, and low = 0.2. If no verdict is returned, a perfect score of 1.0 is
assigned by default.
Fluency evaluates an LLM’s language proficiency when an-
swering tickets. To evaluate fluency, we analyze the generated text
for linguistic issues such as grammar errors, awkward phrasing,
unnatural sentence structure, repetitive expressions, and other dis-
ruptions to readability. The evaluation identifies specific problems
and categorizes their severity ashigh, medium, or low, with explana-
tions provided for each issue. Each identified issue contributes to a
cumulative penalty score, weighted by severity: high = 0.4, medium
= 0.2, low = 0.1. The final fluency score is calculated as1.0−penalty,
where a perfect fluency (no issues) yields a score of 1.0, and greater
penalties proportionally reduce the score.
Cosine similarity: We compute the normalized cosine similar-
ity between the TACC history response and the LLM-generated
answer to obtain an unbiased measurement of their similarity. We
use the same model, nomic-embed-text-v1.5, as in the RAG and ICL
approaches.
Correctness: We engage computing center staff to evaluate the
correctness of LLM answers. We classify each answer as correct,
partially correct, or incorrect. A generated answer is classified as
correct if it logically follows the operation process of how TACC
A Comparative Study on LLM-enabled HPC User Support PEARC ’26, July 26–30, 2026, Minneapolis, MN, USA
staff would address the corresponding question, especially for code
debugging questions that require further clarification from the HPC
users. For instance, when users who lack experience using HPC
systems encounter runtime errors while using TACC machines,
they tend to send the error messages only and forget to include
the software modules and the path to the code, which are essential
for reproducing errors and troubleshooting them. Therefore, in
this case, if the LLM asks for more information about the error
instead of directly addressing the bug, we still consider it as correct.
When the answer contains partial misinformation, we classify it
as partially correct. If the answer is irrelevant to the question, i.e.,
only summarizing or repeating the content in the tickets, we mark
it as incorrect. We assign scores of 1, 0.5, and 0 to correct, partially
correct, and incorrect answers, respectively.

RAG and ICL information are incorporated into the prompt
as part of the system messages. For RAG, we insert the retrieved
documents immediately after each conversation, following common
prompt design practices to enhance the model’s use of relevant
information. In contrast, the few-shot examples for ICL are placed at
the beginning of the conversation, following the system instruction,
to clearly demonstrate the desired response style.
We fine-tune Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct using DeepSpeed [30] with
a maximum context length of 2048 tokens on 64 GH200 GPUs of
TACC machine. We choose this length for two main reasons: 1)
Only less than 5% of the fine-tuning data contain more than 2048
tokens, and the average number of tokens in these data is more
than 60,000, which are either error messages or TACC’s internal
multi-round conversations across a very long time. Therefore, we
consider them as outliers and remove them from the fine-tuning
dataset. 2) The context length of 2048 is the maximum number of
tokens that can be accommodated on 64 GPUs. We apply many
DeepSpeed’s unique features to fit the model and data into a com-
pute node’s High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) and RAM. First, we
use ZeRO stage 3 [ 24] to partition the AdamW optimizer states,
gradients, and model parameters across all GPUs. Second, we lever-
age DeepSpeed’s CPU-offloading [25] to offload the computation
of updating model parameters and optimizer states to the CPU.
Because of the high computational power provided by the CPU
and the high CPU-GPU communication bandwidth (900 GB/s) of
GH200, CPU offloading does not introduce significant overhead.

We apply LLM-as-a-judge with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B as
the judge model to evaluate the answers generated by using differ-

Instruct), RAG, ICL, RAG+ICL, SFT (i.e., supervised fine-tuned
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), SFT+ICL, SFT+RAG, and SFT+RAG+ICL.

metrics for each category, including technical, debugging, and pol-

5.2.1 Technical questions. From the technical column in Table 1,
we see that approaches that use the base model have much higher
scores than those using the SFT model. The RAG approach has the
highest relevance score, the base model has the highest fluency
score, and the ICL approach has the highest scores for coherence

These results align with our expectations: ICL and RAG can help

SFT model performs worse. A similar result has been observed in
Crystall [28]: when performing SFT with a code dataset on an LLM,
its capability of understanding general human instructions and
reasoning is degraded.
5.2.2 Debugging questions. The evaluation results of the debug-
ging questions are shown in Table 1 under the debugging column.
Similar to the technical questions’ results, the base model has the
highest relevance and fluency scores, the ICL approach has the
highest coherence and cosine similarity between answers, and the
SFT+RAG+ICL has the lowest scores for relevance, fluency, and
cosine similarity.
These evaluation results further highlight the importance of ex-
ternal data sources for answering TACC tickets. ICL and RAG not
only improve the performance of the base model but also help the
SFT model generate better answers. However, when combining the
SFT model, ICL, and RAG together, the quality of the generated an-
swer degrades, as the SFT model fails to extract helpful information
from the external data source.

During the manual evaluation process, we observe several inter-
esting phenomena that help explain the correctness scores achieved
by different approaches. The SFT+RAG performs the worst because
the documentation provided on TACC’s website can provide very
limited information for solving practical technical, debugging, and
policy questions, which explains why the approaches that use ICL
have much higher correctness scores. However, the ICL approach
with irrelevant examples can greatly influence the generated an-
swers, especially when access to the machine is needed. Taking
the issue of SLURM jobs being canceled as an example, there are
two possible reasons: 1) the machine has network and file sys-
tem issues, and 2) the user account is suspended due to improper
activities, such as high I/O activities or running computation- or
communication-intensive tasks on the login nodes. Without access
to the machine to check the file system and the user database to
check the user account status, the LLM can only rely on historical
examples to generate ungrounded answers.
5.3 Lessons Learned from Experiments
In this subsection, we describe the lessons learned from the experi-
ment results.
5.3.1 Prompt Engineering. The prompt format and prompt engi-
neering play an important role in determining the quality of the
generated responses. In early-stage experiments, we utilized the de-
fault prompt formatting provided by LangChain to structure model
input. This approach offered convenience but lacked the flexibility
and token-level controllability. As our work progressed, we moved
to manual prompt formatting aligned with the structure outlined
in the Llama 3.1 documentation, using model-specific tokens. This
resulted in significantly improved generation quality because the
model was able to recognize the intended input structure better
and focus on the salient information.
5.3.2 Data Privacy. We tried to remove user private information
from the question-answer pairs, such as the email address, phone
number, and working address, using the Named Entity Recognition
(NER) technique with BERT. However, some are still left in our
training and evaluation dataset, causing the LLMs to leak such
sensitive information to the generated answers.
5.3.3 Supervised Fine-tuning. Supervised fine-tuning with long
context length is expensive. With 64 GH200 GPUs and DeepSpeed
ZeRO stage 3, we could only fine-tune an 8B-parameter LLM, which
is much smaller than most of the powerful open-source LLMs,
with a maximum context length of 2048 tokens. As the size of the
powerful models continues to increase, the fine-tuning approach for
open-ended question-answering tasks becomes increasingly more
intractable. Moreover, by comparing the answer qualities above,
we have seen that the quality of answers generated by SFT with
ICL and RAG is lower than that of other approaches. Although
performing supervised fine-tuning on an LLM improves its ability
to follow the patterns in history TACC tickets, its performance
of extracting useful information from the retrieved external data
sources degrades. Additionally, with the augmented input prompt
exceeding the SFT model’s maximum number of tokens, during the
inference process, the special tokens for assistant at the end of the
prompt in Listing 1 are truncated by the tokenizer. Without having
the correct system and role prompt that the SFT model is trained
upon, it will be confused by the instruction, causing its performance
to drop. This issue can also be approached by reducing the number
of tokens in every text chunk in the vector database, but to preserve
the information integrity, we did not try smaller text chunks in our
experiment.
6 Related Work
LLMs have been applied in the domain of HPC to address various
tasks. For instance, HPC-GPT [9] performs supervised fine-tuning
using question-answer instances for two applications: 1) detecting
data race in C/C++ and Fortran tasks and 2) managing AI models
and datasets for programming language processing and MLPerf
tasks. Miyashita et al. [ 17] propose Hypothetical Command Em-
bedding (HyCE), which extends the existing RAG architecture for
HPC-specific data, to simplify the access to HPC data for users
unfamiliar with HPC commands and workflows. Jin et al. [15] fine-
tuned a Llama model to detect anomalies in computational work-
flow and compared performance between the fine-tuned LLaMA
model and ICL with GPT2-127M, Mistral-7B-v0.1, and LLama2-7B.

7 Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we design and evaluate SFT, ICL, and RAG, as well
as their possible combinations for the application of answering
tickets from HPC centers. We use five metrics to evaluate the qual-
ity of the generated answers: relevance, coherence, fluency, cosine
similarity between the generation and response from TACC, and
correctness. We use LLM-as-a-judge to automate the evaluation of
relevance, coherence, fluency, and cosine similarity, and we man-
ually evaluate correctness. Through extensive experiments and
careful comparison studies, we observe that ICL+RAG performs
better than other approaches in terms of correctness. Although the
base model without ICL or RAG achieves the highest scores for
relevance and fluency, its correctness is much lower than ICL+RAG.
The supervised fine-tuning model fails to extract important infor-
mation from the external data sources retrieved by RAG and ICL.
Through the manual evaluation process, the groundedness and
effectiveness of the retrieved information can significantly affect
the accuracy of the generated answers. To further improve the cor-
rectness of LLMs for answering user tickets, we will combine an AI
agent with access to the system and the user database to provide
real-time information for LLM generation in future work.
