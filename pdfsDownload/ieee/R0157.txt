Abstract—Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is rising globally,
underscoring the need for scalable and accurate patient educa-
tion. This paper presents the design and evaluation of a CKD
education chatbot specifically for hemodialysis cases in Indonesia
that runs on-premises and employs a Retrieval-Augmented Gen-
eration (RAG) framework. We conducted a comparative study
of a general-purpose LLM (Llama 3.1) and a language adapted
LLM (Sahabat AI). Both models were used in identical RAG
pipelines and paired with several retrieval embeddings. Using
70 unique questions evaluated across 10 runs per configuration
(n = 700), we assessed semantic similarity (METEOR), factual
correctness, and RAG-specific metrics (faithfulness, answer rele-
vancy, context precision) with a Gemini 2.0 Flash evaluator. RAG
consistently improved performance over non-RAG baselines,
with statistically significant gains across metrics. Sahabat AI
outperformed Llama 3.1, posting the highest METEOR (0.4116,
mContriever) and correctness (4.0293 with BGE-M3). It also
exhibits higher, more stable faithfulness (∼0.95), indicating closer
adherence to the source evidence. These findings suggest that, for
healthcare education, localized LLMs combined with carefully
chosen embeddings can yield more effective and trustworthy
systems.
Index Terms—Chatbot; Chronic Kidney Disease; Hemodialy-
sis; Large Language Models; Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
I. INTRODUCTION
Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is an escalating global
health problem, particularly at advanced stages where
hemodialysis is often required as a life-saving intervention. In
2024, an estimated 134,057 individuals underwent hemodial-
ysis in Indonesia, underscoring the national scale of the
challenge [1]. The economic burden is substantial; Indonesia’s
National Health Insurance (BPJS Kesehatan) reportedly allo-
cated approximately IDR 11 trillion in 2024 for CKD-related
treatment [1]. Major risk factors include hypertension, dia-
betes, obesity, and population aging, jointly driving increases
in CKD incidence and prevalence.
Timely detection and patient education are crucial to slow-
ing disease progression and reducing healthcare costs. Conven-
tional education approaches often suffer from limited reach
and barriers to understanding medical terminology. In this
context, artificial intelligence (AI)—particularly chatbots—
offers transformational potential; however, challenges remain
regarding medical accuracy, prevention of model hallucina-
tions, and alignment with up-to-date clinical guidelines, all of
which must be addressed systematically [2].
To tackle these challenges, this study introduces Chatbot
Lisa, an AI solution that evolved from a rule-based system
(version 1.0) to a system based on Large Language Mod-
els (LLMs) combined with Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG) (version 2.0) [3], [4]. RAG was selected for its ability
to anchor LLM outputs to verified external knowledge sources,
thereby reducing the risk of inaccuracies [5]. Integrating
LLM+RAG enables the delivery of medical information that
is contextual, current, and source-traceable, thereby enhancing
patient engagement and understanding, including support for
dietary adherence and lifestyle modifications essential in CKD.
CKD management is resource-intensive, especially at end-
stage, which requires ongoing treatments such as hemodial-
ysis [6]. Many patients remain undiagnosed until advanced
stages, highlighting the urgency of early detection and sus-
tained education [7]. Specifically, RAG addresses the limita-
tions of generic LLMs by injecting relevant external evidence
during inference, improving response accuracy and alignment
with the latest clinical guidelines [8]. In nephrology—where
practice heavily depends on clinical literature and up-to-date
guidelines—the combination of LLMs and RAG can enrich
real-time patient education on complex topics such as dialysis
management and dietary interventions [7], [8].
Despite rapid adoption of LLMs and RAG in medical
domains, important gaps persist: (i) comparative evaluations
between global LLMs and linguistically–culturally localized
LLMs within a specific medical domain; and (ii) deployment
and validation of advanced RAG architectures in healthcare
contexts within developing countries such as Indonesia. The
contributions of this paper are: (1) presenting the implemen-
tation of a hemodialysis-focused medical chatbot built on a
localized LLM (Sahabat AI); (2) providing an empirical com-
parative analysis of a global LLM (Llama 3.1) vs. a local LLM
(Sahabat AI) within a RAG framework; and (3) delivering a
quantitative evaluation that demonstrates the system’s viability
as a CKD patient education tool in Indonesia.

II. METHODOLOGY
The methodology was designed to systematically build and
evaluate a hemodialysis-focused medical chatbot, encompass-
ing system-architecture selection, experimental design, and
a quantitative evaluation framework across key performance
dimensions.
A. LLM Generator
The generator component was responsible for producing
responses. Two LLMs served as the foundation models:
1) Llama 3.1. A foundation LLM from Meta with strong
benchmark performance and a long-context window
suitable for complex medical information [9]. It was a
general-purpose model with solid reasoning and con-
textual understanding, though it might not have fully
captured Indonesian local nuances.
2) Sahabat AI. A Llama-architecture model that under-
went continued pre-training on Indonesian and local-
language corpora, which made it more sensitive to
linguistic–cultural context and local entities [10].
The selection of these LLMs was motivated by three
factors: (1) technical capability—as a cutting-edge global
LLM, Llama 3.1 offered strong reasoning and a long-context
window, both relevant for processing complex medical con-
tent [9]; (2) local relevance—Sahabat AI’s continued pre-
training on Indonesian and local corpora yielded more accurate
clinical-terminology handling and a more natural style for
local users [10]; and (3) data security & sovereignty—open-
source, on-premises deployment ensured full control over data
storage and access, reducing leakage or unauthorized-access
risks in healthcare settings [11].
B. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Architecture
RAG improved LLM accuracy and factuality by integrating
external knowledge at inference time, turning a “closed-book
exam” into an “open-book exam” [5], [12], [13]. The main
flow comprised:
1) Retrieval. The system searched for the most relevant
context from an external knowledge base using a re-
triever based on embedding models.
2) Augmented Generation. Selected context snippets were
injected into the prompt to constrain the LLM’s answer
space to validated evidence, reducing hallucinations and
improving source traceability [5], [12], [13].
C. Embedding Models
Embedding models converted text into vectors so that se-
mantic similarity could be computed during retrieval.
• BGE-M3. A multilingual, open-source embedding
with multi-objective training (multi-purpose, multi-
granularity) that was effective for semantic search,
including Indonesian.
• mContriever. A multilingual, label-free contrastive
embedding for unsupervised dense retrieval that was ef-
fective at distinguishing fine-grained meaning for specific
queries [14].

III. EXPERIMENT
A. Dataset
We compiled dataset resources across three types: (1) open-
domain QA (Alodokter QA pairs and doctor-validated Q&A);
(2) articles from trusted health sites (Halodoc, Alodokter,
KPCDI); and (3) knowledge-base PDFs covering scientific
publications, government regulations (Ministry of Health), and
nephrology consortium guidelines. Table I reports statistics for
the corpora used.
B. RAG
The experiment was designed to evaluate a Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline using LlamaIndex
with Chroma as the vector database. All documents were
preprocessed through semantic chunking, in which the corpus
was segmented into semantically coherent units (e.g., para-
graphs or sections) rather than fixed token windows, ensuring
that each chunk preserved natural meaning boundaries. These
semantic chunks were embedded using multiple embedding
models and stored in Chroma. The retriever was configured
with semantic similarity search and a top-k value of 5, so that
for each query, the five most relevant semantic chunks were
consistently returned.
The workflow proceeded as follows: documents were se-
mantically chunked, embedded, and indexed; queries were
executed to retrieve the top-5 chunks; answers were generated
based on the retrieved context; and both retrieval and gener-
ative quality were evaluated against a gold-standard dataset.
The entire pipeline was executed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU,
which provided the computational power needed for efficient
large-scale embedding, indexing, and evaluation.
C. Evaluation Framework
Experimental design. (i) Scenario 1—Effect of RAG : com-
pared base LLM vs. LLM+RAG; metrics were METEOR and
Correctness (LlamaIndex) [12], [15]. (ii) Scenario 2—Em-
bedding Performance: compared multiple embedding models
within RAG; metrics were Correctness, Faithfulness, Answer
Relevancy, and Context Precision (Ragas) [13].
Evaluation metrics. We assess both generation quality and
retrieval quality using five metrics:
(1) METEOR [15] measures linguistic quality and ade-
quacy of the model response against a reference. Unigram

(2) Correctness quantifies factual accuracy with respect
to the gold answer. For Scenario 1 we use LlamaIndex
CorrectnessEvaluator [12] which provides an LLM-graded
score on a 1–5 scale (higher is better) and a pass/fail flag
given a threshold (we use τ =4). For Scenario 2 we use Ragas
correctness/factuality scoring [13].
(3) Faithfulness / Groundedness (Ragas) [13] evaluates
whether an answer’s key atomic claims are entailed by the
retrieved context; scores are in [0, 1] (higher is better). The
pipeline decomposes the answer into claims, maps each claim
to candidate evidence from the retrieved context, verifies
entailment with an NLI/LLM-based judge, then aggregates
outcomes. In node-based RAG, each context chunk is a node;
a claim is grounded if at least one linked node entails it,
otherwise it is a hallucination. Thus, the reported score directly
reflects the answer’s groundedness with respect to the retrieved
nodes.
(4) Answer Relevancy (Ragas) [13] quantifies how well a
system’s response fulfills the question’s intent by applying an
LLM-based rubric over three aspects—on-topicness (focus on
the query), sufficiency/coverage (completeness of the required
information), and conciseness/non-rambling (absence of irrel-
evant or verbose content). The judge produces per-dimension
scores in [0, 1], and the final relevancy score is obtained by
averaging these values (higher indicates better alignment).
This metric is context-agnostic (i.e., independent of retrieved
evidence) and can be extended in multi-turn scenarios to
include instruction adherence and consistency with prior turns.
(5) Context Precision (Ragas) [13] captures retrieval
“cleanliness,” i.e., the proportion of retrieved chunks that are
relevant to the question/ground truth:
Context Precision = |Crel||C| ,
with C the retrieved set and Crel the subset judged relevant.
IV. RESULTS & DISCUSSION
A. Results
This subsection presented comparative experimental results
for two LLMs (Llama vs. Sahabat AI) at baseline and after
RAG integration, as well as the effects of embedding models
(BGE-M3, mContriever, ft-BGE-M3) on core and RAG-
specific metrics. The numerical summary for all configurations
was shown in Table II, while metric distributions were given
in Figure 1. The data design used 70 questions × 10 repe-
titions per configuration (n=700). Evaluation was conducted
with Gemini 2.0 Flash as the LLM backend.
At the baseline without RAG, Sahabat AI outperformed
Llama 3.1 on two core metrics. Sahabat AI achieved a
METEOR of 0.2922 vs. Llama’s 0.2157, while Correctness
was 3.9257 vs. Llama’s 3.1717. Two-sample t-tests on metrics
yielded p ≈ 0 (machine precision), which indicated significant
differences and confirmed that the base LLM quality was the
determinant of QA performance prior to RAG integration.
Once RAG was enabled, universal improvements over the
baseline were observed (p ≈ 0 across comparisons). For
METEOR, Llama’s best configuration was llama-BGE-M3
(0.3228) from a baseline of 0.2157; Sahabat AI peaked with
mContriever (0.4116) from a baseline of 0.2922. For Cor-
rectness, Llama rose to 3.6779 with BGE-M3 (its highest), and
Sahabat AI reached 4.0293 also with BGE-M3 (its highest),
which underscored RAG’s role in boosting factual accuracy.
Embedding comparisons within RAG revealed model-
specific patterns. For Llama, the METEOR ordering was
BGE-M3 > mContriever > ft-BGE-M3, with BGE-M3
vs. mContriever differences being significant (p = 9.53×
10−4). For Sahabat AI, mContriever (METEOR 0.4116;
Correctness 3.9721) vs. BGE-M3 (0.3921; 4.0293) was not
significantly different (METEOR p = 0.1783; Correctness
p = 0.3063), while ft-BGE-M3 was consistently the low-
est on both LLMs. RAG-specific metrics reinforced these
findings: Sahabat AI’s Faithfulness was high and stable (≈
0.9397–0.9452) compared with Llama (≈ 0.8043–0.8796),
with between-LLM tests yielding p ≈ 0; Answer Relevancy
tended to plateau around ≈ 0.6466–0.6588, with many com-
parisons not significant; and Context Precision showed an
LLM×embedding interaction—Sahabat AI+mContriever
was superior (mean ≈ 0.3570), with, for example, Sahabat
AI-BGE-M3 vs. llama-mContriever being significant (p =
6.83 × 10−6).

B. Discussion
Grounded in the quantitative findings, the system’s perfor-
mance exhibited a clear hierarchy of determinants. The base
LLM quality emerged as the dominant factor that set the per-
formance “ceiling”; at the no-RAG baseline, Sahabat AI con-
sistently surpassed Llama 3.1 on METEOR and Correctness,
with statistical tests indicating significant differences (p ≈ 0).
This underscored that selecting a linguistically–culturally lo-
calized base model established a realistic upper bound for
medical question–answering systems.
On this foundation, enabling RAG acted as a second-stage
lever that universally improved fluency and factuality (baseline
vs. RAG: p ≈ 0), which indicated that grounding LLM
outputs in validated external evidence effectively suppressed
hallucinations and strengthened answer traceability. Mechanis-
tically, the consistent rise in Correctness aligned with Sahabat
AI’s higher and more stable Faithfulness relative to Llama,
which suggested more effective use of retrieved context when
evidence was injected into prompts.
Not all metrics were equally sensitive to architectural
variations: Answer Relevancy tended to plateau around ∼
0.65, with many across-configuration comparisons not signifi-
cant, which implied that question-intent design and prompt-
ing schemes might have dominated over LLM or embed-
ding choice for this metric. By contrast, Context Preci-
sion indicated an LLM×embedding interaction; the Sahabat
AI+mContriever combination yielded higher context preci-
sion, which suggested synergy between embedding character-
istics and the model’s approach to formulating and exploiting
retrieval signals. Meanwhile, the domain-tuned ft-BGE-M3
underperformed in this setup, ranking lowest on Faithfulness
and Context Precision, especially when paired with Llama.
V. CONCLUSION
This study established a clear performance determinant
hierarchy: the base LLM set the ceiling, RAG was the primary
lever for improving factuality and traceability, and embed-
ding choice tuned retrieval specificity. In Indonesian-language
CKD education, the localized LLM (Sahabat AI) consistently
surpassed the global model (Llama 3.1) on METEOR and
Correctness. Enabling RAG yielded universal gains over the
baseline, which indicated the effectiveness of grounding in
external evidence to reduce hallucinations. At the component
level, BGE-M3 and mContriever were the most stable
embedding options, while ft-BGE-M3 tended to be infe-
rior under the tested setup. Nevertheless, Answer Relevancy
exhibited a plateau (∼ 0.65), which suggested that inter-
ventions on intent engineering, query rewriting, and explicit
instructions regarding assumptions/constraints/relevance crite-
ria might have been more impactful than simply switching
embeddings.
For implementation in Indonesian healthcare, we recom-
mended adopting a localized model as the foundation and
treating RAG as the default configuration. Practitioners chose
BGE-M3 or mContriever while considering cost–latency
trade-offs, and enforced disciplined knowledge-base gover-
nance (curation, source dating, periodic updates) so that
grounding stayed aligned with the latest clinical guidelines.
Teams operationalized human-in-the-loop mechanisms for am-
biguous cases, added safety guardrails (clinical disclaimers,
risky-intent detection, escalation to clinicians), and moni-
tored quality metrics (Correctness/Faithfulness) alongside op-
erational metrics (latency, per-query cost). To move beyond
the relevancy plateau, efforts focused on clarifying intent,
automatic query rewriting, and explicit instructions regarding
assumptions, limits, and user-expected relevance criteria.
Priority future directions will include broadening scope
beyond hemodialysis to the broader CKD spectrum with multi-
site evaluations across diverse populations; integrating clini-
cian assessments and prospective studies linking chatbot use
to behavior change (diet/medication adherence) and clinical
outcomes; exploring hybrid RAG (distinct embeddings for ini-
tial retrieval vs. reranking) together with intent-understanding
modules to further improve relevance; and strengthening re-
liability via safe personalization (on-premises/federated), un-
certainty estimation, and out-of-distribution detection. Techni-
cally, model distillation/quantization for constrained devices,
multimodal integration (e.g., lab charts, visual education ma-
terials), and scheduled knowledge updates will warrant inves-
tigation to keep the system efficient, safe, and aligned with
evolving guidelines.
