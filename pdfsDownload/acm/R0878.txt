Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has been widely adopted
to enhance Large Language Models (LLMs) by incorporating exter-
nal knowledge, yet its effectiveness in medical question answering
remains underexplored. This study presents a systematic evalu-
ation of RAG systems on the MedQA-USMLE dataset, revealing
counterintuitive findings that challenge the "always-RAG" para-
digm. We evaluated three RAG approaches (BM25, Dense Retrieval,
and Hybrid) against a pure LLM baseline using Qwen3-8B-Instruct
as the primary LLM, with a knowledge base of 125,847 medical
textbook chunks from 18 authoritative sources. Surprisingly, all
RAG methods underperformed the pure LLM baseline (60.69%),
with BM25-RAG, Dense-RAG, and Hybrid-RAG achieving 59.43%,
60.14%, and 59.91% accuracy, respectively. Our analysis reveals that
RAG introduces a dual effect: while correcting 5-6% of questions, it
simultaneously misleads 6-7% of cases, resulting in a net negative
impact of 7-16 questions. Critically, we demonstrate that retrieval
confidence scores fail to predict RAG utility (AUC≈ 0.53, Cohen’s
d = 0.06), indicating current retrieval systems cannot distinguish
helpful from harmful contexts. Oracle analysis suggests a theoreti-
cal upper bound improvement of +6.05% if perfect RAG selection
were achievable, highlighting the urgent need for selective RAG
strategies. These findings challenge the assumption that external
knowledge universally improves medical AI systems and emphasize
the importance of context-aware retrieval mechanisms for clinical
safety.

1 Introduction
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become a dominant
paradigm for enhancing Large Language Models (LLMs) in knowledge-
intensive tasks [1], particularly in high-stakes domains like medicine
where factual accuracy is critical [14]. However, a fundamental ques-
tion remains underexplored: does retrieval augmentation always
help, or can it sometimes harm performance?
While most RAG research focuses on demonstrating benefits [7],
retrieval inherently introduces noise—irrelevant, outdated, or con-
flicting information that may override correct parametric knowl-
edge [15]. Systematic analysis of these negative effects remains
scarce, and whether retrieval scores can reliably predict augmenta-
tion utility has not been rigorously examined.
Our empirical study on MedQA-USMLE reveals surprising find-
ings: all RAG methods (BM25, Dense, Hybrid) underperform the
pure LLM baseline, with retrieval harming 6-7% of questions while
helping only 5-6%. Oracle analysis shows a modest upper bound of
+6.05% even with perfect selection, and retrieval scores fail to dis-
tinguish helpful from harmful cases (AUC=0.53). This paper makes
three contributions:
(1) Systematic quantification of negative effects: We demon-
strate that RAG actively misleads 6-7% of medical questions
where the LLM alone would answer correctly.
(2) Oracle analysis and score failure : We establish a +6.05%
theoretical ceiling and show that retrieval confidence cannot
predict RAG utility (AUC=0.53, Cohen’s d=0.06).

2 Related Work
RAG and Adaptive Retrieval.RAG combines retrieval with gener-
ative models. Lewis et al. [1] first proposed the RAG framework that
retrieves relevant documents and conditions generation on them.
REALM [2] introduced retrieval augmentation during pre-training.
Dense passage retrieval [3] improved retrieval quality using learned
embeddings, with contrastive learning further enhancing unsu-
pervised retrieval [4]. Recent surveys [5] provide comprehensive
overviews of RAG architectures. More recently, Self-RAG [6] en-
ables models to reflect on retrieved content and decide when to use
it. Self-BioRAG [7] extends self-reflection mechanisms to biomed-
ical domains. RAG2 [8] introduces rationale-guided retrieval for
medical QA. AdaptiveRAG [9] learns to adapt retrieval strategies
based on question complexity. However, most work focuses on
demonstrating benefits rather than systematic analysis of negative
effects.
Medical QA and RAG Failures. Medical QA benchmarks in-
clude MedQA [10], PubMedQA [11], and MMLU [12], with MedRAG [13]
establishing RAG-specific evaluation. Studies show retrieval quality
doesn’t always predict performance [15], and retrieved content can
conflict with parametric knowledge [16–18]. Our work provides
the first systematic quantification of RAG’s negative impacts in
medical QA.
3 Methodology
Our framework (Figure 1) compares a pure LLM baseline against

3.1 Dataset and Knowledge Base
We use the MedQA-USMLE validation set [ 10] containing 1,272
four-option multiple-choice questions covering diverse clinical top-
ics including diagnosis, treatment, pathophysiology, and clinical
reasoning. Each question presents a clinical vignette followed by
options (A–D). Following established medical QA evaluation pro-
tocols [13], the model is prompted to output a single letter (A, B,
C, or D) representing the selected answer, enabling direct accuracy
computation and fair comparison with prior work. The knowledge
base comprises 125,847 pre-segmented chunks from the MedRAG
Textbooks corpus [ 13], extracted from 18 authoritative medical
textbooks.

4 Results
4.1 Overall Performance
Table 1 reveals a counterintuitive finding: all RAG methods under-
perform the pure LLM baseline (60.69%). RAG methods range from
59.43% to 60.14%, representing degradations of 0.55–1.26 percent-
age points. While McNemar tests yield p-values of 0.21–0.63 (not
significant at 𝛼=0.05), the consistent negative direction across all
three methods suggests a systematic pattern rather than random
fluctuation.
This trend aligns with RAG2 [8], which reported similar effects:
Llama-3-8B-Instruct dropped from 57.7% to 55.3% with MedCPT
retrieval, and GPT-3.5 (5-shot) decreased from 53.6% to 51.0%. The
reproducibility across different retrieval methods, model architec-
tures, and research groups indicates this phenomenon reflects a
fundamental challenge in medical RAG rather than methodological
artifacts.

4.3 Retrieval Score Analysis
A critical question for practical RAG deployment is whether re-
trieval confidence scores can predict when augmentation will be
beneficial versus harmful. If such prediction were reliable, we could
implement selective RAG strategies that apply augmentation only
when retrieval quality exceeds certain thresholds. We investigated
this using Hybrid-RAG, comparing RRF scores between 87 negative
impact cases and 77 positive impact cases.

5.1 Root Causes of RAG Performance
Degradation

phrases without broader understanding; knowledge fragmenta-

5.2 Dual Nature and Safety Implications
RAG exhibits a dual nature: it helps 5–6% of questions by filling
knowledge gaps but harms 6–7% by overriding correct parametric
knowledge, yielding net negative impact. Critically, these negative
cases represent instances where the pure LLM would have answered
correctly without retrieval. Validation with Qwen-Plus [19] (85%
baseline→ 83% with RAG) confirms this effect generalizes across
model scales and is not limited to open-source models. For Qwen-
Plus, we re-run the same MedQA-USMLE validation set (1,272 ques-
tions) with identical prompts and retrieval settings.
The 6–7% negative impact rate raises serious concerns for clin-
ical deployment. While this percentage may appear modest, its
significance must be evaluated in the context of medical practice
where even small error rates can affect patient safety and clini-
cal decision-making. In high-volume clinical AI applications, this
rate would translate to a substantial number of potentially harmful
recommendations.
6 Conclusion
This study provides systematic evidence that RAG can harm medical
QA performance. All three RAG methods (BM25, Dense, Hybrid)
underperform the pure LLM baseline on MedQA-USMLE, with 5–
6% positive impacts outweighed by 6–7% negative impacts. Oracle
analysis shows only +6.05% maximum improvement with perfect
selection, and retrieval scores fail to predict utility (AUC=0.53).
Our findings demonstrate that optimizing when to apply RAG
is insufficient—the modest Oracle ceiling suggests fundamental
improvements in retrieval quality and knowledge integration are
needed. The failure of confidence scores to distinguish helpful from
harmful cases undermines standard quality control assumptions
and raises safety concerns for clinical deployment. These results
hold under our MedQA-USMLE validation setting and textbook-
based knowledge corpus; generalization to other tasks or corpora
may vary.
Limitations. Our study has four limitations: (1) single dataset
(MedQA-USMLE); (2) single knowledge base (textbooks only); (3)
primary model Qwen3-8B-Instruct, with limited Qwen-Plus valida-
tion; (4) single-run evaluation without multiple seeds.
We advocate shifting from “always-RAG” to selective, context-
aware strategies. Future work should focus on: (1) developing re-
liable utility prediction mechanisms beyond retrieval scores; (2)
designing context-aware retrieval that recognizes clinical nuances
and acute vs. chronic distinctions; (3) implementing robust quality
control that can identify potentially harmful retrievals before they
affect outputs; and (4) exploring hybrid approaches that leverage
RAG’s benefits while mitigating its risks. In safety-critical medical
AI, patient safety must take precedence over performance metrics.
