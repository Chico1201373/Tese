Abstract—This paper presents Diabot, a specialized conversa-
tional chatbot designed to assist individuals managing diabetes.
The development of Diabot addresses a critical need for accurate,
accessible, and empathetic diabetes information in a digital
format. Three approaches were explored to enhance the chatbot’s
capabilities: prompt engineering, fine-tuning of Large Language
Models (LLMs), and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG).
The implementation focuses on contextual filtering, specialized
LLM utilization, and an intuitive user interface. The RAG
approach emerged as the most effective solution, overcoming
hardware limitations and resource constraints while maintaining
response accuracy. The system architecture, implementation
challenges, and future improvements are discussed, including
the integration of calculation capabilities for precise numerical
responses. Diabot demonstrates the potential of specialized con-
versational AI in healthcare, particularly for chronic condition
management like diabetes.

I. INTRODUCTION
Diabetes affects approximately 537 million adults world-
wide, and projections suggest an increase to 783 million by
2045 [14]. In Morocco specifically, the prevalence rate is
estimated at 10.6% among those over 20 years old, translating
to approximately 2.5 million affected individuals, with over
40% unaware of their condition. This growing health concern
necessitates innovative approaches to diabetes education, man-
agement, and support.
The digital transformation of healthcare has accelerated the
development of AI-driven solutions, yet general-purpose chat-
bots often fall short in providing the specialized, accurate
information required for medical domains. When individ-
uals search for health information online, they frequently
encounter misinformation or struggle to verify the reliability of
sources. This can lead to unnecessary healthcare consultations
driven by fear and confusion, or worse, to inappropriate self-
management decisions. In this context, we developed Diabot,
a specialized conversational AI system focused on diabetes
care and management. Unlike general-purpose chatbots trained
on broad datasets, Diabot is designed to provide precise,
contextually relevant information specific to diabetes. The

II. RELATED WORK
The development of healthcare chatbots has moved quickly,
shifting from simple rule-based systems to more advanced AI-
driven tools. A systematic review by Laranjo et al. [1] pointed
out both opportunities and risks: these systems can expand
access to health services, but they also raise questions of safety
and effectiveness.
Later, Montenegro et al. [2] examined in more detail the
difficulties of applying such tools in real healthcare envi-
ronments. They drew attention to both technical constraints
and ethical concerns, insisting that domain-specific knowledge
and context sensitivity are crucial. These reflections strongly
shaped the way specialized healthcare chatbots should be built,
particularly with respect to accuracy and user trust. Within the
diabetes field, a number of solutions have appeared. Winkler
et al. [4], for instance, tested a scaffolding-oriented conversa-
tional agent in online education. Their findings suggested that
structured dialogues can make it easier to understand complex
material.
Cappon et al. [5] also reviewed digital technologies for man-
aging diabetes, paying close attention to continuous glucose
monitoring and its interaction with decision-support mech-
anisms. They concluded that data integration, along with
contextual awareness, is essential for effective interventions.

III. SPECIALIZED VS. GENERALLANGUAGEMODELS
Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-
4, Claude, and LLaMA can generate fluent text and handle
diverse topics with ease. Their training relies on huge and
varied datasets, which explains their versatility. That same
general training, however, limits their precision in highly
technical areas like healthcare.
General-purpose systems frequently stumble over domain-
specific vocabulary and constraints. They may produce an-
swers that sound smooth but are misleading or even wrong. In
medicine, such errors are not trivial—they can affect clinical
decisions or patient self-care. Recent studies have emphasized
the need for domain-specific models in healthcare to ensure
accuracy and relevance in medical contexts.
Choosing between general and specialized models involves
several trade-offs. General-purpose models provide broad cov-
erage, but often at the cost of depth. Specialized models, on
the other hand, tend to be more reliable within their niche but
demand curated datasets, extra training, and suitable infrastruc-
ture. Outside their domain, their performance usually drops.
The decision thus depends on whether the priority is breadth
or depth, and on the resources available for development.
IV. APPROACHES TOLLM SPECIALIZATION
To adapt LLMs for specific medical purposes such as dia-
betes support, three strategies are usually considered: prompt
engineering, fine-tuning, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG).
Prompt engineering.With this method, the model is left
unchanged, and the effort lies in crafting inputs that steer it
toward the intended response. This approach is lightweight in
terms of resources and quick to apply. Yet, its success depends
strongly on the quality of the prompts, and it rarely matches
the depth of more resource-intensive methods.
Fine-tuning.In contrast, fine-tuning changes the model
itself by retraining it on domain-specific examples. This pro-
cess adjusts its internal weights so that it captures medical
knowledge more accurately. The outcome is usually more
precise, but the costs are high: specialized datasets, computing
power, and careful monitoring of biases are all necessary.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation.RAG combines two
elements: retrieval from an external knowledge source and
generative processing by the model. The query first retrieves
relevant passages, and then the model uses them to generate
its answer. This keeps the conversation natural but grounds it
in reliable data. Because the knowledge base can be updated,
RAG provides a way to stay current without full retraining.
Each of these methods carries its own balance of effort,
accuracy, and cost. The “right” choice depends largely on
the available resources, the intended scope of the healthcare
application, and the trade-off the designers are willing to make
between speed and specialization.
V. SYSTEMARCHITECTURE
The architecture of Diabot was designed in layers, each
with a distinct purpose. At the front end lies acontextual
filter, which acts like a gatekeeper: only questions related to
diabetes are allowed through. This filtering step is vital to
keep the system focused and avoid off-topic answers. Behind
it sits thecore engine, where the actual reasoning takes place.
Depending on configuration, it can rely on prompt engineering,
fine-tuning, or RAG to produce responses. On top of this core,
auser interface—inspired by ChatGPT’s simplicity—makes
interaction straightforward, so users can ask questions and
get answers without friction. We also sketched a future en-
hancement: acalculation modulethat would handle numeric
queries with greater accuracy.
In practice, the process is sequential. First, the filter checks
whether a query is truly about diabetes. If yes, it passes the
query to the chosen reasoning approach (prompt engineering,
fine-tuning, or RAG) to generate an appropriate response. The
answer is then displayed through the interface. This modular
structure lets us evaluate and swap approaches without dis-
rupting the overall user experience.
A. Layer 1: Contextual Filtering
We needed a way to make sure the chatbot stayed focused
on diabetes and did not drift into unrelated topics. For this
reason, we built a filtering step using the Groq API. In practice,
it works like a gatekeeper: each incoming question is checked
first, and only those judged relevant to diabetes are passed on.
The filter is guided by a system message that spells out its
role clearly.
The instructions given to the API are simple: it only needs
to decide whether a question is diabetes-related or not, and
then return “true” or “false.” That decision is wrapped into
a JSON object, which also carries a small message back to
the user. When the question is valid, the message confirms
it; when not, the reply gently asks the user to try again with
a diabetes-related query. This approach not only filters out
irrelevant queries but also provides constructive guidance to
users, helping them understand the scope of the chatbot.

B. Layer 2: Core System
For the core system, we explored and evaluated three dis-
tinct approaches: prompt engineering, fine-tuning, and RAG.
Each method offered different advantages and challenges in
developing a specialized diabetes chatbot.
1) Prompt Engineering:The prompt engineering approach
involved designing an effective system prompt that defined the
chatbot’s role, capabilities, limitations, communication style,
and workflow. This method leverages the inherent capabilities
of pre-trained language models without requiring additional
training or specialized resources.
We developed a comprehensive system prompt that speci-
fied the chatbot would serve as a diabetes assistant offer-
ing evidence-based, empathetic advice. The prompt detailed
the chatbot’s capabilities, including comprehensive knowledge
about diabetes (types, symptoms, treatments), personalized
guidance on diet, exercise, and medication, educational re-
sources for understanding complex medical concepts, and
problem-solving support for diabetes management challenges.
To ensure safety, the text reminded users that the chatbot could
not replace professional care. For emergencies, it redirected
people to the right services. Privacy was also underlined: no
personal health information should be stored or shared. We
also set the tone. The chatbot was told to stay empathetic,
speak in plain language rather than jargon, and offer a space
where users could feel supported without judgment. Finally,
the workflow was included: start by assessing the user’s needs,
then give tailored advice, encourage check-ins, and point to
credible resources. Altogether, this prompt worked as a set
of “rules” that guided the model’s responses, even though
the model itself remained unchanged. While this approach
required minimal computational resources and could be im-
plemented quickly, its effectiveness heavily depended on the
quality and specificity of the prompt, as well as the capabilities
of the underlying language model.
2) Fine-Tuning:In the fine-tuning attempt, we first as-
sembled a general healthcare dataset and reformatted it into
structured pairs of questions and answers. The goal was to
give the model direct exposure to medical-style queries, with
a focus on diabetes.
We selected the LLaMA model for fine-tuning due to its
high accuracy and scalability. LLaMA offers several advan-
tages for specialized applications, including strong perfor-
mance in generating accurate, contextually relevant responses
and efficient handling of a large volume of queries. To
optimize resource usage during the fine-tuning process, we
implemented QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Approximation),
a technique that significantly reduces memory requirements
while maintaining model performance.
The QLoRA implementation included several key compo-
nents. First, we applied 4-bit quantization to the LLaMA-
3 model weights, compressing them to reduce the memory
footprint. Most of the pre-trained model parameters were
frozen post-quantization, preventing updates during fine-tuning
to conserve computational resources. We then added trainable
low-rank adapter layers to capture task-specific knowledge
without significantly increasing the parameter count. Finally,
we implemented gradient-based fine-tuning that updated only
the low-rank adapters, minimizing computational overhead.
However, the fine-tuning process encountered significant chal-
lenges that ultimately prevented its completion. We faced
library conflicts that required specific versions of CUDA and
PyTorch, which we resolved by creating a dedicated virtual
environment with the appropriate dependencies. More criti-
cally, our local hardware proved insufficient for the demands
of training, even with the optimizations provided by QLoRA.
When exploring cloud-based alternatives, we found that the
costs would exceed $50, presenting a financial barrier to
pursuing this approach.
These constraints highlight the practical challenges of im-
plementing fine-tuning for specialized models, particularly in
resource-limited environments. Despite the potential benefits
of fine-tuning for creating highly specialized language models,
the hardware requirements and associated costs can make
this approach inaccessible without substantial infrastructure
investments.
3) Retrieval-Augmented Generation:The RAG approach
combines retrieval and generation techniques to enhance re-
sponse accuracy and relevance. This method addresses some
of the limitations of both prompt engineering and fine-tuning
by enabling the system to access and incorporate specific
information without requiring extensive model modification.
Our RAG implementation consisted of three main com-
ponents. First, we selected Google Gemini as the language
model for generating coherent responses based on retrieved
information. While various LLMs could serve this purpose,
Gemini was chosen for its balance of performance and acces-
sibility. Second, we implemented ChromaDB as our vector
database for storing and retrieving vectorized data. After
testing multiple vector databases including Qdrant, Chroma,
and Pinecone, we selected ChromaDB for its open-source
nature and effective performance in managing vectorized data.
Third, we utilized HuggingFace’s all-MiniLM-L6-v2 for gen-
erating text embeddings, choosing this model after considering
alternatives such as OpenAI’s embedding model, which was
more costly and token-intensive.

The RAG process flow begins by breaking documents
into manageable chunks using RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter.
These chunks are then converted into vector embeddings that
capture their semantic meaning. The embeddings are stored
in ChromaDB, creating a searchable repository of diabetes-
related information. When a user submits a query, it is
also converted into an embedding, and a similarity search
is performed in the vector database to retrieve the most
relevant information chunks. These retrieved chunks are then
provided to the language model, which generates a response
incorporating the specific information while maintaining a
natural, conversational tone.
During testing, the RAG approach demonstrated excellent
effectiveness in extracting accurate information from various
sources, including tables and PDFs, and providing precise,
contextually relevant answers. The ability to incorporate spe-
cific information from reliable sources significantly enhanced
the accuracy and trustworthiness of the responses, making
RAG particularly well-suited for a specialized medical domain
like diabetes care.
C. Layer 3: User Interface
The user interface was designed to be intuitive and accessi-
ble, drawing inspiration from ChatGPT’s successful interface
design. We recognized that a clear, straightforward interface
would be essential for ensuring user engagement and satis-
faction, particularly given the diverse user base of a diabetes-
focused chatbot.
The interface features a minimalist layout with an input
field where users can enter their questions or requests. The
responses are displayed in a dedicated area, presented in a
readable and structured manner to enhance comprehension.
We implemented a filtering mechanism that screens irrelevant
queries, ensuring users receive appropriate guidance when
their questions fall outside the chatbot’s diabetes focus. The
system also automatically generates titles for conversations,
helping users organize and reference their interactions over
time.

ing the vectorized data efficiently. Finally, HuggingFace’s all-

effective similarity searching. This technology stack was care-
fully selected to balance performance requirements with re-

chatbot system without requiring extensive infrastructure or
prohibitive computational resources.

and real-world implementation constraints, providing insights
into the trade-offs between different specialization strategies.

The RAG approach demonstrated effectiveness across multi-

as the most viable solution for the Diabot project. The imple-

generation components to create a cohesive system capable
of accessing domain-specific information while maintaining

VII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented Diabot, a specialized conversational
AI system designed to provide accurate, accessible, and em-
pathetic information about diabetes. Through the exploration
of prompt engineering, fine-tuning, and Retrieval-Augmented
Generation, we identified RAG as the most effective approach
for our specific application, balancing accuracy and resource
requirements.
The development of Diabot addresses a critical need in dia-
betes care, where access to reliable, personalized information
can significantly impact management outcomes. By creating a
specialized chatbot focused exclusively on diabetes, we aimed

to provide a resource that offers more accurate and contextu-
ally appropriate guidance than general-purpose conversational
systems. The chatbot’s ability to understand diabetes-specific

Testing all three methods gave us insight into the trade-offs

Development was not without obstacles—limited hardware

choosing open-source tools and efficient methods, we showed
that practical and specialized AI systems can be built even in
resource-constrained environments. This lesson is relevant for
others aiming to build similar healthcare applications.
