Abstract—Integrating large language models (LLMs) into
academic infrastructure requires rigorous safety mechanisms.
This paper presents a comparative evaluation of two guard
models, Llama Guard 3 (with a Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
adapter) and GPT OSS Safeguard, in the context of securing
an administrative chatbot at Warsaw University of Technology
(WUT), incorporating a custom Topic Restriction category that
enforces domain boundaries. Results reveal a fundamental trade-
off: Llama Guard 3 exhibits high recall and low classification
latency, but at the cost of numerous false positives. GPT OSS
Safeguard achieves higher precision and better threat categoriza-
tion, yet its response time precludes synchronous deployment.
The study also revealed a significant discrepancy between expert
labels and user perception, underscoring the inherent subjectivity
of safety classification.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Conversational systems based on large language models
are being more commonly adopted in higher education as
administrative assistants. Such deployments face the problem
of scope creep: systems designed for administrative queries
are gradually repurposed for tasks they were not intended for
(code generation, homework solving, essay writing), incurring
unjustified costs, risk of hallucinating academic facts, and
vulnerability to jailbreak attacks [1].
These challenges make it necessary to evaluate guardrail
mechanisms capable not only of detecting toxicity, but also
of effectively enforcing domain restrictions. The problem is
further complicated by the language barrier – safety models
are trained primarily on English-language datasets, and their
effectiveness for under-represented languages such as Polish
remains unclear [2].
This paper presents a comparative evaluation of Llama
Guard 3 (open-source model with LoRA fine-tuning) and
GPT OSS Safeguard (zero-shot model with Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) reasoning) in the context of the Warsaw University of
Technology administrative chatbot, incorporating the custom
category S15 (Topic Restriction). The main research questions
concern: (1) domain restriction effectiveness, (2) robustness
against prompt injection attacks, (3) the over-refusal problem,
and (4) the cost-effectiveness of open-source versus com-
mercial models. Among the contributions is one of the first
comparisons of these systems in the context of a strictly
administrative university chatbot with a high-rigor topic re-
striction policy.
The emergence of compound AI systems [3] has led to
the establishment of external guardrail layers as a necessary
component for supervising LLM input and output [4]. Built-
in safety mechanisms based on Reinforcement Learning from
Human Feedback (RLHF) fail due to the conflict between
helpfulness and safety objectives, as well as generalization
errors on unusual input formats [1]. Strict filters in turn
amplify over-refusal, reaching up to 50% for safe queries
containing ambiguous keywords [5]. The most serious threat
vector in academic settings are prompt injection and jailbreak
attacks, in which students deliberately attempt to circumvent
safety filters using techniques ranging from role-play scenarios
to intent obfuscation (Base64 encoding, ciphers) [1].
LLM integration in education also introduces scope creep:
assistants designed for administrative support are often used
for academic tasks, pushing them beyond their intended do-
main [6]. Over-reliance on AI for academic purposes risks
eroding students’ critical thinking skills [7]. Enforcing do-
main restrictions, however, creates tension between utility and
safety [5].
Llama Guard 3 is a generative model (8B) based on the
Llama 3 architecture [8], enabling safety policies to be defined
as textual instructions [9]. It supports 14 threat categories from
the MLCommons benchmark [10] and achieves competitive
results in binary safety classification on Polish-language data
after fine-tuning, though with a potential over-refusal risk [2].
GPT OSS Safeguard (20B) is a reasoning model with a native
Chain-of-Thought mechanism, requiring no fine-tuning, where
the policy is injected into the system prompt [11]. The model
employs an instruction hierarchy, giving priority to system
instructions over user requests [12].

Local models such as Bielik Guard [13] – a compact family of
encoder-based classifiers (RoBERTa) – achieve high accuracy
on Polish text classification (precision 77.65%, false positive
rate (FPR) 0.63%), outperforming large multilingual models in
this domain. However, a rigid classification head supporting
exactly five predefined categories prevents taxonomy changes
without costly retraining, the model by design omits jailbreak
attack detection, and its exclusively Polish-language nature
does not meet the requirements of an academic environment
with a large international student population. For these reasons,
it is necessary to turn to the newer generation of generative
guard models capable of multilingual reasoning and flexible
domain policy adaptation.
This paper constitutes one of the first comparative anal-
yses of Llama Guard 3 and GPT OSS Safeguard in the
context of a strictly administrative university chatbot with
a custom Topic Restriction category. A further contribution
is the creation of a Polish-language ground truth dataset of
1802 samples, collected during a 24-hour red teaming study
involving over 30 Warsaw University of Technology (WUT)
students – a dataset that, due to the lack of publicly available
safety benchmarks for Polish educational chatbots, constitutes
a standalone research contribution. The technical analysis is
complemented by an empirical comparison of expert labels
with subjective user perception, revealing discrepancies that
are difficult to capture through classification metrics alone.
The paper proceeds as follows: methodology (Section II),
evaluation results (Section III), and conclusions (Section IV).
II. METHODOLOGY
Experiments were conducted on a workstation with an
NVIDIA RTX PRO 4500 Blackwell GPU (32 GB VRAM).
Llama Guard 3 8B was fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA) (r= 32,α= 64, dropout0.05,η= 2×10 −4,
3 epochs, effective batch size 16, NF4 quantization during
training, bfloat16 inference) on a custom WUT dataset (∼2800
samples). GPT OSS Safeguard was deployed in few-shot mode
with a dedicated safety policy in the system prompt; the policy
evolved iteratively from an initial zero-shot formulation, as
zero-shot prompting proved insufficient to close attack vectors
identified during red-teaming.
The taxonomy comprised 15 threat categories: S1–S14 from
the MLCommons benchmark [10] plus the custom category
S15 (Topic Restriction), flagging any query unrelated to the
university.
The training set (∼2800 samples, 50/50 SAFE/UNSAFE)
was generated using the PLLuM 8B Instruct model [14] with
a role-assignment strategy – the model assumed the roles of a
security auditor (generating UNSAFE queries for S1–S14), a
red teaming expert (S15), and a WUT student (SAFE queries).
A high sampling temperature (T= 0.95) was applied to ensure
diversity. The synthetic test set comprised 2929 samples with
uniform category representation.
A key contribution of this work is the creation of an
independent ground truth dataset (1802 samples) serving as
the primary test set. Queries were generated directly by over
30 WUT students during a 24-hour red teaming study, in
which participants actively interacted with the Llama Guard 3-
secured chatbot, attempting to trigger misclassifications. Col-
lected queries were manually verified twice by the first author
two weeks apart. Distribution: 58.9% SAFE, 41.1% UNSAFE;
among UNSAFE, S15 (37.3%) and S13 (16.2%) dominated.
Evaluation was conducted at the binary level (Accuracy,
Precision, Recall, F1, FP, FN) and the multiclass level (cat-
egory assignment accuracy). Inter-model agreement and Mc-
Nemar’s test with continuity correction were also computed.

On the synthetic set, both models achieved considerably
higher scores. Llama Guard 3 results (F1=98.1%) should how-
ever be interpreted with caution – the model was fine-tuned
on data generated by the same process as the test set, intro-
ducing a risk of performance overestimation. GPT OSS Safe-
guard shows a smaller synthetic-to-real gap (85.1%→71.7%,
−13.4 pp) than Llama Guard 3 (98.1%→69.4%,−28.7 pp);
this difference should be interpreted carefully, as the few-
shot policy was iteratively refined with awareness of real
query patterns from the red-teaming study, providing a slight
distributional advantage. A key advantage of synthetic data
is complete taxonomy coverage – the set contained uniform
representation of all 15 categories, including S3, S4, and S8,
which due to the academic context did not occur naturally
during red teaming sessions. Synthetic data thus serves as a
necessary complement for rare categories, but cannot replace
evaluation on real-world data. For context, the TF-IDF (char
n-gram) + Logistic Regression baseline trained exclusively on
synthetic data (F1=55.1% on ground truth) confirms that LLM-
based guardrails outperform this classical approach by 14–
17 pp, demonstrating better generalization from synthetic to
real user queries.
Analysis of ground truth errors reveals that Llama Guard 3’s
elevated FPR stems from two dominant patterns: over-
triggering on topic restriction (S15) for short benign inputs
such as greetings, and flagging hate speech (S9) when queries
merely reference discriminatory language in an informational
framing (e.g., “Can racist insults have legal consequences?”,
100 cases). Both models share a systematic blind spot for
authority-impersonation jailbreaks (S13) and academically-
framed adversarial prompts (S2), which ranked among the
most frequently missed threats in both classifiers (LG3: 53 FN,
GPT OSS Safeguard: 59 FN).

the optimal production choice: it operates locally without
dependence on external APIs, and borderline queries could be
delegated to the downstream model rather than unconditionally
blocked. The system should also allow users to report unjus-
tified blocks, providing data for iterative model improvement.
E. User Feedback
In the user study (over 30 WUT students), feedback was
collected on Llama Guard 3 classifications. Students rated
classifications as correct in 62.9% of cases and as incorrect
in 20.9% (16.1% no response). Breakdown by prediction type
reveals an asymmetry: of the 377 Incorrect responses, 238
(63.1%) concerned SAFE predictions and only 139 (36.9%)
concerned UNSAFE predictions. Students more frequently
disputed decisions to allow a query than to block it.
Comparison of expert labels with user perception revealed
agreement of only 64.9% (948/1461). The asymmetry is clear:
465 queries labelled SAFE by experts were rated UNSAFE
by students, while only 48 queries deemed UNSAFE were
considered safe by students. Students systematically labelled
a broader range of content as unsafe, including questions about
alcohol, lecturers, and anti-discrimination topics in academic
context. User perception thus proved more conservative than
expert classification based on a precise taxonomy – implying
that a ground truth dataset built solely on user ratings would
overestimate the scope of threats. Evaluating guard models
solely through technical metrics is insufficient in an educa-
tional context, where user experience and trust in the system
are equally important.
IV. CONCLUSION
This paper presents one of the first comparative evaluations
of LLM guard models in the context of a Polish-language
academic chatbot. Three key conclusions are drawn.
First, the choice of guard model is a trade-off between
safety and utility. Llama Guard 3 minimizes the risk of missing
threats at the cost of excessive blocking; GPT OSS Safeguard
minimizes disruption at the cost of higher risk.
Second, within the experimental context studied, open-
source models showed competitive performance relative to
commercial solutions for securing academic chatbots in under-
represented languages. Llama Guard 3, with full data control
and local deployment, suggests a viable path toward sovereign
deployment in public institutions, though broader validation
across institutions would be needed to generalise this conclu-
sion.
Third, the low agreement between expert labels and user
perception (64.9%) underscores that safety classification is
inherently subjective and requires incorporating the end-user
perspective.
The study has several limitations: the ground truth dataset
comes from a single institution (WUT Faculty of Electrical
Engineering students), categories S3, S4, and S8 did not
appear in the collected data, and the evaluation did not cover
structured adversarial attacks (GCG, AutoDAN). The shared
training and test data distribution of the synthetic set artificially
inflates Llama Guard 3 results on that set (F1=98.1% vs 69.0%
on ground truth); the TF-IDF + LogReg baseline trained on
the same synthetic data reaches F1=55.1% on ground truth,
confirming the value added by the LLM-based approach.
Future work should cover: (1) systematic evaluation of
robustness against structured adversarial attacks (GCG, Au-
toDAN) in a Polish-language context, (2) creation of a pub-
licly available safety benchmark for Polish educational chat-
bots, (3) investigation of cascaded architectures in which a
lightweight binary classifier performs initial filtering and a
reasoning model analyzes only borderline cases, and (4) com-
parison with emerging dedicated Polish guard models such as
Bielik Guard [13].
