Abstract— Cancer is the second leading cause of death 
globally, highlighting the urgent need for innovative solutions to 
support patients and healthcare providers. Remote and digital 
cancer care interventions, such as chatbots, are more relevant 
than ever, offering timely information and personalized 
assistance. With the rise of conversational AI, chatbots have 
emerged as a potential solution to bridge this gap, offering 24/7 
assistance and personalized responses. This paper introduces 
CancerBot, a Retrieva l-Augmented Generation (RAG) system 
designed to address the need for reliable, contextually relevant 
cancer-related information. Built on top of a large language 
model LLaMA -2, and integrated with a vector database, 
CancerBot retrieves relevant medical literature to generate 
accurate responses, minimizing the risk of hallucinations. 
Through synthetic testing and evaluation using metrics such as 
faithfulness, context relevancy, and response relevancy, our 
results demonstrate that the RAG -based CancerBot 
significantly outperforms the standard LLaMA -2 model in 
addressing cancer -specific queries. This advancement 
showcases the potential of RAG systems to improve patient care 
by providing reliable, context-driven information that enhances 
patient engagement and healthcare decision-making. 
Keywords— Cancer, Chatbot, Llama2, LLM, RAG, Vector 
Databases  
I. INTRODUCTION  
The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that 
cancer is the second leading cause of death globally. In 2022, 
there were approximately 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 
million cancer -related deaths worldwide. By 2040, these 
numbers are expected to rise significantly, with an estimated 
29.9 million new cases and 15.3 million deaths annually [1]. 
This growing burden emphasizes the need for innovative 
solutions to enhance support for patients, caregivers, and 
healthcare providers. Timely diagnosis and optimal treatment 
are essential for improving cancer survival rates. 
Consequently, there is a rising demand for an effective 
healthcare system to address the complex treatment and care 
needs of cancer patients. Many patients experience challenges 
related to physical, emotiona l, and practical concerns, yet 
often struggle to find adequate information to manage these 
issues [2]. Furthermore, misinformation about cancer is 
widespread on online platforms such as Google and YouTube, 
exacerbating these challenges [3]. 
AI-driven chatbots have gained increasing importance by 
offering 24/7 assistance and personalized information to 
patients navigating cancer care [4], [5]. These tools can 
enhance patient engagement, provide emotional support, and 
help alleviate the workloa d of healthcare professionals, 
offering a more robust and accessible approach to cancer care 
[6]. Conversational AI chatbots have demonstrated the 
potential to provide patients with responses that are as 
accurate, empathetic, and readable as those provided  by 
healthcare professionals [7]. Expert AI chatbots trained on 
extensive medical text corpora can serve as point -of-care 
electronic tools, offering essential information to vulnerable 
populations while also providing emotional support to cancer 
patients, thus improving oncology care. Additionally, chatbots 
have been increasingly used in applications such as cancer 
screening, prevention, risk stratification, treatment, 
monitoring, and management. Studies have shown high user 
satisfaction with these tools, as they enhance patient-centered 
communication, make cancer -related information more 
accessible, and improve access to care [7]. 
The rise of large language models (LLMs) has further 
advanced the capabilities of chatbots, enabling them to 
understand and generate human-like responses [7]. LLMs are 
a subset of artificial intelligence that relies on deep learning 
techniques, particularly transformers, to process and generate 
human language. These models are trained on massive 
datasets that include text from books, research papers, 
websites, and other textual resources. By leveraging this vast 
corpus of information, LLMs can predict and g enerate 
coherent, contextually relevant responses to a wide variety of 
inputs. LLMs like GPT -4 [8] and others operate through 
billions of parameters, which help them to capture nuances in 
language, including grammar, context, and meaning. When a 
user inputs a question or statement, the model analyzes the 
sequence of words and predicts the next word or phrase, 
building an informed response that often mirrors human 
conversation. These advancements in natural language 
processing (NLP) have significantly improv ed the 
effectiveness of chatbots in healthcare, enabling them to 
engage in more meaningful and empathetic interactions. 
However, LLMs face significant challenges, including 
knowledge cutoffs and the potential for generating 
hallucinations that are incorrect or misleading [9]. These 
limitations can undermine trust and hinder the effectiveness of 
chatbots in delivering reliable information, especially in 
critical areas like oncology. To overcome these challenges, 
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (R AG) models offer a 
promising solution. By integrating sophisticated retrieval 
mechanisms with the generative capabilities of LLMs, RA G 
ensures that chatbot responses are both accurate and 
contextually relevant. This approach not only mitigates the 
risk of hallucinations but also bridges the gap created by 
knowledge cutoffs, providing access to the latest research and

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
The AI chatbot has immensely revolutionized the health 
sector, particularly in oncology. These digital tools seek to 
offer timely information and support to patients and increase 
their understanding of cancer -related issues. There is a 
narrative review by Piau et al. (2023) citing that chatbots have 
been used in many applications such as cancer screening and 
prevention, risk stratification, treatment management, and 
monitoring. Out of the 21 assessed chatbots, the majority of 
them indicated high patient satisfaction and positively 
impacted the care's efficiency compared to conventional 
practices. In particular, 9 of the 12 studies mentioned that the 
outcome of oncological care was positive through the use of 
chatbots [10]. Nevertheless, AI chatbots have some 
limitations. Some of the major r estrictions of the chatbots 
based on AI include dependence on pre-programmed response 
patterns and lack of substantial contextual perceptions. This 
often results in too technical terms that will only confuse 
patients rather than help them. A review of the effectiveness 
of several AI chatbots on the quality of their response reported 
that although they had correctly provided information on the 
most common cancers, many of the responses were 
unintelligible by laypersons. In addition, such chatbots are 
vulnerable to inaccuracy in their training data which could be 
outdated or even biased, leading to providing the wrong 
information that has adverse effects on patient care [11], [12]. 
Advanced natural language processing, like more 
improved chatbots, paved the way to finally understanding 
and even responding to the demands of a patient. NLP-based 
chatbots possess very promising positions over simple AI 
systems in the sense that they allow for very personalized 
interaction. For instance, performance and comparison 
evaluation of three NLP -based chatbots were co nducted 
against licensed physicians, and the chatbots were concluded 
to have outperformed human specialists in empathy and 
readability when answering questions related to cancer [13]. 
The result indicates that the mean empathy scores of the best-
performing chatbot were significantly higher than those of 
human physicians, which may indicate that NLP improves 
patients' engagement and sa tisfaction. Despite  this, NLP 
models have their very own limitations. Although they 
improve the quality of interaction, they tend to generate vague 
or ambiguous responses, which might confuse users. 
Additionally, research studies have indicated that system s 
based on NLP often make incorrect recommendations or are 
contrary to clinical guidelines. For example, one study found 
that a third of treatment recommendations from an NLP 
chatbot did not follow NCCN guidelines [4]. This 
inconsistency indicates that the se models need to be 
continuously validated and updated to ensure they stay 
reliable. 
The second major landmark in chatbot technology applied 
to health is Large Language Models. They are LLMs, the likes 
of ChatGPT, which can give meaningful coherent responses 
in some context to vast datasets. Because they can imitate 
human speech, they tend to engage patients more than the 
traditional model would. However, while LLMs can pump out 
abundant detail quickly, new problems were also introduced. 
Indeed, it has been documented that LLMs can produce 
"hallucinations," which involve generating entirely incorrect 
information or recommending interventions not supported by 
clinical guidelines; this happened in approximately 12.5% of 
cases during evaluations[14]. Further, LLMs may have 
difficulties handling nuanced medical decision -making 
because of their generalized training data. 
After LLMs, there is an increasing interest in Retrieval -
Augmented Generation models for applications in healthcare, 
since these models seemingly address some of the limitations 
of Large Language Models (LLMs). It's a combination of 
traditional Information Retrieval and generative capabilities 
that empower chatbots to give accurate, timely responses with 
real-time integration of verified data from medical databases 
[13]. The particular approach has been known to reduce 
diagnostic errors in health care by up  to significant numbers, 
thus providing more precise treatment recommendations and 
outcomes for the patients [15]. For example, some recent 
implementations, as witnessed by the use of technology RAG 
by Apollo 24\7 in confluence with Google's MedPaLM [16], 
came to prove that RAG does its job effectively in bringing 
clinicians in real -time access to de -identified patient data as 
well as new medical research [13]. Of course, it's all about the 
models of RAG that can automate various administrative tasks 
such as scheduling patient visits, which made their operational 
efficiency better. With the synthesis of the relevant 
information from vast medical databases, RAG deepens the 
understanding of the patients as well as facilitates their 
cooperation-the ideal chain of communication between health 
care providers and patients [15]. As RAG technology evolves, 
it will change the medical world map in accessing and using 
medical information for the betterment of both patient care 
and operational workflows [17]. 
While substantial advancements have been made in 
elevating cancer care through enhanced patient engagement 
and access using AI -powered chatbots, concerns about their 
accuracy and reliability remain paramount. The transition 
from basic AI systems, then to NLP -based models, and now 
LLMs indicates unwavering attempts to strengthen the 
effectiveness of these tools in health care. However, there is 
still further work that needs to be done to overcome the current 
limitations of such technologies so they can be used accurately 
by patients searching for information about cancer. 
III. METHODOLOGY 
A. Data Collection, Chunking and Embeddings 
The process for gathering data for CancerBot, a cancer -
specific Retrieval -Augmented Generation system, was 
exhaustive and included drawing information from both 
medical and academic sources. Using an API key, we 
searched PubMed for papers with "cancer" as the main 
keyword. This led to the downloading of 100 full -text PDFs 
covering a wide range of topics, including cancer diagnosis, 
treatment strategies, new therapy advancements, and ongoing 
clinical trials. Apart from these papers, we obtained seven 
books from a Google search on the topic of cancer that dealt 
with treatment protocols, preventive measures, patient care, 
and educational resources for both patients and healthcare 
providers. The inclusion of book materials with the journal

After collecting the data, the next phase involved

text coherent.  Then, the sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-

Embeddings allow the model to find contextually similar 
information, thus providing more accurate and meaningful 
responses. This is particularly important in the medical 
domain, where scientific literature is often dense, and 
understanding the context behind specific terms or treatments 
can drastically affect the quality of the information provided. 
 
The sentence  Transformer [18] model is well -known for 
generating high -quality, context -aware embeddings, which 
allowed CancerBot to retrieve the most relevant sections of 
the text based on user queries. This model is powered by pre-
trained nuanced relationships between words, phrases , and 
sentences. These conditions make CancerBot achieve its real-
time accuracy with answer -providing performance within 
areas like cancer treatment, research, and patient education. 
B. Vector Database 
Pinecone Vector Database 3 Was employed to efficiently 
manage the storage and retrieval of embeddings within the 
CancerBot system (Fig. 1). Pinecone is perfectly suitable for 
our use cases since it allows quick similarity search across 
large datasets. This follows the architecture of CancerBot 
wherein the embeddings are vital for associating user queries 
with pieces of cancer literature. 
 
For the database configuration, an index in Pinecone with 
a dimensionality of 384  was created , reflecting the 
dimensions of the embeddings generated by the `sentence -
transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2` model [19]. Cosine 
similarity as the metric for this index which can be calculated 
as in (1), was employed . Cosine similarity is the effective 
measure of the angular distance between vectors and hence is 
the ideal measure that should be applied for text embedding 
semantic content comparison.  The initialization p rocess 
included checking whether the index existed and, if not, then 
it created it. The system then monitored the index until it was 
fully initialized and ready for use.

C. Large Language Model for Generation 
This study uses the Llama -2-13b model which is 
part of the Llama-2 [20] family of pre -trained generative 
text models created by Meta and released in July 2023. 
They use an auto -regressive approach - which 
sequentially generates text based on the context provided 
to the model. We use the Llama -2-13b model since it is 
capable of handling complex linguistics since it has been 
pre-trained on a vast corpus of about 2 trillion tokens of 
text data and supports longer context lengths of up to 4096 
tokens. For dialogue cases, the model has been fine-tuned 
in a supervised manner using labeled data. The model is a 
great resource to use for the use case of this study since it 
is built to balance both helpfulness and safety in its 
responses. In most cases, the Llama -2-13b model has 
been found to outperform most of its closed -source 
competitors such as ChatGPT [20]. 
 
The model is built on the powerful transformer 
Architecture model. The transformer model was 
introduced in 2017 and relies on self-attention techniques 
to capture contextual information from the input text. It 
consists of an encoder -decoder structure, wher e the 
encoder processes input tokens and the decoder generates 
output tokens. The multi -head attention mechanism 
allows the model to attend to different parts of the input 
sequence simultaneously, enhancing its ability to learn 
complex patterns [20].  
 
The LLaMA -2 13B model was optimized for 
deployment on limited -memory hardware using 
quantization techniques. To minimize model size, 
quantization converts high -precision weights and 
activations into lower -precision formats retaining 
performance. By impleme nting this strategy, large 
models can be effectively run on GPUs with limited 
memory, expanding their accessibility for various 
applications with reduced computational requirements. 
The model was loaded with 4 -bit precision to prioritize 
both memory efficiency and computational accuracy. The 
chosen quantization method maximizes performance with 
minimal effect on model accuracy.

D. RAG-based Response Generation 
The retrieval -augmented generation improves the 
correctness of responses by combining large language 
models with external data sources. The RAG draws real -
time factual data into its context window without having 
to retrain the LLM for efficient responses. It accesses this 
database after receiving an inquiry to gather the necessary 
context for the LLM's limited knowledge. After that, the 
LLM generates a reply based on current data. This method 
goes beyond the LLM as it provides context -specific 
resolutions. 
The Knowledge Base of the cancer chatbot will be 
enriched using RAG to extract information from related 
literature. Retrieving relevant parts from the vector 
database deepens the context of responses to user 
inquiries. RAG equips the chatbot with real-time medical 
knowledge. RAG allows the chatbot to receive real -time 
updates, eliminating the need for costly LLM retraining. 
With new cancer data, the vector database effortlessly 
expands, improving the chatbot's cancer -related 
knowledge. By employing RAG appr oaches, we harness 
the reasoning ability and factual knowledge of a large 
language model, which is relationally stored and 
dynamic. Due to the synergy between the chatbot and the 
knowledge base, it delivers more elaborate responses 
based on current information. RAG is crucial for enabling 
the chatbot to store accurate and contextually relevant 
cancer information. 
IV. RESULTS 
In this study, Gradio was utilized to develop a web-based 
application interface, hosted locally, for interacting with an 
RAG system that combines large language models LLMs with 
a vector database. The design of the interface created using 
Gradio is presented in Fig . 2, which illustrates how users 
interact with the system. We employed this interface to ask a 
series of questions to the LLM -based system, focusing on 
cancer-related queries. The system's responses were generated 
based on relevant context extracted through a similarity search 
from a vector database that had been populated with cancer -
related documents, research articles, and medical literature.  
The system generated answers that were accurate and 
rooted in the provided dataset by retrieving the most 
contextually relevant information. Fig. 3 shows how this 
process works, presenting both the questions posed to the 
system and the corresponding responses based on the retrieved 
context. Several questions from research papers and medical 
documents were selected manually and used to assess the 
system's performance. Among all questions, one was the 
question "What is the Patient Experience of Radiation 
Therapy for Cancer Treatment?" Here, since this model pulled 
out context-relevant information from the database, therefore 
it came up with correct response as demonstrated in Fig. 3(a). 
Now it confirmed the model capability of retrieving 
contextually relevant information relating to oxidative 
stress—the vital component of the disease and also could give 
thoughtful response for the given context.

In another example, the question, "How does 
occupational exposure to pesticides contribute to oxidative 
stress in cancer patients?" was posed (Figure 3 (b)). An 
additional instruction was given to the system to answer 
based strictly on the retrieved context and, if no relevant 
information was found, to reply, "I don’t know." This 
instruction was implemented to mitigate the risk of 
hallucinations, an issue common in LLMs where they 
generate false or misleading information. Fig ure 4 illustrates 
how the system effectively handled this instruction, providing 
accurate responses based only on the available data, thereby 
preventing hallucinations. To test it, a control question that 
was unrelated to the cancer context was asked: "Who  is 
Barack Obama?" Since the vector database did not contain 
any information related to Barack Obama, the system 
correctly responded with “ can not provide information about 
him”. This highlights the effectiveness of the RAG system in 
adhering to the provi ded context and only generating 
responses when relevant information is available. 
After building and manually testing the RAG pipelines, we 
created a synthetic testing dataset along with ground truth 
using GPT -4. This dataset was carefully structured with 
headers like question, answer, context, and ground truth to 
ensure a thorough eval uation. To assess the performance of 
the RAG system, we used the Retrieval -Augmented 
Generation Assessments (RAGAs) [21]. The evaluation 
focused on several key metrics: faithfulness, context 
relevancy, and response relevancy. Faithfulness ensures that 
the response generated is contextual to prevent hallucinations 
and to provide factual consistency, whereas relevancy of the 
response addresses how well the answer has been generated 
about a specific question. Context Relevance determines 
whether or not the retrieved context is concise and irrelevant-
free to maximize LLM's performance. 
We tested both the LLaMA -2 model and the RAG -
enhanced LLaMA-2 model by asking them the same set of 
cancer-related questions. The results, shown in Table 1, 
clearly demonstrate that the CancerBot, powered by RAG, 
performed significantly better than the standard LLaMA -2 
13B model when handling these cancer-specific queries. 
This shows that the CancerBot’s ability to pull relevant 
information and provide accurate, contextually rich answers is 
much stronger due to the RAG system, especially in the 
complex and sensitive field of healthcare.

However, despite its brilliant prospects, there are a couple of 
scopes where improvement is needed in order to take it to the 
next level. Currently, it relies on cosine similarity for 
retrieving relevant information. Although this works very well 
in most cases, this methodology might not be useful in some 
instances in bringing the outcomes closest to perfection or 
even contextual accuracy [22]. The size of the corpus that it is 
currently using is also pretty small. Though adequate for its 
current scope, it limits the range of cancers and medical 
concerns it can address entirely. 
V. CONCLSUION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 
The development and evaluation of the RAG -based 
CancerBot highlight important progress in how AI can help 
retrieve and generate accurate, contextually relevant 
information for cancer-related questions. CancerBot became a 
better model combined with a vector database as opposed to 
the standard LLaMA -2 model. Answers became clearer and 
more accurate, bringing down the possibility of spreading 
misinformation. Additionally, the use of Gradio for the 
interface made the system more accessible, offering a user -
friendly and intuitive way for people to interact with the tool. 
Future development would be to expand the corpus to address 
more diverse kinds of cancers and even more medical 
concerns. A further step would be to consider better retrieval 
methods than cosine similarities to extend relevance and 
precision in searching information. We will also experiment 
with different open-source models and embedding techniques 
in our attempt to make the system even more adaptive and 
efficient. Another interesting possibility would be its 
integration into wearable technology like smart glasses, which 
makes it more portable and easy to access.  In conclusion, this 
paper demonstrates the possibilities of Retrieval -Augmented 
Generation systems in truly impacting health care by 
producing reliable information that is based on actual 
evidence.
