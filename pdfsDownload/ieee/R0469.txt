Abstract—Large language models (LLMs) have great potential
for educational tutoring, but hallucinations, poor curriculum
alignment, and limited access to domain specific knowledge
limit their application in academic settings. Retrieval augmented
generation (RAG) is one way to overcome these shortcomings
by anchoring model outputs to external sources. Still, most
educational RAG systems are limited to single turn question
answering and offer little pedagogical assistance. The paper will
provide the design and assessment of the RAG enhanced LLM
chatbot in Socratic tutoring and adaptive quiz generation at
Arkansas Tech University (ATU). The system employs a hybrid
retrieval pipeline with FAISS, BM25, and cross encoder re
ranking on a knowledge base of 2,328 chunks from 17 open
educational resources textbooks, as well as a retriever fine tuned
on 929 domain specific query passage pairs. The Qwen3 8B model
supports both tutoring and assessment. In tutoring mode, the
system generates grounded explanations and Socratic follow up
questions; in assessment mode, it generates curriculum aligned
multiple choice and short answer questions with immediate feed-
back. The assessment of 14 questions demonstrated strong results
in relevance, conciseness, contextual grounding, and resistance
to hallucinations, with overall heuristic and LLM scores of
4.00/5. Lower scores in empathy and Socratic scaffolding indicate
areas for future improvement. The findings show the potential
of retrieval based tutoring and self adaptive assessment in a
university specific curriculum.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) have become a growing
focus in education due to their ability to generate natural
language responses, interactively answer questions, and pro-
vide individualized learning experiences [1], [2], [3]. In post
secondary education, these abilities enable scalable tutoring
mechanisms that help students access on demand academic
assistance outside the classroom, particularly in environments
where individual teacher care is limited [1], [2]. More recent
research has demonstrated growing concern about conversa-
tional AI systems capable of explaining concepts, answering
follow up questions, and supporting inquiry based dialogue in
realistic academic settings [2]. This tendency is a symptom of a
broader shift in the model of content delivery, from a one way
approach to incorporating more interactive, student centered
education with the help of AI [4]. Despite this potential,
general purpose LLMs are inadequate as academic tutors and
cannot be trusted to be reliable and safe when not grounded
or maintained under pedagogical control [3]. They can include
hallucinated, misleading, or factually inaccurate information
in their output, particularly when accuracy, credibility, and
curriculum coherence are needed [4]. Moreover, these models
typically rely solely on static pretraining data. They thus may
not fully capture the institution specific course material, the
goals of the current instruction, or the terminology used in a
given discipline [1]. Consequently, efficient academic tutoring
systems should not simply produce plausible answers but
also provide systematic, credible, and pedagogically significant
assistance that guides students in accumulating knowledge
step by step [5], [6], [7]. To overcome these constraints,
RAG offers a pragmatic approach by leveraging model outputs
based on curated external sources of knowledge, including
textbooks, teaching resources, and academic documents [1].
The RAG based system retrieves passages of a document
collection that are relevant to the query but are not based on
parametric knowledge, and makes these passages available as
contextual evidence as the response is generated. This design
helps increase grounding in fact, reduces the possibility of
hallucination, and enables the inclusion of domain specific
information without retraining a whole language model [3].
These properties are especially valuable in education, as they

allow a tutor’s response to align with authoritative learning

supporting the pedagogical interaction required to drive real

directed inquiries, comments on incomplete knowledge, and
opportunities for self evaluation [6]. Specifically, the Socratic

and actively engage with the content rather than passively
accept the answer [7]. Based on these considerations, one
can notice a research gap: educational systems that inte-

gap in quiz based learning is very important. Assessment
is not only applicable to grading but also to strengthening
comprehension, uncovering misunderstandings, and enabling
learners to check their own development [5]. Multiple choice
and short answer questions, when aligned with instructional
material and followed by immediate feedback, may facilitate
retrieval practice, reinforce understanding, and enhance self
regulated learning [7]. However, in most AI learning systems,
tutoring and assessment remain distinct, even though they
are inherently complementary in actual educational practice
[5], [4]. The ability to explain content and generate grounded
and curriculum relevant quizzes, therefore, provides students
with a comprehensive and educationally significant learning
workflow through a tutoring system [8]. To mitigate these
shortcomings, this paper introduces a RAG enhanced LLM
chatbot that can serve A TU students and facilitate Socratic
tutoring and adaptive quiz generation. The system utilizes
a knowledge based retrieval pipeline consisting of dense
semantic retrieval, sparse lexical retrieval, and neural reranking
to enhance evidence selection and domain alignment, based
on a body of knowledge comprising 17 open educational re-
source textbooks [9]. The assessment and the tutoring module
retrieve content relevant to producing grounded explanations
and scaffolding questions to promote active reasoning [10],
[11] and the assessment module produces multiple choice and
short answer quizzes grounded in feedback and aligned with
the curriculum [12], [13]. The proposed system is designed
exclusively for tutoring and formative assessment and does
not require the collection of sensitive personal information.
When deployed locally or in a controlled environment, user
queries do not have to be exposed to external internet services
and are thus less prone to privacy and data leakage. The
proposed system merges grounded retrieval, conversational
tutoring, Socratic scaffolding, and adaptive assessment within
a university congruent architecture as opposed to generic
educational chatbots [14] , [12]. It is a design that goes beyond
mere academic question answering to a better grounded, more
pedagogically organized form of AI assisted learning in higher
education [15], [16].

Recent educational tutoring systems are progressively inte-
grating retrieval augmented generation (RAG) with large lan-
guage models (LLM) to enhance factual grounds, instructional
relevance, and learner adaptation. LPITutor reflects this trend,
combining LLMs, RAG, and prompt engineering to generate
tailored tutoring responses based on learner proficiency and
question difficulty [17]. Likewise, RoboTuB demonstrates that
domain specific learning can be facilitated through retrieval
grounded tutoring, which creates multi step STEM problems
using structured explanations, simulations, and code examples
[18]. Nevertheless, such systems primarily focus on individ-
ualized support and responsive material presentation, at the
expense of direct Socratic conversation or built in formative
evaluation. Another parallel line of research concerns Socratic
and dialogic tutoring. A notebookLM is a Socratic physics
tutor in which retrieval grounded, source based interaction is
employed to provide students with question based learning,
demonstrating that RAG can facilitate more traceable and ped-
agogically organised tutoring [19]. The Socratic AI for clinical
case based learning also uses an adaptive strategy similar to
the LLM based tutoring approach, based on guided reasoning,
real time feedback, and systematic assessment of learning
strengths and weaknesses [20]. The studies are closely related
to our system’s tutor system, but they are usually area specific,
lack shared context, broad academic corpus grounding, and
a separate assessment generation component. Regarding the
assessment side, it has previously been demonstrated that RAG
and LLM stimulation are applicable for producing adaptive
quizzes with high topical and contextual relevance [21]. Other
researchers also show that course aligned retrieval and domain
adaptation can enhance the quality of tutoring in curriculum
specific environments [22]. Though this has been done, it can
still be said that the current work remains disjointed among the
individualized RAG tutoring, Socratic instructional dialogue,
and the generation of quizzes, which are mostly distinct. On
the other hand, our system combines a hybrid retrieval pipeline
for course relevant textbooks, a retrieval grounded Socratic
tutoring module, and an agentic quiz generation workflow into
a single university centric architecture for guided learning and
formative assessment.

B. Knowledge Base Construction
The knowledge base was constructed from 17 Open Edu-
cational Resources (OER) textbooks retrieved from Arkansas
Tech University’s Online Research Commons across a variety
of academic subjects. All the books were gathered as PDFs
and processed page by page with PyPDF. Removal of page
numbers, text page breaks, and page whitespace; normalization
of Unicode characters; and repair of hyphenation were all
done to extract text and cleanse it. The cleaned corpus was
then segmented using a sliding window chunking method with
chunks of about 900 tokens and a 140 token overlap; very short
chunks were discarded. This fixed length overlapping design
was used as a practical baseline for long document retrieval,
as has been done before and in line with previous studies
demonstrating that chunking quality and overlap affect long
document retrieval performance [23]. A chunk was assigned
a SHA 1 identifier and stored with metadata, including title,
source path, page range, and an approximate token count, to
support reproducible provenance and indexing [24] [25]. The
pipeline created 2,328 indexed chunks, stored in JSONL for-
mat for downstream access. The system specific information
below corresponds to the uploaded methodology description.
C. Hybrid Retrieval Pipeline
A hybrid retrieval pipeline has been designed to maximize
the relevance and coverage of the context provided to the
LLM. The pipeline combines three retrieval mechanisms that
are complementary, each more effective in its own right: dense
semantic retrieval, sparse lexical retrieval, and neural cross
encoder re ranking [26]–[28]. Dense V ector Retrieval: Each
chunk in the knowledge base was represented as a 384 dimen-
sional dense vector using the Sentence Transformer model, all
MiniLM L6 v2. The embeddings were L2 normalized and
indexed with a FAISS IndexFlatIP inner product index to
perform efficient similarity search on the corpus [29]. Sparse
Lexical Retrieval: In parallel, a BM25Okapi index was built
over the tokenized corpus. Tokenization included lowercasing,
removing invalid characters, and removing tokens shorter than
3 characters. This component captures exact lexical matches
for domain specific terminology that may not be fully pre-
served in dense embedding space [26], [28]. Score Fusion: For
each incoming query, dense retrieval and BM25 retrieval were
performed separately, and each returned the top 50 candidate
chunks. The two ranked lists were fused (weighted linear
combination with weights of 0.6 for dense retrieval scores
and 0.4 for BM 25 scores).These weights were chosen as
an intuitive heuristic to give greater weight to semantic simi-
larity and keep lexical matching for course-specific terms. A
complete ablation study of different dense and sparse retrieval
weights remains to be done. A soft boilerplate filter was used
for the front matter (license page, table of contents, citation
packed sections, etc.), and a minimum of 12 candidates were
retained to avoid retrieval failure in edge cases [28]. Cross
Encoder Reranking: The fused candidate set was reranked
using the cross encoder/ms marco MiniLM L6 v2 model,
which jointly encodes each query passage pair to obtain fine
grained relevance scores. The five best passages/propositions
re ranked were used as the last retrieval context for the
generation module [27], [30]. This three stage retrieve, fuse,

within the same textbook. This process produced 929 query

E. Large Language Model Configuration and Inference Con-
trols
The generative aspect of the system is grounded in Qwen3
8B, an 8 billion parameter language model. To allow deploy-
ment on a single Nvidia Tesla T4 GPU with 16GB of VRAM,
the model was loaded with the 4 bit NormalFloat (NF4)
quantization using the BitsAndBytes framework, switching to
double quantization and a computation data type of float16.
This configuration significantly reduced memory usage while
maintaining very good generation quality for tutoring and quiz
generation applications [33], [34]. To improve response quality
during inference, a lightweight output control pipeline was
employed. Internal reasoning traces and meta commentary
were removed using pattern based filtering. In addition, to
detect outputs with unusually low alphabetic character content,
a simple corruption detection heuristic was used to provide a
fallback response when needed. Finally, the pad token was tied
to the end of sequence token to allow for stable generation
behavior [34], [35].
F . Socratic Tutoring Module
The Socratic tutoring module is the main pedagogical
interface of the proposed system. It focuses on supporting
active learning through very short, retrieval based explanations
and a selected follow up question. The LLM was prompted
with a structured prompt that elicited a two part response:
EXPLAIN, which provides a short answer using only the
retrieved notes, and QUESTION, which includes one Socratic
follow up question. The prompt precluded outside knowledge,
code generation, and vague questioning, and a lack of suffi-
cient evidence triggered a controlled fall back response [36],
[37]. To make the system more reliable, topic based query
expansion, lightweight affect sensitive routing of confused
or frustrated learners, and grounding checks to minimize
unsupported generation were employed. In the event of a
retrieval failure, the system performed a second expanded
search, followed by a fallback message. These mechanisms
support concise, grounded, and pedagogically guided tutoring
behaviour [6], [38].
G. Agentic Quiz Generation Module
The quiz generation module extends the system from con-
versational tutoring to formative assessment. It operates as a
goal directed, multi step workflow for topic selection, support-
ing passage retrieval, item generation, output quality checking,
and grounding feedback [39], [40]. Selection of Topics: The
module includes a pool of quiz topics that can be configured
to align with the 17 textbooks. At quiz initialization, topics are
either selected by the user or randomly sampled, then divided
into multiple choice and short answer fields according to the
desired quiz structure [41]. Retrieval Grounded Generation:
The module follows the hybrid retrieval pipeline outlined in
Section III C to retrieve the top 5 passages for each chosen
topic. These notes are then fed to Qwen3 8B with a structured
prompt that limits question generation to the retrieved material
[42]. For multiple choice questions, the prompt asks the
model to generate a single question, four choices, the correct
answer letter, and a one sentence explanation, in the form
QUESTION, A, B, C, D, ANSWER, and EXPLANA TION.
In short answer questions, the model should generate one
answerable question along with a model response in the form
of a question and answer pair, two to three sentences [41],
[42]. Output Parsing and V alidation: The generated output
is purged of reasoning traces, and then regular expressions
are used to process it. Questions are validated based on three
criteria: all required fields must be present, the question text
must be a minimum length, and the explanation or model
answer must have sufficient detail. Items that do not pass
validation are discarded [43]. Interactive Quiz Delivery: V alid
questions are displayed to the student in random order. For
the multiple choice questions, the student clicks an answer
and receives instant feedback. The grounded explanation is
used to reinforce correct responses, whereas the incorrect
answers display the correct response and explanation. For short
answer items, the model answer is shown to the student after
they respond, and the student is invited to self assess their
performance on a three point scale [39]. Formative Feedback:
A summary report that appears at the end of the quiz session
includes multiple choice accuracy, the average self assessment
for short answer questions, and a list of topics associated with
incorrect answers. This enables the student to identify areas
that require further study [43].

IV. EVALUATION AND RESULTS
A. Evaluation Framework
The suggested system has been tested using a multi di-
mensional assessment framework developed to quantify the
quality of tutoring responses and the pedagogical effectiveness
of the Socratic dialogue. The set of 14 questions was prepared
to address academic content queries across various subjects,
misconception related prompts, emotionally charged student
responses, and one boundary testing question that was not
within the scope of the primary tutoring. Examples were
included: “What is database normalization?” “Why use R
instead of Python?” “R is paid software, right?” “I have no
idea about this topic”, and “What happened during Hurricane
Andrew?” This diversity was meant to provide stress tests
for the system based on realistic student interaction patterns,
to measure several dimensions of tutoring performance, such
as conceptual support, misconception management, affective
responsiveness, and safe boundary behavior [5], [6], [44].
The test was an automated, heuristic scoring, rubric directed
LLM as judge test. The heuristic measures captured relevance,
empathy/instructional tone, conciseness, and context ground-
ing, which are shared dimensions for gauging the quality of
educational dialogue and feedback to the learner [6], [45]. The
evaluation based on the LLM was used to assess the ability
to resist hallucinations, the quality of the Socratic scaffold,
and the use of context, using a pedagogically informed rubric.
This two trial design aligns with existing research suggesting
the use of multi method assessment of educational AI sys-
tems, specifically evaluating response quality and assessing
the greater teaching value of scaffolded dialogue [37], [46].

B. Analysis of Results Matrix
The results Table II indicates that the A TU RAG Socratic
Tutor performs best in relevance, conciseness, grounding, and
hallucination resistance, while empathy and Socratic scaffold-
ing are weaker. Relevance (4.21/5) suggests that, in general,
the system responded to student queries accurately and well
domain matched, particularly for well formulated academic
queries. The best major measure was hallucination resistance
(4.64/5), indicating that the retrieval augmented design effec-
tively limited unsupported or fabricated claims. On the same
note, the scores in the context related category, including
Context Heuristic (4.07/5), Context Use (4.36/5), indicate that
the tutor gave meaningful responses based on the retrieved
information, and Conciseness (4.57/5) demonstrates that the
tutor gave short and student oriented answers [47], [48].
Empathy (3.14/5), in turn, along with Socratic Scaffolding
Quality (3.00/5), was relatively low. Despite the fact that the
system regularly maintained a helpful tone and created follow
up questions, it did not always go deep enough or be adaptive
and pedagogically strategic. Such a trend is consistent with
previous studies of tutoring history, which have demonstrated
that automated systems tend to be effective at reliably deliv-
ering knowledge, albeit with comparable affective sensitivity
and adaptive guidance to human expert tutors [48]–[50]. On
the whole, the heuristic and LLM based aggregate scores were
4.00/5, indicating high overall performance. These findings
imply that the tutor is already satisfactory as an instruction
grounded academic support tool. Still, it should be enhanced
in the future by incorporating more meaningful scaffolding
techniques, misconception related prompts, and more overt,
affect sensitive feedback [50]. In a broader sense, current
research on educational chatbot evaluation also facilitates the
assessment not only of answer quality but also of learner
engagement and the perceived value of instruction [5].

D. Quiz Generation Assessment
The quiz generation agentic module was tested indepen-
dently of the tutoring element. It has been assessed across
various academic subjects using multiple choice and short
answer quizzes. The module was able to extract relevant notes
and formulate legitimate quiz items based on the scholarly
corpus. The practicality of automated quiz building, delivery
of formative assessment, and immediate feedback processes in
learning institutions can be justified by the previous research
on digitally supported and AI enabled assessment [51], [52].
For multiple choice questions, the system generated a question
stem, four viable response choices, a correct response, and
a brief response using retrieved information. The distractors
were intended to reflect probable misconceptions rather than
random options, which is in line with the current assessment
design paradigm [53], [54]. For short answer questions, the
system would generate a question and a brief model answer
based on the supporting notes. Response tracking, immediate
feedback, and a formative summary indicating the aspects
that needed to be reviewed again were also supported by
the interactive quiz workflow, which is consistent with the
literature on digital formative assessment [55], [56]. These
findings suggest that an agentic quiz item can independently
convert the retrieved course content into an applicable for-
mative assessment. In contrast, the tutoring item is largely
retrieval based and Socratic in format but not entirely agentic
[57].
V. CHALLENGES AND LIMITATIONS
A. Socratic Scaffolding Quality
Socratic scaffolding was the least developed dimension of
evaluation, with an average score of 3.00 out of 5. The
system often generated quite superficial follow up questions
rather than more probing inquiries that ask individuals to
compare, reflect, or analyze outcomes. These findings suggest
that, despite the tutor’s ability to guide basic questioning, the
Socratic prompting strategy is not robust across domains and
query types. This finding aligns with earlier studies showing
that maintaining sustained, high quality pedagogical guidance
and deep interaction is difficult for AI tutoring systems [14].
B. Transparency and Source Attribution
Although the system achieved good context grounding per-
formance, learners did not always refer to it in their interac-
tions. Although retrieved passages were typically incorporated
well into answers, the system was not always able to explicitly
provide attribution or the source. The ongoing design dilemma
for retrieval grounded educational systems is the trade off
between conversational fluency and opaque source attribution.
Too little attribution can undermine trust and traceability, while
too much can interfere with the learning process [58].
Moreover, the evaluation was conducted on a limited set
of 14 questions; therefore, the reported scores should be
interpreted as preliminary evidence of system behavior rather
than broad generalizable performance claims. Future work will
expand the evaluation using a larger question set, student-
facing studies, and baseline comparisons.
C. Computing and Data Resource
The system was tested and trained using two Tesla T4 chips
with 16 GB VRAM and 4 bit quantized inference. Although
it enabled cost effective, practical experimentation for deploy-
ment, the configuration limited the size of the model that
could be inferred and the scale of experimentation. Although
more advanced models, stronger rerankers, or pipeline based
tutoring architectures could provide further improvements,
these options were beyond the available hardware budget.
The retriever fine tuning phase also used 929 query passage
pairs, limiting the amount of supervised adaptation in retrieval.
These trade offs align with the existing literature on model
compression and the resource constrained implementation of
a large transformer system [59].

VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper presents an intelligent system based on a chat-
bot, intelligent Socratic tutoring and adaptive quiz generation
system, retrieval augmented with large language models, for
Arkansas Tech University. The system used a hybrid re-
trieval pipeline comprising FAISS, BM25, and cross encoder
reranking of 2,328 chunks from 17 open educational resource
textbooks, a retriever fine tuned on 929 domain specific query
passages, and the Qwen3 8B language model. Assessment of
14 varied items yielded an overall score of 4.00/5, indicating
that the system demonstrated strength in relevance, concise-
ness, contextual grounding, and hallucination resistance. Still,
there are some areas for improvement, such as empathy and
the quality of Socratic scaffolding. Further research will be
conducted in four directions. The first is to enhance adaptive
scaffolding, in which the tutor can respond dynamically by
adjusting guidance based on learners’ progress and recurring
misconceptions, whilst maintaining a fruitful balance between
Socratic prompting and direct support to prevent learner frus-
tration [1], [7]. Second, the contribution of retrieval, reranking,
and prompts, as independent variables, to the overall quality of
tutoring requires controlled ablation studies [2]. Third, control
over adaptive difficulty and alignment to the Taxonomy of
Bloom should be added to the quiz generation module [60],
[61]. Lastly, formal user tests assessing learning outcomes,
cognitive load, and student satisfaction are needed to objec-
tively evaluate the pedagogical efficacy of the system, beyond
heuristic assessment [62].
