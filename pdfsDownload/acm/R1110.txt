Abstract
Reasoning is essential for closed-domain QA systems in which pro-
cedural correctness and policy compliance are critical. While large
language models (LLMs) have shown strong performance on many
reasoning tasks, recent work reveals that their reasoning traces
are often unfaithful—serving more as plausible justifications than
as causally grounded derivations. Efforts to combine LLMs with
symbolic engines (e.g., Prover9, Z3) have improved reliability but
remain limited to static forms of logic, struggling with dynamic,
state-based reasoning such as multi-step progressions and condi-
tional transitions.
In this paper, we propose MCFR (Model Checking for Formal
Reasoning), a neuro-symbolic framework that integrates LLMs with
model checking to support property verification. MCFR translates
natural language into formal specifications and verifies them over
transition models. To support evaluation, we introduce EduMC-
QA, a benchmark dataset grounded in real academic procedures.
Our results show that MCFR improves reasoning faithfulness and

1 Introduction
Question Answering (QA) has emerged as a central task in nat-
ural language understanding (NLU) due to its wide applicability
in domains such as education, healthcare, and human-computer
interaction. A key aspect of QA lies in its inherent requirement
19

for reasoning—drawing logical conclusions from given premises
or background knowledge. While traditional information retrieval-
based QA systems focus primarily on pattern matching or keyword
alignment, modern intelligent QA systems aim to reason about
input questions using deeper semantic understanding. This work is
presented as part of the AIQAM’25 workshop on AI-powered Ques-
tion Answering Systems for Multimedia, which highlights emerging
approaches in intelligent QA across domains and modalities [6].
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have signifi-
cantly enhanced QA performance, particularly in tasks involving
mathematical or symbolic reasoning. Techniques like Chain-of-
Thought (CoT) prompting [3] and Multi-Agent Reasoning [9] simu-
late human-style step-by-step thinking and have achieved impres-
sive results. However, growing evidence indicates that LLMs often
fail at faithful reasoning—i.e., their generated reasoning traces do
not causally influence the final answer [7, 11]. These models tend to
produce post hoc rationalizations rather than genuinely grounded
inferences, exposing a gap between surface-level coherence and
internal logical validity. To close this gap, neuro-symbolic (NeSy)
systems offer a promising hybrid approach by combining the rep-
resentational power of LLMs with the rigor of symbolic solvers. In
this paradigm, LLMs translate NL inputs into formal representa-
tions (e.g., first-order logic), which are then processed by engines
like Z3 or Prover9 [10].
Example 1. Questions on Student Lifecycle.
Context. The academic progression of a student typically
involves multiple stages, such as enrollment, advancement
through academic years (Year 1–4), participation in courses and
social activities, taking temporary leave, and ultimately reaching
either graduation or program withdrawal. Certain actions, such
as course registration, are constrained by institutional rules (e.g.,
prerequisite requirements), while others are subject to timing
and eligibility conditions.
Questions. The following questions illustrate four core verifi-
cation properties that are critical in ensuring the correctness of
such educational processes:
⋄ Q1 (Liveness). Can a student graduate within eight
semesters if all requirements are satisfied on time?
⋄ Q2 (Safety). Is it possible to register for CourseX without
passing its prerequisite CourseY?
⋄ Q3 (Reachability). Can a student advance to Year 3 within
three semesters?
⋄ Q4 (Fairness). Could a student accumulate 100 credits yet
fail to advance to Year 3 owing to missing social activity points?
However, symbolic solvers are typically limited to static logic
tasks and struggle with dynamic, state-dependent reasoning—
common in domains like education, where actions such as course
registration or academic progression depend on temporal con-
straints and evolving eligibility. As illustrated in Example 1, ques-
tions involving safety, liveness, reachability, and fairness require
reasoning over sequential transitions and conditional policies—posing
challenges for symbolic solvers due to limited procedural expres-
siveness, and for LLMs due to a lack of verifiable grounding.
For instance, Q1 (Liveness) asks whether a student can grad-
uate within eight semesters if all requirements are met on time,
requiring that a “good” state is eventually reachable. In contrast,
Q2 (Safety) checks whether the system prevents invalid paths, such
as registering for a course without completing its prerequisite. Q3
(Reachability) concerns whether an accelerated academic path is
viable—for example, if a student can reach Year 3 in just three
semesters by fulfilling requirements early. Q4 (Fairness) explores
whether non-academic factors, like missing social activity points,
could unfairly block academic advancement despite strong aca-
demic performance. Both cases underscore the need for nuanced
temporal and policy-aware reasoning, which remains a challenge
for current methods.
To address these limitations, we introduce MCFR (Model Check-
ing for Formal Reasoning), a hybrid framework that augments QA
systems with verifiable inference capabilities. MCFR employs LLMs
to generate formal representations from NL input, which are then
verified using model checking (MC) over full state-transition sys-
tems. This allows the system to capture procedural behaviors and
validate complex properties such as reachability or fairness in aca-
demic workflows. To facilitate systematic evaluation, we also con-
struct a new dataset grounded in real-world educational processes
that require both symbolic abstraction and temporal reasoning.
Our contributions are as follows.
• We propose MCFR, a formal reasoning framework for QA
that integrates LLM-based semantic translation with model
checking for verifying temporal and procedural properties.
• We implement a QA prototype grounded in real academic
scenarios at Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology (HC-
MUT), demonstrating the feasibility of state-based formal
verification in educational domains.
• We construct a novel QA dataset, EduMC-QA, based on
real institutional processes at HCMUT, and use it to perform
comprehensive evaluations assessing the reasoning capabil-
ity, coverage, and faithfulness of MCFR. We also compare
MCFR’s performance against state-of-the-art LLMs such as
ChatGPT1, Claude2, and DeepSeek3 to contextualize its ef-
fectiveness.
2 Related Works
2.1 Reasoning Patterns of LLMs
LLMs have demonstrated strong performance across various reason-
ing tasks, particularly through prompting-based techniques such
as CoT prompting [3], along with various enhancements that elicit
intermediate reasoning steps and improve answer accuracy [4]. Re-
cent advances also explore multi-agent reasoning setups, in which
one LLM generates the reasoning steps while another acts as a
verifier, identifying errors and prompting the first model to revise
its reasoning accordingly [9].
Despite these improvements, concerns remain about the faithful-
ness of LLM-generated reasoning. Studies have shown that models
often produce coherent reasoning traces that are not causally tied
to their final answers. For example, [11] inserted misleading or
biased cues into reasoning chains and found that LLMs often relied
on these biases rather than engaging in true inference. Similarly,

2.2 Formal Reasoning
Recent work has explored integrating LLMs with symbolic solvers
to achieve more faithful and verifiable reasoning. Logic-LM [10] de-
composes the reasoning process into three phases: problem formu-
lation, symbolic inference, and interpretation—showing improve-
ments over traditional CoT-style prompting. Other approaches such
as SpecGen [5] use mutation-based corrections and verifier feedback
to iteratively refine LLM-generated formal specifications. Logic-
guided theorem proving [10] further scaffolds LLM outputs with
formal logic to reduce semantic errors. In practical applications,
LLMs have also been combined with SMT solvers for task plan-
ning [2], where the solver verifies constraints and offers corrective
feedback during planning failures.
Despite these advances, symbolic solvers like Z3 or Prover9 re-
main inherently static: they require precise and well-formed logical
inputs and struggle with ambiguity, incomplete specifications, or
procedural reasoning involving state changes over time. This rigid-
ity makes them fragile in QA pipelines where LLM outputs may be
noisy or underspecified. Furthermore, such solvers lack mechanisms
to reason over dynamic transitions or evolving states—common
in domains like education—limiting their standalone applicability
without iterative repair or hybrid integration.
These limitations motivate the development of hybrid frame-
works like MCFR, which combine LLM-based translation with state-
based formal verification techniques such as model checking.
3 Background
3.1 Neural Symbolic AI
NeSy systems aim to combine the strengths of neural networks—such
as perception and natural language understanding—with the for-
mal rigor and interpretability of symbolic reasoning. A common
architecture involves using neural models (e.g., LLMs) to translate
natural language inputs into formal representations like first-order
logic, which are then passed to symbolic engines (e.g., theorem
provers or model checkers) for inference. This modular design al-
lows LLMs to handle linguistic ambiguity while relying on symbolic
solvers for precise, verifiable reasoning [7].
Recent surveys, such as [12], have categorized NeSy systems into
various types based on the level of integration between neural and
symbolic components. In many practical QA applications, loosely
coupled hybrids—where LLMs act as semantic translators and sym-
bolic modules verify logical constraints—have shown promising
results.
3.2 Model Checking
Model checking [1] is an automated formal verification technique
used to determine whether a finite-state system model satisfies a
given logical specification. The system is typically modeled as a
transition system 𝑀, and the specification 𝜑 is expressed in Com-
putation Tree Logic (CTL). CTL enables the formal specification
of critical system properties, including safety (the avoidance of
undesirable states), liveness (the guarantee of system progress),
reachability (the ability to attain desired goal states), and fairness
(the assurance that no enabled action is indefinitely postponed).
Given a transition system and a CTL formula, model checking
tools apply algorithms to exhaustively explore the system’s state
space, thereby enabling the formal verification of the specified prop-
erties. This makes model checking a robust and effective method
for reasoning about procedural behaviors across various domains,
including educational systems.

Our proposed framework comprises four main components that
collaboratively enable question answering grounded in formal veri-
fication. The architecture is illustrated in Figure 1.
(1) Model Building Phase. In the first stage, domain experts man-
ually analyze raw data sources such as policy documents, institu-

texts are used to construct a domain-specific QA model that en-
codes the system behavior, logical constraints, and domain rules
required for formal reasoning. This process is performed offline and
serves as the static knowledge base for the downstream inference
pipeline. The detailed implementation of this QA model using a
concrete model checking tool is presented in Section 4.2.
(2) Question Processing and Specification Generation. Given a user-

facts associated with the question context. These inputs are pro-

checking formalisms.
(3) Model Checking Inference. The generated formal specifica-

ing neural and symbolic components in a sequential pipeline. Specif-
ically, Components 2 and 4 leverage the Google Gemini model4 for
natural language processing and high-level conversion tasks. In
contrast, Components 3 and 4 focus on formal verification, where

5 EduMC-QA Dataset
To evaluate the capabilities of QA systems in performing verifiable
reasoning, we construct a novel dataset, EduMC-QA, centered
on procedural and temporal logic within the educational domain.
Rather than focusing on factual recall, the questions in EduMC-QA
are designed to assess whether certain properties hold over dynamic
systems, such as academic workflows. Each question is annotated
with one of four key verification types inspired by formal methods,
which reflect core reasoning challenges commonly addressed in
model checking.
Annotation Guidelines. Each question in EduMC-QA is manually
assigned to one of the four verification categories based on its se-
mantic intent and the property it seeks to verify within the academic
system. The classification is grounded in definitions widely used
in formal verification, with specific identification cues as shown in
Table 2.
Table 2: Verification types with classification criteria
Type Classification Criteria
Safety ⋄ Ensures that nothing bad ever happens.
⋄ Checks whether the system prevents invalid or undesir-
able actions (e.g., bypassing prerequisites).
Liveness ⋄ Verifies that something good will eventually happen.
⋄ Asks whether a desirable outcome can be achieved as-
suming all conditions are met (e.g., graduation).
Reachability ⋄ Asks whether a specific state can be reached from the
current state.
⋄ Considers whether a goal can be achieved under certain
conditions (e.g., fast-tracking to Year 3).
Fairness ⋄ Evaluates whether the system behaves in a just or non-
discriminatory way.
⋄ Checks for unreasonable blocking despite strong com-

To ensure annotation quality and conceptual alignment, each
question was independently categorized by three domain experts
with backgrounds in formal methods and logical reasoning. The
final label was determined by majority vote, and in the rare case
of full disagreement, resolution was achieved through structured
discussion and consensus.
Illustrative Classification Examples. For instance, the question
Q1-“Can a student graduate within eight semesters if all require-
ments are satisfied on time?” is labeled as Liveness because it asks
whether a desirable end state (graduation) will eventually occur
given favorable conditions, emphasizing guaranteed progress rather
than rule enforcement. Conversely, Q2-“Is it possible to register
for CourseX without passing its prerequisite CourseY?” is labeled
as Safety since it seeks to prevent an invalid action (bypassing a
prerequisite), thus ensuring that no undesirable state can occur in
the system.

result. Additionally, two auxiliary fields are included: context,
which contains relevant logical facts extracted from the knowledge

fication and contextual information expressed in UPPAAL syntax.

6.2 Discussion
Among LLMs, ChatGPT-reason performs best overall, particularly
in liveness and reachability questions, suggesting a certain abil-
ity to reason over sequential constraints. DeepSeek-casual also
delivers consistent results, while Claude-Sonnet underperforms
significantly—especially on fairness—highlighting its difficulty with
multi-step, policy-based reasoning. In contrast, MCFR achieves re-
liable reasoning via formal verification. However, it relies on a
manually constructed domain model, an expert-driven and time-
consuming process, which limits scalability. This points to future
potential in semi-automated model construction, where LLMs assist
in abstraction and experts validate critical elements. Another key
challenge lies in translating NL into formal specifications; errors
in this step can lead to incorrect verification outcomes even when
the model is sound. Robust validation mechanisms are therefore
essential to ensure end-to-end QA integrity.
NL-to-CTL Conversion Errors. A major source of error arises in
converting natural language questions into formal UPPAAL queries.
Current LLMs lack knowledge of the UPPAAL syntax, making it
difficult to map NL descriptions into correct CTL formulas without
explicit information about the target model. For accurate NL→CTL

translation, the LLM must be aware of both the UPPAAL model in
use and its precise syntactic rules. Moreover, there are currently
no established metrics for systematically measuring the quality of
NL-to-CTL conversion, which makes it harder to benchmark and
improve this step. We argue that improving the prompt—rather
than extending the underlying logic model—is a more effective and
immediate solution. This is because UPPAAL requires absolute syn-
tactic correctness for verification; while logic model enhancements
may improve runtime efficiency, they do not inherently boost ac-
curacy. In contrast, prompt refinement can guide the LLM toward
producing syntactically valid and semantically aligned queries.
For example, given the NL question: “Can a student graduate
within eight semesters if all requirements are satisfied on time?”,
the correct query is E<> (Student.Graduated && time ≤ 8).
Without prompt tuning, LLM might output E<> (semester <=
8 && AllRequirementsMet && Student.Graduated) , which is
incorrect for two reasons: (1) the model uses the variable time
rather than semester, and (2) AllRequirementsMet does not exist
in the UPPAAL model.
QA Model Scalability and Domain Adaptation. Constructing
and validating the current QA model required approximately 56
person-hours. Reproducing the model for other universities or or-
ganizations would take about 2–8 hours, depending on the extent
of policy differences. The process could follow three main steps: (1)
Modify institutional rules affecting student progression and gradua-
tion (up to 8 hours depending on complexity), (2) Adjust parameter
values such as minimum passing grades, and (3) Conduct thorough
verification testing. Beyond the academic domain, the approach
can be adapted to other fields such as law or healthcare. In such
cases, the time and complexity of adaptation depend heavily on
the number of states, transitions, and governing rules that must be
identified and modeled within each specific domain.
7 Conclusion
We introduced MCFR, a neuro-symbolic QA framework that in-
tegrates LLM-based parsing with model checking for verifiable
reasoning. Evaluated on the EduMC-QA dataset, MCFR outper-
formed state-of-the-art LLMs in tasks requiring logical consistency
and temporal verification. Our results highlight the limitations of
purely neural methods and the value of hybrid approaches. Future
work includes integrating Hoare Logic for local consistency check-
ing, complementing model checking for global verification, and
enabling scalable, semi-automated model development.
