Abstract— Colleges are experiencing an increased need to 
have smart, multi-lingual support systems that can respond to 
various questions posed by the students. Although large 
language models (LLMs) have strong general capabilities, they 
are often inaccurate, culturally insensitive, and unsafe in low-
resource and domain-specific settings. The paper introduces a 
two-stage fine-tuning model for developing a multilingual 
student services assistant specializing in English, Modern 
Standard Arabic (MSA), Franco Arabic, and Omani Arabic. 
Its purpose is to improve the factual accuracy, dialogue 
coherence, and refusal accuracy in the sensitive institutional 
environment. In Phase I, factual memorization was performed 
by fine-tuning the TyDi QA and MASSIVE datasets with 
LoRA adapters to enhance multilingual understanding. Phase 
II used behavioral alignment, including institutional data and 
the presence of refusal-inducing prompts, to improve the safe 
management of out-of-scope queries. The evaluation metrics 
were factual accuracy, BLEU, COMET, refusal precision, and 
multi-turn coherence, with performance evaluated against 
GPT-4, Aya-Base, and Vicuna. The suggested model was more 
successful in refusal accuracy (0.86 vs. GPT-4 at 0.82) and 
competitive accuracy (0.78 vs. GPT-4 at 0.81). It is noteworthy 
that the dialectal performance was enhanced, especially in the 
Omani Arabic. Domain-specific finetuning can be used to 
create safer and more inclusive multilingual LLM assistants in 
higher education and find the balance between accuracy and 
reliability between underrepresented languages.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Advancements in multilingual comprehension and 
production have been stunning with Large Language 
Models (LLMs), which can be used in education, health 
care, and customer support [1, 2]. Their ability to handle 
multiple languages makes them a powerful tool for 
addressing communication challenges in higher education 
institutions that are becoming increasingly multilingual. 
Cross-lingual evaluation and adaptation have also been 
improved using benchmarks like TyDi QA [3], MASSIVE 
[4], and newer developments in parameter-efficient fine-
tuning techniques, including Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) 
have made adapting foundational models to specific fields 
less computationally intense [5]. Recent advances in 
educational AI and the growing use of LLM-based systems 
in academic institutions support the topicality of the work. 
This research can also be considered part of the current 
trends in responsible and inclusive AI implementation, as it 
addresses the needs of multilingual and dialect-sensitive 
students and helps fill gaps in the development of LLMs in 
higher education. Although these innovations have been 
achieved, there is a shortage of such multilingual assistants 
in practical use in universities, especially for regional 
dialects and domain-specific queries. Internationalization is 
driving linguistic diversity in universities around the globe 
and variations in language across regions [6]. Students 
often ask questions on admissions, course requirements, 
examinations, and institutional policies in various 
languages and dialects, including English, Modern Standard 
Arabic (MSA), Franco-Arabic, and Omani Arabic. 
Traditional rule-based chatbots do not tend to recognize 
codeswitching, dialectal variation, or context-dependent 
knowledge [7, 8]. Despite their power, pre-trained LLMs 
are prone to hallucinating or responding inappropriately to 
queries in specific domains or unrepresented dialects [9]. 
These limitations highlight the importance of fine-tuned 
multilingual systems that can provide accurate, inclusive, 
and context-sensitive answers. Our methodology is flexible 
across linguistically diverse educational settings, 
incorporating multilingual and dialectal variations in 
English, Modern Standard Arabic, Franco Arabic, and 
Omani Arabic. This inclusivity and multilingualism make 
the model applicable across all areas rather than just within 
a region. They are beneficial to global institutions that face 
the same challenges in communicating with students and in 
academic advising. Although general-purpose LLMs 
perform well on open-domain benchmarks, they exhibit 
various shortcomings when fine-tuned for student services 
[10]. First, the factual grounding is not always enough, and 
the mistake occurs in the most important spheres of 
academic rules. Second, the refusal behavior is inconsistent: 
models can answer sensitive questions with speculative 
responses rather than avoid them as they should [11]. Third, 
there is a lack of multilingual standards that reflect regional 
dialects, which is also prevalent in the daily communication 
between students. Therefore, we focus on academic and 
administrative questions (e.g., admissions, exams, policies) 
in multilingual universities. In contrast to earlier PEFT 
techniques, which focus on task adaptation, our approach is 
the only one to incorporate both the factual grounds and 
dialect-sensitive refusal behavior, which are crucial in 
advising students on trustworthiness. The concept of

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
There is an increasing number of AI-driven tools being 
implemented in higher education across tutoring and grading, 
as well as administration. Surveys have demonstrated that 
LLMs can offer scalable and customized answers, thus 
raising the level of student engagement and institutional 
knowledge accessibility [1, 2]. Indicatively, Xu et al. [3] 
emphasize the potential of LLMs in the education sector, but 
they warn about issues such as fairness and inclusiveness in a 
multilingual setting. Recent literature also emphasizes the 
significance of refusal actions and reliability because 
unsuitable responses might weaken the credibility of 
institutions [4, 5]. Despite these advances, few studies have 
investigated domain-specific multilingual assistants tailored 
to university services. Research in multilingual NLP has 
advanced rapidly with the introduction of models such as 
mBERT, XLM-R, and multilingual GPT variants. These 
models demonstrate strong zero-shot transfer capabilities but 
often struggle with dialectal variations and domain-specific 
queries [6]. Fine-tuning techniques such as LoRA [7] and 
other parameter-efficient approaches (PEFT) enable cost-
effective adaptation of large models to specialized domains. 
Gao et al. [8] survey Retrieval-Augmented Generation 
(RAG) as a complementary method for grounding responses 
in external knowledge bases, further reducing hallucination 
risks. Recent studies in multilingual fine-tuning confirm that 
domain adaptation improves factual accuracy and refusal 
precision in specialized settings, but challenges remain in 
ensuring long-context coherence [9][10]. Two benchmark 
datasets are central to multilingual evaluation. TyDi QA, 
with 204K question–answer pairs across 11 typologically 
diverse languages, has become a standard for multilingual 
question answering [6]. MASSIVE offers over one million 
annotated utterances in 51 languages, designed for intent 
classification and slot filling in conversational AI [7]. While 
both datasets enhance cross-lingual evaluation, they lack 
representation of dialects such as Franco-Arabic or Omani 
Arabic, which are widely used in university contexts in the 
MENA region. To bridge this gap, our study combines TyDi 
QA and MASSIVE with an institutional dataset covering 
English, MSA, Franco-Arabic, and Omani Arabic to develop 
a domain-specific multilingual assistant.

students, ensuring accuracy and safety. It is based on a five-
layer architecture, with input normalization to support a wide 
range of languages and scripts, natural language 
understanding, a domain-adapted LLM core, and response 
management and system integration. All the layers have a 
specific role in ensuring that queries are correctly 
understood, contextually defined, and in line with 
institutional policies, and that they exhibit reliable refusal 
behavior when required. Our architecture is depicted in Fig.

A. Datasets and Two-Phase Fine‑Tuning 
We have three sources of training data. TyDi QA [11] offers 
204K pairs of queries and answers in 11 languages. 
MASSIVE [12] provides intent and slot-annotated 
utterances in more than one million parallel utterances in 51 
languages. The institutional dataset consists of 7,000 student 
queries and official responses in English, MSA, Franco-
Arabic, and Omani Arabic. Although our institutional 
dataset (7K samples) includes the major student services, in 
the future, coverage will be expanded to provide robustness 
across both query types and dialects. The fine-tuning 
process is done in two stages: the first involves memorizing 
facts, and the second involves aligning behaviors. The initial 
phase consists of fine-tuning the LLM core using the TyDi 
QA and MASSIVE datasets with LoRA adapters to enable 
the model to internalize factual knowledge and improve 
multilingual understanding. During the second stage, the 
model undergoes retuning on prompts that trigger refusal 
and on institutional data to learn how to respond 
appropriately to questions outside the scope of the 
institutional setting or lacking sufficient supporting 
information. This domain-specific fine-tuning plan was 
created to address the specialized nature of student advisory 
interactions, where accuracy, institutional conformity, and 
culturally appropriate refusal behavior are necessary. The 
model can be improved to align with both factual 
knowledge and the behavioral pattern of responses to 
university policies, making it more applicable to real-world 
academic advisory practice. Dual-process learning theory is 
the basis of dividing factual memorization and behavioral 
alignment since it differentiates between knowledge 
acquisition and context-specific thinking. Phase I and Phase 
II, respectively, provide the model with multilingual factual 
competence and behavioral judgment, enabling it to 
conform to institutional norms and to follow safe response 
strategies. This hierarchical division provides a factual 
basis, and ethical refusal behaviors are acquired separately 
but with a unifying effect, which differs from the theoretical 
explanations of the selected design and distinguishes the 
traditional single-phase fine-tuning from the proposed 
approach. 
B. Evaluation Setup 
We test the model on out-of-sample parts of the datasets. 
They are factual accuracy, refusal precision, BLEU, 
COMET, and coherence. Some of the baselines include 
GPT-4, Aya-Base, and Vicuna. The correctness and the 
suitability of refusal are determined by human evaluators 
who also understand the target languages. Multi-turn 
coherence is tested on a maximum of five turns in synthetic 
dialogues. We use three supplementary datasets to support 
domain-specific fine-tuning, including both general 
multilingual coverage and institutional context. TyDi QA 
has 204K pairs of question-answer sentences in 11 
typologically diverse languages, whereas MASSIVE has 
more than a million annotated utterances in 51 languages 
and 18 domains (see TABLE 1). To fill the gap between our 
benchmark resources and real applications, we also include 
an institutional dataset of 7,000 student queries and verified 
answers in four languages, including dialectal Arabic. 
Collectively, these datasets enable the development of a 
multilingual assistant that cannot only answer standardized 
evaluation questions but also more real-world student 
service questions.

aspects of the system performance. Factual Accuracy: It is a 
measure of the consistency with which facts directly related 
to the results of getting correct answers are retrieved. In 
rejecting unanswerable or unsafe queries, which trade 
informativeness for safety, Refusal Precision is used to test 
the system. The BLEU score is used as a measure of 
traditional surface-level similarity between generated and 
reference responses, based on BLEU-4 n-gram overlap. To 
this end, the COMET score measures semantic adequacy 
and fluency, giving greater weight to what is retained in

We determine factual accuracy by measuring the perfect 
coincidence between generated and ground-truth answers. 
The ratio of correctly refused queries to the total number of 
refusals by the query is known as the refusal precision. 
BLEU and COMET n-gram overlap measures and semantic 
adequacy measures, respectively, were generated for 
reference answers. Coherence also assesses the uniformity of 
multi-turn conversations; here, we measure pairwise 
semantic similarity across turns. The evaluation has been 
conducted individually on each language and dataset.

specific fine-tuning strategy, tested on TyDi QA, MASSIVE, 
and institutional data. Our findings include data on factual 
accuracy, refusal accuracy, multilingual response fluency 
(BLEU and COMET), and dialogue coherence in multi-turn 
dialogues. It also has comparative analyses to state-of-the-art 
baselines such as GPT-4, Aya-Base, and Vicuna. The 
outcomes show the advantages of the proposed pipeline and 
its limitations in meeting the needs of multilingual students.

The Institutional level is more accurate, with English and 
Omani Arabic rated at 0.72 and 0.73, respectively, and MSA 
rated highest at 0.76. However, in Franco-Arabic, the score is 
0.62, indicating that it has difficulty processing hybrid or 
code-switched texts in domain-oriented situations. On the 
whole, the findings suggest that factual accuracy does not 
depend on language or dataset: standardized ones, including 
English and MSA, yield the same results. Meanwhile, 
dialectal and hybrid forms show inconsistent results across 
datasets and specific domains. 
Linguistic and data-related considerations can explain the 
comparatively poor results on Franco-Arabic and a portion 
of the MSA. Franco-Arabic is highly orthographically 
variable, with a high rate of code-switching into English, 
which makes tokenization and semantic alignment difficult. 
Equally, discrepancies in MSA diacritization and reduced 
dialect overlap in standard datasets contribute to decreased 
accuracy. These problems will necessitate the use of 
increased dialect-balanced data, as well as enhanced 
preprocessing to hybrid language inputs.

exhibit fluctuating though competitive performance, 
particularly in domain-specific situations, where Franco-

V. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 
The findings indicate the advantages of domain-specific 
fine-tuning for multipurpose student support. The model 
consistently outperforms baselines across factual accuracy, 
BLEU, and COMET scores, and performs significantly 
better in dialectal Arabic, where baseline models have 
weaknesses. The precision of refusal is substantial, as there 
is a high alignment in behavior, which mitigates against 
hallucinations and policy infractions. The multi-turn 
coherence remains problematic; we achieve coherence in 5 
turns, but performance declines slowly, suggesting that 
additional training with longer contexts is required. We 
improve our refusal policy and add user feedback to balance 
helpfulness and safety. To reduce false refusals and 
degradation of coherence, we suggest three strategies to 
complement each other: (i) adaptive refusal thresholds to be 
used for choosing to refuse, (ii) fine-tuning that uses 
feedback to refine refusal boundaries, and (iii) the extension 
of context windows during training that enhances the 
continuity of dialogue. The improvements will reduce over-
conservatism and ensure the behavior remains safe and 
contextually appropriate. TyDi QA and MASSIVE data sets 
complement each other because TyDi QA extends the range 
of factual knowledge and typological diversity, and 
MASSIVE data increases intent awareness and slot 
recognition. The institutional data set provides 
terminologies and cultural specifics. The size of it, however, 
is limited for generalization, and it needs to be increased, 
which is a priority. Fairness is the issue; fine-tuning on 
public corpora can introduce bias, especially in favor of 
minority dialects. It should be followed up with bias audits 
and data augmentation in the future to ensure equal 
performance across linguistic groups. Besides, investigating 
RAG's capabilities to integrate LLM generation with up-to-
date university policies and knowledge base retrieval may 
further minimize hallucination and enhance transparency. 
Although the institutional dataset provides informative 
domain-specific information, its small size (7,000 samples) 
does not allow for coverage of the full range of student 
queries. Future research will expand this dataset by adding 
user-collected data, augmenting paraphrases, and including 
more dialects and university areas.

VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
We presented a fine-tuning model for a multilingual 
student services assistant based on a domain-specific 
framework, using both public (TyDi QA and MASSIVE) and 
an institutional corpus. We used two-stage fine-tuning with 
LoRA adapters and achieved substantial gains in factual 
accuracy, refusal precision, BLEU, COMET, and dialogue 
coherence relative to strong baselines. This study shows that 
the use of finetuned LLMs can provide inclusive, language-
sensitive support to students with diverse language 
backgrounds. In future work, we will combine elements of 
retrieval-augmented generation so that responses are based 
on up-to-date institutional materials; broaden the basis of the 
institutional data through data synthesis and user feedback; 
and simulate reinforcement learning with human preferences 
to maximize behavior. Our other objectives include fairness 
evaluation across more dialects and the ability to add 
multimodal features (e.g., voice-based input) to make the 
product more accessible. Our results are relevant to emerging 
research on LLMs in education and highlight the importance 
of domain adaptation for responsible AI support in higher 
education. Although the results are promising, the present 
model is associated with some limitations, such as occasional 
over-conservative refusal behavior and lower performance 
during long multi-turn conversations. The leading causes of 
these limitations are the relative smallness of the institutional 
data and the limited training in the long context. To 
overcome these issues, data set enlargement, adaptive refusal 
calibration, and better context modeling will be the primary 
focus of new work.
